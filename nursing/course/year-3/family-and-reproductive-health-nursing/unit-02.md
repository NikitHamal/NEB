## 2.1 Principles of family planning and the Nepal basket

**Family planning (FP)** lets couples decide the **number and spacing** of their children by using contraception and by treating infertility. It saves lives: a gap of **at least 24 months** between a birth and the next pregnancy (WHO recommends 24 months, which means about 33 months from birth to next birth) lowers neonatal, infant and maternal deaths, and delaying the first pregnancy until **after 20 years** protects girls. Nepal's FP programme is run by the **Family Welfare Division** and gives most methods **free** at health posts, PHCCs and hospitals; **FCHVs** give pills and condoms; **NGOs** such as Marie Stopes Nepal and FPAN also serve. NDHS 2022 shows **modern method use at 43%** (female sterilisation 13%, injectables 9%, implants 6%, pills, condoms and IUCD each a few per cent) and an **unmet need of 21%**.

**The Nepal basket** (the choices a nurse must be able to offer):

| Method | How it works | Dose, schedule and duration | Typical-use failure in year 1 (per 100 women) |
|---|---|---|---|

| **Progestin-only pill (POP)** | Thickens mucus, may stop ovulation | 1 tablet daily without a break, same time | 7 |
| **DMPA injectable (Depo)** | Stops ovulation | 150 mg IM every 3 months (13 weeks) | 4 |
| **Implants** (Jadelle 2 rods; Implanon or Nexplanon 1 rod) | Stops ovulation, thick mucus | Jadelle 5 years; single rod 3 years | 0.1 |
| **Copper IUCD (Cu T 380A)** | Copper kills sperm, blocks implantation | Insert once; works 10 years | 0.8 |
| **LNG-IUS** | Thin endometrium, thick mucus | 5 years | 0.2 |
| **Male condom / female condom** | Blocks sperm; protects against STI | New one for every act | 13 / 21 |
| **Emergency contraception (ECP)** | Delays ovulation | Levonorgestrel 1.5 mg once within 72 hours (up to 120 hours) | Prevents about half to nine-tenths of expected pregnancies |
| **Lactational amenorrhoea method (LAM)** | Breastfeeding suppresses ovulation | Under 6 months, no periods, full breastfeeding | 2 |

Failure figures follow the WHO and Johns Hopkins handbook (*Family Planning: A Global Handbook for Providers*); the Nepal programme also lists withdrawal (20 per 100), which is the commonest traditional method (13%).

## 2.2 Counselling and medical eligibility

Good counselling is the **heart of the FP service**. The **balanced counselling strategy** and the **GATHER** steps guide the nurse: **G**reet and give privacy; **A**sk about the woman's plans, health, partner and past methods; **T**ell about the methods that suit her; **H**elp her to choose, using her own words; **E**xplain how to use the method, side effects and warning signs; **R**eturn visit and follow-up. The nurse offers **all** methods, not only the nurse's favourite, and treats the **woman's choice as final**. **No husband permission** is needed; the nurse may invite the partner if the woman wishes.

**Medical eligibility** follows the **WHO Medical Eligibility Criteria (MEC)** with four categories: **1** no restriction, **2** advantages outweigh risks, **3** risks usually outweigh advantages, **4** unacceptable health risk. Examples every nurse must know: **COC is category 4** for breastfeeding under 6 weeks, **blood pressure 160/100 mmHg or more**, **migraine with aura**, **smoker aged 35 or over (15 or more cigarettes a day)**, **past deep vein thrombosis** and **current breast cancer**; an **IUCD is category 4 in pregnancy and current pelvic infection**. Progestin-only methods and IUCDs are safe in **breastfeeding women after 6 weeks** (category 1 or 2). Always **rule out pregnancy** before starting: no period in the last 4 weeks is not enough on its own; use the **pregnancy checklist** (no sex since the last period, baby under 6 months with LAM, within 7 days of a period, within 4 weeks postpartum, a reliable method used correctly, or a negative test).

### Eight steps of family planning counselling

1. **Welcome the woman and give privacy.** Close the door, sit at her level, and say your name. Many women are shy to speak about sex. A respectful start builds trust.
2. **Ask about her needs and plans.** Find out how many children she has, when she wants the next one, her health, her partner's travel and what she used before. The answers decide which methods fit. Do not assume.
3. **Check the medical eligibility.** Use the MEC wheel or checklist to rule out pregnancy, high blood pressure, clots, migraine, liver disease, cancer and breastfeeding stage. This keeps the woman safe from serious harm.
4. **Explain the methods that suit her.** Show samples and say how long each lasts, how effective it is, and what side effects to expect. Use simple Nepali and avoid long lists. A woman cannot choose what she does not understand.
5. **Help her make the choice.** Ask which she prefers and what worries her; correct myths such as "pills cause cancer" or "an IUCD travels to the heart". Respect her final decision. A freely chosen method is used longer.
6. **Give the method and teach its use.** Give the first pack, injection, implant or IUCD only with her informed consent, and teach exactly how to use it. Use teach-back. Correct use is the difference between 0.1 and 7 failures per 100.
7. **Explain side effects and warning signs.** Tell her about bleeding changes, headache, weight and what needs urgent return (for example the **PAINS** signs of IUCD: Period late, Abdominal pain, Infection or discharge, Not feeling well with fever, String missing). Women who are warned seldom stop the method.
8. **Arrange follow-up and record.** Give the return date, write it on the card, and enter the data in the FP register and HMIS. Remind her that she may change the method at any time without cost. Follow-up detects problems early.

## 2.3 Hormonal methods: pills, injectable, implants and emergency contraception

**Combined oral pills (COC).** Each pack has 21 active tablets (or 21 plus 7 iron or placebo). The first pill is taken **on days 1-5 of the period** (no back-up needed if started in the first 5 days) and then **one tablet every day at about the same time**, starting the next pack the day after the last. **Missed pills:** if **one pill** is missed or late by under 48 hours, take it as soon as remembered and continue; if **two or more** are missed, take the latest missed pill at once, continue the pack, and use **condoms or abstain for 7 days**. If pills are missed in the **last 7 active tablets**, skip the hormone-free tablets and begin the next pack at once. If she missed pills in the first week and had unprotected sex, offer **emergency contraception**. Vomiting within 2 hours or severe diarrhoea counts as a missed pill. Benefits: regular light periods, less anaemia, less dysmenorrhoea, lower risk of ovarian and endometrial cancer. Side effects: nausea, breast tenderness, spotting in the first 3 months, headache. Danger signs (the **ACHES** list): **A**bdominal pain, **C**hest pain or breathlessness, **H**eadache severe, **E**ye problems, **S**evere leg pain.

**Progestin-only pills (POP).** One tablet **every day without a break**, within the same 3 hours for levonorgestrel types. Suitable for **breastfeeding women after 6 weeks** and for women who cannot take oestrogen. Bleeding may become irregular.

**DMPA (Depo-Provera).** **150 mg IM** (deep into the gluteus or deltoid, not rubbed) **every 3 months**; start in the **first 7 days of the period** with no back-up, or at any other time if pregnancy is excluded with 7 days of condoms. The next injection is **due at 13 weeks**, and one up to **4 weeks late** may be given without testing in the WHO guidance. **Side effects:** irregular bleeding, then **amenorrhoea** in many users (not harmful), weight gain, and **delayed return of fertility by about 4 months** after stopping. Bone density falls a little but recovers.

**Implants.** Jadelle (2 rods) lasts **5 years**; Implanon or Nexplanon (1 rod) **3 years** (evidence supports longer use, follow the MoHP label). Inserted **within 5 days of the period** with local anaesthesia on the inside of the non-dominant upper arm, and removed at any time on request by a trained provider. The commonest side effect is **bleeding change** (spotting, long bleeding or none); it is not harmful and is managed by counselling or a short course of ibuprofen. Fertility returns at once after removal.

**Emergency contraception (ECP).** **Levonorgestrel 1.5 mg once** as soon as possible after unprotected sex, **best within 72 hours and still useful up to 120 hours**; it works by delaying ovulation, and **does not end an existing pregnancy**. A **copper IUCD within 5 days** is the most effective emergency method. The next period may be early or late; if it is more than a week late, a pregnancy test is done. Start a regular method afterwards with 7 days of condoms.

## 2.4 The IUCD

The **copper IUCD (Cu T 380A)** is a small T-shaped frame with **copper wire and sleeves** that is placed in the uterine cavity. It works mainly by **killing sperm and blocking fertilisation**; it is effective **for 10 years**, can be removed at any time, and fertility returns at once. It is **very suitable for Kamala** after 6 weeks (and for women who want a hormone-free long-acting method). **Timing of insertion:** any time **within 12 days of the start of a period**, or at any time when pregnancy is reasonably excluded; **post-placental within 10 minutes to 48 hours of delivery (PPIUCD)**, **after 4 weeks postpartum**, and **immediately after a first-trimester abortion** if there is no infection.

**Indications:** a woman who wants long-term spacing, who is breastfeeding, who cannot take hormones, or who needs emergency contraception. **Contraindications (category 4):** pregnancy, current **pelvic inflammatory disease or purulent cervicitis**, puerperal or post-abortion sepsis, **unexplained vaginal bleeding**, **cervical or endometrial cancer**, a distorted uterine cavity, and pelvic tuberculosis. **Complications:** cramps and heavier periods in the first 3 months, **expulsion** (highest postpartum), **perforation** (about 1 in 1,000), **PID** in the first 20 days (screen first), and **pregnancy with the device in place** (remove if the thread can be seen, to avoid miscarriage and infection).

### Eight points of nursing responsibility in IUCD insertion and follow-up

1. **Counsel and take consent.** Explain how it works, the 10-year duration, the common effects, and that removal is easy. Get her free and informed consent. A woman who knows what to expect is less likely to ask for early removal.
2. **Screen for eligibility.** Take the history of the last period, pregnancy, pelvic infection, STI risk, bleeding and cancer signs; exclude pregnancy by checklist. This prevents inserting an IUCD in a woman for whom it is category 4.
3. **Prepare the woman and the instruments.** Ask her to empty the bladder, explain each step, position her in lithotomy and use a sterile set (speculum, tenaculum, sound, IUCD in its sterile pack). Use the **no-touch technique** and wash hands. Sterile technique avoids PID.
4. **Assist the provider and reduce pain.** Talk to her, advise slow deep breathing and give ibuprofen 400 mg before the procedure if allowed. The provider performs a bimanual exam, sounds the uterus and inserts the device. Calm support lowers pain and fainting.
5. **Monitor after insertion.** Keep her lying for 15-30 minutes and check pulse, blood pressure and pain, because vasovagal fainting can occur. Look for heavy bleeding. Early signs of perforation or shock must be reported at once.
6. **Teach self-care and the thread check.** Tell her to avoid sex for 24 hours or use condoms if she has an STI risk, to expect cramps and spotting, and to feel for the thread after each period. Check helps notice expulsion early.
7. **Teach the warning signs (PAINS).** Return at once for Period late, Abdominal pain, Infection or foul discharge, Not feeling well with fever and String missing or longer. Quick return prevents ectopic pregnancy, sepsis and perforation going unnoticed.
8. **Arrange follow-up and record.** Visit at 3-6 weeks and then yearly or when worried; write the insertion date, device and removal date (10 years later) on the card and in the register and HMIS. Good records prevent a forgotten device.

## 2.5 Condoms, natural methods and sterilisation

**Condoms** are the only method that also **prevents HIV and STIs**. Use a **new condom every time**, put it on the erect penis before any genital contact, pinch the tip, unroll fully, hold the rim when withdrawing, tie it and bin it; use water-based lubricant only (oil weakens latex). Condoms are free at health posts and FCHVs. **Natural methods** need motivation: **LAM** is 98-99% effective in the first 6 months **only if all three** are present (**baby under 6 months, no menstrual bleeding since 56 days postpartum, full or nearly full breastfeeding day and night**); the **Standard Days Method** needs a 26-32 day cycle (Figure 2.2); **withdrawal** is unreliable (20 per 100).

**Female sterilisation (minilap)** is done by a small suprapubic incision, **within 7 days postpartum or in the interval**, under local anaesthesia; **male NSV** is done through one puncture and needs **3 months or 20 ejaculations** before semen is safe, so condoms are used until a **semen test** shows clearance. Both are **permanent**, **voluntary** and need **written informed consent** after counselling that the decision is final; the Nepal programme holds regular **sterilisation camps** in districts and the service is free. **Contraindications** are only temporary (infection, bleeding, anaemia, pregnancy, uncontrolled illness); **complications** are wound infection, haematoma and failure.

**Postpartum and post-abortion FP.** The nurse should counsel every woman during **ANC** and **before discharge**. After birth: **LAM** from day 1; **POP, implants, DMPA** safe from **6 weeks** (and earlier by MEC 2 if no other option); **PPIUCD** within 48 hours; **minilap** within 7 days; **condoms** any time; avoid **COC before 6 weeks** (category 4) and before 6 months (category 3) in a breastfeeding mother. **Post-abortion:** any method the same day.

### Seven points on managing side effects of hormonal methods

1. **Counsel before the first dose.** Tell every woman the common changes (spotting, nausea, headache, amenorrhoea) before she starts, and say they are usually not dangerous. A woman who is warned will not stop in fear. Counselling is the best treatment for side effects.
2. **Spotting and irregular bleeding.** Reassure her, check for pregnancy or infection if bleeding is heavy or new, and offer ibuprofen 400 mg three times a day for 5 days with food. Bleeding often settles in 3 months. Do not remove an implant for spotting alone.
3. **Amenorrhoea with DMPA or implants.** Explain that the lining is thin, blood is not "stored", and it does not harm fertility. Give a pregnancy test only if she has symptoms. Many women like having no periods.
4. **Nausea and breast tenderness (pills).** Take the pill with food or at night, and expect improvement in 2-3 months. Check that she is not pregnant. Simple changes stop most complaints.
5. **Headache.** Mild headache is treated with paracetamol; a new severe headache or migraine with aura means the **pill is stopped** at once and the woman is referred, because of stroke risk. Take the blood pressure every visit.
6. **Weight change and mood.** Check weight and diet at each visit, give food advice and exercise, and ask about low mood. Most weight changes are small. Offer a change of method if distress is real.
7. **Danger signs.** The **ACHES** (pills) and the signs of infection, abscess at an implant site or heavy bleeding need urgent referral. Explain them to the woman and her family. Early return prevents serious harm.

## 2.6 Calculations

The **Pearl index = pregnancies × 1,200 ÷ total woman-months of exposure** (pregnancies per 100 woman-years). **Couple-years of protection (CYP)** converts all methods issued in a year into years of protection: **condoms ÷ 120, pill cycles ÷ 15, DMPA doses ÷ 4, copper IUCD × 4.6, implant × 2.5** (teaching factors from USAID; check the current HMIS factor). The **Ogino-Knaus rule** gives the first fertile day = **shortest cycle - 18** and the last = **longest cycle - 11**.

## Formula and fact sheet

- **Pearl index** = pregnancies × 1,200 ÷ woman-months. **CYP**: condoms ÷ 120, pill cycles ÷ 15, DMPA doses ÷ 4, copper IUCD × 4.6, implant × 2.5.
- **Typical-use failure (per 100, year 1):** implant 0.1, vasectomy 0.15, LNG-IUS 0.2, sterilisation 0.5, copper IUCD 0.8, LAM 2, DMPA 4, pill 7, SDM 12, male condom 13, female condom 21, withdrawal 20.
- **DMPA** 150 mg IM every 13 weeks; **Jadelle** 5 years; single rod 3 years; **Cu T 380A** 10 years; **ECP** levonorgestrel 1.5 mg within 72 hours (up to 120 hours); **COC** missed 2 or more: condoms for 7 days.
- **MEC 4 for COC:** breastfeeding under 6 weeks, BP 160/100 or more, migraine with aura, smoker 35 or over with 15 or more a day, past DVT, current breast cancer.
- **LAM:** baby under 6 months, amenorrhoea, full breastfeeding. **SDM:** cycle 26-32 days, avoid days 8-19. **NSV:** 3 months or 20 ejaculations. **NDHS 2022:** modern 43%, unmet need 21%.

## Chapter summary

- Family planning spaces births by at least 24 months and delays first pregnancy beyond 20 years; Nepal's basket has pills, DMPA, implants, IUCD, condoms, ECP, natural methods and permanent methods, all free in the public sector.
- Counselling uses GATHER, offers all methods, respects the woman's final choice and rules out pregnancy with a checklist; the MEC categories 1-4 decide eligibility.
- COC missed-pill rules, DMPA timing, implant duration, IUCD timing and the PAINS warning signs are the high-yield facts.
- Side effects are managed first by counselling; danger signs such as ACHES and PAINS need urgent referral.
- Sterilisation is permanent and voluntary with written consent; NSV needs a clearance test at 3 months or 20 ejaculations.
- The Pearl index measures failure and CYP measures programme output.

**Short answer (3 marks): State the criteria of the lactational amenorrhoea method (LAM).** LAM is a temporary method, 98-99% effective for 6 months, when all three conditions are present: the baby is under 6 months, the mother has had no menstrual bleeding after 56 days postpartum, and she is fully or nearly fully breastfeeding day and night (including at night, without long gaps). If any one fails, she must start another method. Pills with oestrogen are avoided until 6 months.

**Short answer (3 marks): Write the rules for missed combined pills.** If one pill is missed or late by under 48 hours, take it at once and continue; no extra protection is needed. If two or more pills are missed, take the latest missed pill immediately, continue the rest of the pack, and use condoms or abstain for 7 days. If the missed pills are in the last 7 active tablets, finish them and start the next pack without the hormone-free break. If she had unprotected sex after missed pills in week 1, give emergency contraception.

**Medium answer (5 marks): Describe the nursing responsibilities when giving DMPA (Section 2.3).** DMPA is 150 mg given by deep intramuscular injection every 13 weeks. The nurse counsels about how it works, the common effects (irregular bleeding then amenorrhoea, weight gain, delayed return of fertility of about 4 months) and obtains consent. She excludes pregnancy by the checklist, takes blood pressure and checks that there is no unexplained vaginal bleeding or breast cancer. She shakes the vial, uses a new auto-disable syringe, gives the dose in the gluteus or deltoid without rubbing, and records the date and the next due date on the card. A woman starting in the first 7 days of her period needs no back-up; otherwise 7 days of condoms are advised. She gives a return date at 13 weeks, with a grace of up to 4 weeks, and teaches danger signs: heavy bleeding, severe headache, abscess at the site. She disposes of sharps safely and records in the register and HMIS.

**Long answer (10 marks): Kamala Rai, 28, has two children; her baby is 4 months old and she is fully breastfeeding with no periods. Her husband is abroad. She wants to avoid pregnancy for 2-3 years. Counsel her, state her LAM status and eligibility, and write the nursing care plan.**

*Assessment and eligibility.* The baby was born on 10 November 2024, so Kamala meets all **three LAM criteria** (under 6 months, no periods, full breastfeeding); LAM stops protecting her on **10 May 2025** or earlier if any criterion fails. Her health is normal (BP 110/70 mmHg), so no condition gives MEC category 4. Pregnancy is excluded by the checklist (baby under 6 months with LAM). **COC is not suitable** (category 3 before 6 months of breastfeeding). The progestin-only methods, implants, DMPA and the copper IUCD are all suitable (category 1-2).

*Counselling (GATHER).* The nurse greets her privately, asks about her plan and husband's travel, tells her the options (implant, copper IUCD, DMPA, POP, condoms), and explains that a **24-month gap** means conception no earlier than **10 November 2026**. Using the effectiveness tiers (Figure 2.1), the nurse suggests the top tier: a **Jadelle implant (5 years, failure 0.1 per 100)** or a **Cu T IUCD (10 years, 0.8 per 100)**. Kamala chooses **Jadelle**, because she wants no daily task and no thread to check. She is told that bleeding may be irregular, that it is not harmful, that fertility returns right after removal and that the implant does not protect against STIs, so condoms are advised if her husband has other partners.

The nurse also reminds Kamala about exclusive breastfeeding until 6 months, complementary food after that, and the baby's vaccines. Data are entered in the FP register and HMIS.
