## 3.1 Acute malnutrition in children

**Malnutrition** causes about half of all child deaths directly or indirectly. NDHS 2022 found **stunting 25%, wasting 8% and underweight 19%** in Nepali children under 5. **Acute malnutrition** is graded by the **MUAC** (6-59 months), **weight-for-height z-score (WHZ)** and **bilateral pitting oedema**:

| Grade | MUAC | WHZ | Oedema |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Severe acute malnutrition (SAM)** | below 11.5 cm | below -3 | present (kwashiorkor) |
| **Moderate acute malnutrition (MAM)** | 11.5 to below 12.5 cm | -3 to below -2 | absent |
| Normal | 12.5 cm or more | -2 or above | absent |

**Marasmus** (severe wasting, "old man" face, no oedema) comes from lack of energy; **kwashiorkor** (pitting oedema, moon face, flaky skin, sparse reddish hair, enlarged liver) comes with a lack of protein and infection; **marasmic kwashiorkor** has both. Causes in Nepal are early weaning, thin cereal gruel, repeated diarrhoea, food insecurity, poor feeding practice and lack of care. **Complications** of SAM are **hypoglycaemia, hypothermia, dehydration (hard to judge), infection, electrolyte imbalance (low potassium and magnesium), heart failure, anaemia and vitamin A lack**.

**Nepal's IMAM (Integrated Management of Acute Malnutrition) programme** works in two levels: **outpatient therapeutic care (OTC)** for SAM without complications and with appetite, using **ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF)**; and **inpatient stabilisation care (NRH or hospital)** for SAM with oedema, danger signs, poor appetite or complications. **MAM** gets supplementary feeding and counselling. In hospital the phases are **stabilisation (F-75, about 100 kcal/kg/day), transition and rehabilitation (F-100 or RUTF, 150-220 kcal/kg/day)**.

### Eight points of nursing care in severe acute malnutrition

1. **Check for danger signs and treat hypoglycaemia and hypothermia first.** Measure glucose and temperature at once. Give 10% dextrose or sugar water, keep the child warm with skin-to-skin contact and check every 2-3 hours. These two cause most early deaths.
2. **Rehydrate carefully.** Use **ReSoMal** (5 mL/kg every 30 minutes for 2 hours) by mouth or NG tube, and give IV fluid only for shock. A malnourished child's heart is weak and fast fluids cause heart failure. Weigh and count the pulse and respiration every hour.
3. **Treat infection.** Give amoxicillin for 5 days (and gentamicin or ceftriaxone if complications) even when there is no fever. Infection in SAM is often silent. Treat malaria, TB and HIV as indicated.
4. **Feed with F-75 first, then F-100 or RUTF.** Give 8-12 small feeds a day, day and night, by cup or NG tube. Start slowly, as the gut and liver are weak. Move to RUTF once the appetite returns and the oedema falls.
5. **Give micronutrients and vitamin A.** Give a single dose of vitamin A on admission (unless given in the past month), folic acid, zinc, potassium and magnesium (in the F-75 mix). Do not give iron in the first week, because it can feed bacteria. Correct each deficiency stepwise.
6. **Care for the skin, mouth and eyes.** Keep the skin dry and clean, apply zinc ointment to raw areas, treat thrush, and give eye care for vitamin A deficiency. A malnourished child's skin breaks quickly and becomes infected.
7. **Play, love and stimulation.** Hold, talk and sing to the child and encourage the mother to stay and feed. Apathy and delay in development are part of SAM. Play, with the mother's help, improves recovery and later learning.
8. **Teach the mother and arrange follow-up.** Show how to prepare thick local foods (jaulo, lito, sarbottam pitho, eggs, oil), to continue breastfeeding and to wash hands. Link her with the FCHV, the OTC visit schedule and the family's social support. Without a changed home, the child returns thin.

## 3.2 Acute respiratory infections and asthma

**Pneumonia** is the leading infectious killer of children under 5 worldwide and in Nepal. Causes are *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae* type b, RSV, influenza, measles and, in the newborn, group B streptococci and *E. coli*. Vaccines in the NIP (PCV, pentavalent with Hib, MR) are the best prevention. The IMNCI classification for a child aged 2-59 months with cough or difficult breathing is:

| Classification | Signs | Treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **Severe pneumonia or very severe disease (pink)** | Any **general danger sign**, stridor in a calm child, or SpO₂ below 90% | First dose of antibiotic, oxygen, refer urgently |
| **Pneumonia (yellow)** | **Fast breathing** (50 or more at 2-11 months, 40 or more at 12-59 months) **or chest indrawing**, no danger sign | **Amoxicillin dispersible tablets** twice daily (3 days for fast breathing, 5 days for chest indrawing); follow up in 2 days |
| **No pneumonia: cough or cold (green)** | No fast breathing or indrawing | Soothe throat, fluids, return if worse |

Count the breaths for **a full minute** when the child is calm. **Oxygen** is given when SpO₂ is **below 90%**, through nasal prongs at 1-2 L/min. **Bronchiolitis** (RSV, under 2 years) causes cough, fast breathing and wheeze; it is treated with oxygen, small frequent feeds, nasal suction and, if needed, high-flow oxygen; **antibiotics and routine bronchodilators are not needed**. **Croup** (viral laryngotracheitis) gives a barking cough, hoarse voice and stridor; treat with a single dose of **dexamethasone 0.15-0.6 mg/kg** and nebulised adrenaline for severe stridor, and keep the child calm because crying worsens the obstruction.

**Asthma** is a chronic inflammatory airway disease with wheeze, cough, chest tightness and breathlessness, worse at night or with triggers (dust, smoke from cooking fires, cold air, viral infections, pollen). The first-line **reliever** is **salbutamol 100 µg per puff through a spacer** (2-10 puffs depending on severity, repeated every 20 minutes in the first hour), plus **oral prednisolone 1-2 mg/kg (maximum 40 mg) for 3-5 days** in an attack. A **preventer** (inhaled beclomethasone or budesonide) is needed if the child wakes at night or uses the reliever more than twice a week. A **spacer** with a mask or mouthpiece works as well as a nebuliser and is cheaper, and a home-made one can be made from a 500 mL plastic bottle.

### Eight points of nursing care in childhood pneumonia

1. **Count the breaths and check danger signs.** Count the respiratory rate for 60 seconds in a calm child, look for chest indrawing, stridor and grunting, and check SpO₂. Record every 2-4 hours in severe disease. These signs decide the classification and the treatment.
2. **Give oxygen safely.** Start nasal prong oxygen at 1-2 L/min when SpO₂ is below 90%, humidify it if possible and stop when SpO₂ stays above 90% in air. Check that the prongs are in the nostrils, not blocked. Oxygen is the single most life-saving treatment in severe pneumonia.
3. **Give the antibiotic exactly as ordered.** Give oral amoxicillin (dispersible) twice daily for the full course, and in severe disease the first dose before transfer. Teach the mother the dose and why she must finish it. Half-courses make germs resistant.
4. **Position and clear the airway.** Raise the head of the bed slightly, keep the nose clear with saline drops and gentle suction in infants, and avoid forcing a lying position. Open airways make breathing easier.
5. **Keep up fluids and feeding.** Breastfeed often, give small frequent feeds and extra fluids, and use an NG tube if the child is too breathless to suck. Fast breathing loses fluid and an unfed child tires. Do not stop feeding for fear of vomiting.
6. **Control fever and comfort.** Give paracetamol 15 mg/kg every 6 hours if the temperature is above 38.5 °C, dress lightly and keep the room warm without smoke. Avoid cough syrups, which are not recommended under 2 years.
7. **Prevent spread.** Use hand washing, cover the mouth for cough, keep cooking-fire smoke away (use a chimney stove) and isolate children with measles or pertussis. Smoke and crowding are Nepal's main home risks.
8. **Teach the mother and plan follow-up.** Explain fast breathing and chest indrawing in simple words, give the review date after 2 days, and ask her to return at once if the child cannot drink, vomits everything or has fits. Complete the PCV and measles vaccines.

## 3.3 Diarrhoeal diseases and dehydration

**Diarrhoea** is three or more loose or watery stools in 24 hours. **Acute** lasts under 14 days, **persistent** 14 days or more, and **dysentery** has visible blood. In NDHS 2022, **10% of children under 5 had diarrhoea** in the previous 2 weeks and **48% received ORT**. Causes are **rotavirus, *E. coli*, *Shigella*, *Vibrio cholerae*, *Giardia* and worms**. Spread is **faecal-oral** through contaminated water, food, hands and flies. Dehydration kills; the assessment follows IMNCI:

| Dehydration | Signs (two or more) | Plan |
|---|---|---|
| **Severe** | Lethargic or unconscious; sunken eyes; not able to drink or drinks poorly; skin pinch goes back **very slowly (2 seconds or more)** | **Plan C:** IV fluids at once |
| **Some** | Restless, irritable; sunken eyes; drinks eagerly, thirsty; skin pinch goes back **slowly** | **Plan B:** ORS at the facility |
| **None** | Not enough signs | **Plan A:** home care |

**Zinc and ORS.** **ORS** carries sodium and glucose together across the gut wall; one sachet is dissolved in **1 litre of clean water** and used within 24 hours. Home alternative: 6 level teaspoons of sugar and half a teaspoon of salt in 1 litre. **Zinc** shortens the illness and prevents recurrence: Nepal's IMNCI chart gives **10 mg a day under 6 months and 20 mg a day at 6-59 months for 14 days** (WHO 2024 now suggests a lower 5 mg dose as a conditional recommendation; use the current MoHP chart). **Antibiotics are not used for watery diarrhoea.** They are for **dysentery (ciprofloxacin)**, suspected cholera in older children, and special causes.

**Other gut problems.** **Intussusception** (peak 3-36 months): sudden colicky pain with drawing up of the legs, vomiting, **red-currant-jelly stool** and a sausage-shaped mass; it is an emergency treated by air enema or surgery. **Worms** (*Ascaris*, hookworm, whipworm) cause anaemia, poor growth and rarely bowel blockage; Nepal gives **albendazole 400 mg every 6 months from 12 months** in national campaigns. **Giardiasis** causes greasy, foul stools and is treated with metronidazole. **Hirschsprung disease** and **pyloric stenosis** (projectile vomiting at 2-6 weeks, olive-shaped mass) are surgical.

### Seven points of prevention and health education for diarrhoea

1. **Teach ORS preparation and use.** Show the mother how to mix one sachet in 1 litre of boiled, cooled water, how much to give after each stool, and to throw it away after 24 hours. ORS is safe at home and prevents most diarrhoea deaths. Let her practise with a cup and spoon.
2. **Give zinc for the full 14 days.** Explain that zinc makes the illness shorter and the next episode less likely, even after the stool becomes normal. Mothers often stop at day 3. A full course also helps growth.
3. **Keep feeding and breastfeeding.** Do not starve the child or stop breast milk; give an extra meal a day for 2 weeks after recovery. Food heals the gut, and starvation causes malnutrition.
4. **Wash hands at the critical times.** Use soap and water after defecation, after cleaning a child and before cooking or feeding. This simple habit lowers diarrhoea by about one third. Place a handwashing station (tippy tap) near the toilet.
5. **Make water and food safe.** Boil, filter or chlorinate water, store it covered, keep food covered against flies and wash fruits and vegetables. This breaks the faecal-oral route.
6. **Use a toilet and dispose of child faeces safely.** Promote a latrine in every house (Nepal's open-defecation-free campaign), and bury or flush infant stools. Child faeces are as dangerous as adult faeces.
7. **Vaccinate and recognise danger signs.** Give the rotavirus vaccine at 6 and 10 weeks and measles at 9 and 15 months, and teach the mother to return for blood in stool, a sunken look, not drinking, repeated vomiting or fever lasting more than 3 days. Early return stops death.

## 3.4 Calculations

**ORS for Plan B = 75 mL × weight (kg) over 4 hours.** **Plan C** = 100 mL/kg of Ringer's lactate: infants under 12 months get 30 mL/kg in 1 hour then 70 mL/kg in 5 hours. **Drip rate (drops/min) = mL/h × drops per mL ÷ 60**; a microdrip set has 60 drops/mL (drops/min = mL/h) and a macrodrip 20 drops/mL (mL/h ÷ 3). **Holliday-Segar maintenance:** 100 mL/kg for the first 10 kg, 50 mL/kg for the next 10 kg and 20 mL/kg after that. **RUTF** gives about 500 kcal per 92 g sachet.

## Formula and fact sheet

- **MUAC:** below 11.5 cm SAM; 11.5 to below 12.5 cm MAM; 12.5 or more normal. **WHZ** below -3 SAM. Oedema of both feet = SAM. **NDHS 2022:** stunting 25%, wasting 8%, underweight 19%; diarrhoea 10%, ORT 48%.
- **SAM:** F-75 about 100 kcal/kg/day; RUTF 150-220 kcal/kg/day (175 used); 1 sachet 92 g = 500 kcal; amoxicillin 5 days; vitamin A on admission; no iron in week 1.
- **IMNCI fast breathing:** 50 or more (2-11 months); 40 or more (12-59 months). Oxygen if SpO₂ below 90%. Amoxicillin dispersible bd, 3 days (fast breathing) or 5 days (chest indrawing).
- **Dehydration:** Plan A (ORS after each stool, zinc), **Plan B 75 mL/kg in 4 h**, **Plan C 100 mL/kg** (30 then 70). **Zinc** 10 mg (under 6 months) or 20 mg (6-59 months) × 14 days (Nepal chart). **Ciprofloxacin 15 mg/kg bd × 3 days** for dysentery.
- **Asthma:** salbutamol 100 µg/puff by spacer; prednisolone 1-2 mg/kg (max 40 mg) × 3-5 days. **Croup:** dexamethasone 0.15-0.6 mg/kg.
- **Maintenance fluid:** 100/50/20 mL/kg rule.

## Chapter summary

- Acute malnutrition is graded by MUAC, WHZ and oedema; SAM with appetite is treated with RUTF at home and complicated SAM in hospital with F-75, careful rehydration, antibiotics and micronutrients.
- IMNCI finds pneumonia by fast breathing, chest indrawing and danger signs; oral amoxicillin and oxygen (SpO₂ below 90%) save lives; bronchiolitis and croup are managed supportively; asthma is treated with a spacer, steroids and preventers.
- Diarrhoea is classified by dehydration; Plans A, B and C use ORS and IV fluid by weight, with zinc for 14 days and antibiotics only for dysentery.
- Prevention is exclusive breastfeeding, complementary feeding, vaccines (PCV, rotavirus, MR), clean water, toilets and hand washing.

8. A child has a MUAC of 11.2 cm and no oedema. This is: (a) Normal (b) MAM (c) SAM (d) stunting.

::: note Answers and rationales
**1. (b).** 75 × 9 = 675 mL over 4 hours, about 169 mL/h. 450 mL is 50 mL/kg (the lower Plan B range of old charts) and 900 mL is 100 mL/kg, the Plan C total.
**2. (b).** 30 × 6 = 180 mL in the first hour for an infant under 12 months, then 70 mL/kg (420 mL) over 5 hours. 420 mL is the second phase volume.
**3. (b).** 70 × 12 = 840 mL ÷ 2.5 h = 336 mL/h. 840 mL is the volume and 168 mL/h would run it over 5 hours (the infant schedule).
**4. (b).** 1,000 + (8 × 50) = 1,400 mL/day. 1,000 mL covers only the first 10 kg; 1,800 mL is 100 mL/kg.
**5. (c).** 175 × 8 = 1,400 kcal ÷ 500 = 2.8 sachets. Giving 2 sachets under-feeds the child and slows recovery.
**6. (c).** 0.6 × 10 = 6 mg as a single dose. 60 mg is a ten-fold overdose.
**7. (b).** At 12-59 months a rate of 40 or more is fast breathing, so this is pneumonia (yellow) treated at home with amoxicillin. It would be severe only if a danger sign or SpO₂ below 90% were present.
**8. (c).** A MUAC below 11.5 cm is SAM even without oedema. MAM is 11.5 to below 12.5 cm.

**Short answer (3 marks): Differentiate marasmus from kwashiorkor.** Marasmus is severe wasting from a lack of energy: the child is very thin with a wizened "old man" face, visible ribs, no oedema, an alert but irritable mood and a normal or thin skin. Kwashiorkor comes with protein lack and infection: the child has pitting oedema of both feet and the face, apathy, sparse reddish hair, flaky "crazy-pavement" skin and an enlarged fatty liver. Both are SAM and both are treated with F-75, then F-100 or RUTF, antibiotics and micronutrients.

**Short answer (3 marks): State how to prepare ORS and give Plan A at home.** Wash hands, pour one sachet of low-osmolarity ORS into 1 litre of clean boiled and cooled water and stir. Give 50-100 mL after each loose stool to a child under 2 years and 100-200 mL to a child of 2-5 years, by cup and spoon, in small sips. Give zinc daily for 14 days, continue breastfeeding and feeding, and use the solution within 24 hours. Return if the child drinks poorly, vomits everything, has blood in the stool or becomes sicker.

**Medium answer (5 marks): Describe the management of a child with diarrhoea and some dehydration (Figure 3.3).** Classify using two or more signs: restlessness, sunken eyes, eagerness to drink and a skin pinch that goes back slowly. Give **Plan B: ORS 75 mL/kg over 4 hours** at the facility, by cup and spoon, in small sips; if the child vomits, wait 10 minutes and continue more slowly. Breastfeed between sips, give **zinc for 14 days** (10 mg under 6 months, 20 mg at 6-59 months) and vitamin A where due. **Reassess after 4 hours**: if signs have gone, use Plan A and go home with ORS; if some signs remain, repeat Plan B and offer food; if severe signs appear, go to Plan C. Do not give antibiotics unless there is blood in the stool. Counsel the mother on feeding, hand washing and danger signs, and review in 5 days.

**Long answer (10 marks): Rojina Dahal, 10 months, 7.6 kg, MUAC 11.8 cm, has had 3 days of watery diarrhoea, vomiting, fever 38.5 °C and a respiratory rate of 52. She has sunken eyes, drinks eagerly and her skin pinch goes back slowly. Classify, calculate the treatment and write the nursing care plan.**

*Classification and calculations.* Rojina has **some dehydration** (sunken eyes, thirst, slow skin pinch), **pneumonia** (respiratory rate 52 is at least 50 at 2-11 months, with no danger sign) and **moderate acute malnutrition** (MUAC 11.8 cm; her weight is **80% of the expected 9.5 kg**). Plan B ORS is 75 × 7.6 = **570 mL over 4 hours = 142.5 mL/h**. Zinc 20 mg daily for 14 days = **280 mg (14 tablets)**. Amoxicillin by formula is 40 mg/kg = 304 mg per dose; the IMNCI chart gives one 250 mg dispersible tablet twice daily (33 mg/kg), **6 tablets for 3 days**. Paracetamol 15 mg/kg is **114 mg = 4.6 mL** of 125 mg/5 mL. Had she been severely dehydrated, Plan C would be 228 mL in 1 hour then 532 mL over 5 hours (106 mL/h, 106 drops/min by microdrip).

*Management.* The nurse checks danger signs (none), counts the respiratory rate for 1 minute, and measures SpO₂ (93%, so no oxygen yet). She starts ORS by cup and spoon, 142 mL each hour, with breastfeeding between sips, and reassesses at 4 hours. She gives the first dose of amoxicillin and zinc, paracetamol for the fever, and the vitamin A and albendazole schedule is checked. She plans feeding: breast milk plus 4 meals a day of thick food with egg, oil and green leaves, and an extra meal for 2 weeks. She enrols Rojina in the MAM supplementary programme with MUAC checks every 2 weeks and counsels the mother on hand washing, safe water and rotavirus and PCV vaccines. The review is in 2 days for pneumonia and 5 days for diarrhoea.
