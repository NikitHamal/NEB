## 5.1 Recognising and treating the critically ill child

**Triage (ETAT).** In an emergency the nurse looks for **emergency signs** and acts at once: **obstructed or absent breathing, central cyanosis, severe respiratory distress, signs of shock (cold hands, capillary refill over 3 seconds, weak fast pulse), coma or convulsion, and severe dehydration**. **Priority signs** (to be seen first in the queue) include very small size (under 2 months), a very high temperature, trauma or burns, severe pallor, poisoning, severe malnutrition and oedema. Use the **ABCDE** sequence: **A**irway, **B**reathing, **C**irculation, **D**isability (AVPU, glucose) and **E**xposure (temperature, injuries). A quick weight estimate for children aged 1-10 years is **2 × (age in years + 4) kg**.

**Shock** in a child is a failure to deliver oxygen to the tissues. In Nepal the commonest causes are **dehydration, sepsis, dengue and bleeding**. Give oxygen, obtain IV or intraosseous access, and give **20 mL/kg of Ringer's lactate or saline over 5-10 minutes** (10 mL/kg if the child is severely malnourished, with careful observation) and reassess after each bolus for signs of fluid overload. **Hypoglycaemia** is treated with **2 mL/kg of 10% dextrose**.

**Choking.** In an **infant**, give **5 back blows then 5 chest thrusts**, alternately, until the object comes out or the infant becomes unresponsive. In a **child over 1 year**, give **abdominal thrusts (Heimlich manoeuvre)**. Never do blind finger sweeps. If the child becomes unresponsive start CPR.

**Drowning** is a leading cause of death in Nepali children aged 1-4 years who fall in ponds, canals and water tanks. After rescue, open the airway and give **5 rescue breaths** before compressions, because the cause is oxygen lack.

## 5.2 Poisoning, burns and injury prevention

**Poisoning.** In Nepal the common agents are **kerosene and other hydrocarbons, organophosphate and other pesticides, paracetamol, household cleaning agents, drugs and plants (datura) and, in adolescents, deliberate self-harm**. The first steps are **ABC, remove the poison (clothes, skin, eyes), identify the agent (bring the container), check the dose and time, and call the Poison Information Centre or the hospital**. **Do not induce vomiting** (especially with kerosene or corrosives, which burn again and cause **hydrocarbon pneumonitis**); do not give salt water or milk. **Activated charcoal 1 g/kg** is given within 1 hour for many drugs, but not for hydrocarbons, corrosives or alcohols. **Organophosphates** cause SLUDGE signs (salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, GI cramps, emesis), small pupils, sweating and muscle twitching; treat with **atropine 0.02-0.05 mg/kg** repeated until the chest is dry and **pralidoxime**. **Paracetamol** over **150 mg/kg** may harm the liver; give **N-acetylcysteine 150 mg/kg over 1 hour** by the nomogram. In kerosene ingestion, give oxygen, observe for **6 hours** for cough and fast breathing and take a chest X-ray if symptoms appear.

**Burns.** Burns from cooking fires, hot water and electricity are common in Nepali homes, with children under 5 at highest risk. First aid: **stop the burning, cool the burn with running water for 20 minutes, remove clothing and jewellery that is not stuck, cover with clean cling film or a clean cloth, keep the child warm and send for help**. Never apply ice, butter, toothpaste or ash. Estimate the burn area with the **Lund and Browder chart**, because a child's head is relatively large (**the rule of nines is not accurate in children**). **Refer** burns over 10% of the body, burns of face, hands, feet or genitals, circumferential and electrical burns, and inhalation injury. In large burns the fluid is by the **Parkland formula (4 mL × kg × % burn) in 24 hours, half in the first 8 hours**, plus maintenance fluid in children, titrated to a **urine output of 1 mL/kg/hour**.

**Injury prevention.** The nurse's best work is to prevent injury: **use a child restraint or car seat and helmets**, keep **stoves and hot liquids out of reach**, store **kerosene and pesticides in locked places, never in drink bottles**, fence ponds and water tanks and watch toddlers near water, keep **small objects, coins and batteries** away from infants, and protect from falls with stair gates and window grills. In a village the FCHV and the women's group can run these lessons in the mothers' group meeting.

### Eight points of nursing care in paediatric poisoning

1. **Secure the airway, breathing and circulation first.** Give oxygen if SpO₂ is below 94%, suction vomit gently and place an unconscious child in the recovery position. A clear airway saves more lives than any antidote.
2. **Find out what, how much and when.** Ask the parents, look at the container and note the time; use a pill count or the volume missing. The dose per kilogram tells whether treatment is needed.
3. **Remove the poison from the body.** Take off wet clothes, wash skin with water for 15 minutes and rinse eyes for 15 minutes. Do not induce vomiting. External decontamination prevents further absorption and also protects the nurse.
4. **Give the correct antidote or charcoal.** Give activated charcoal 1 g/kg for suitable poisons, atropine for organophosphate, N-acetylcysteine for paracetamol and naloxone for opioids, exactly as ordered. A wrong antidote or dose can harm the child.
5. **Observe closely.** Chart pulse, respiration, SpO₂, pupil size, consciousness (AVPU), blood sugar and urine output every 15-30 minutes at first. Many poisons cause late effects, so observe for at least 6 hours after kerosene ingestion.
6. **Prevent complications.** Watch for aspiration, seizures, low sugar and low temperature, and keep suction and diazepam ready. Quick action stops a minor problem becoming a death.
7. **Report and document.** Record agent, amount, times and actions and notify the doctor. Poisoning is a medico-legal case if self-harm or neglect is suspected, so report as per hospital rules.
8. **Teach prevention and give psychological support.** Teach locked storage of kerosene and pesticides, use of original labelled containers and supervision. For adolescents with self-harm give a calm approach, mhGAP assessment and referral; the national helpline is 1166. Prevention costs little and saves lives.

**Atraumatic care** is care that **prevents or reduces the physical and psychological distress** of children and families: **prevent separation (rooming-in), promote a sense of control, and prevent or reduce injury and pain**. **Family-centred care** treats the parents as partners. **Play** is the child's work: **therapeutic play** (a doll to show a procedure, a mask to rehearse oxygen) reduces fear; **expressive play** (drawing, clay, story) lets feelings out; **diversion** (bubbles, songs, stories) helps during a painful procedure. A paediatric ward in Nepal can use a play corner with low-cost materials.

**Pain relief.** Paracetamol 15 mg/kg every 6 hours and **ibuprofen 10 mg/kg every 8 hours** (over 3 months, with food) treat mild pain; a doctor orders **morphine** for severe pain (a nurse watches respiration and sedation). **Non-drug methods** are skin-to-skin, breastfeeding, **sucrose 24% 0.5-2 mL on the tongue in newborns**, warmth, distraction and **EMLA cream applied 1 hour before a needle**. Never say, "It won't hurt."

**Giving medicines to children safely.** The commonest serious error is a **ten-fold dose error**. Write the **weight on every chart**, calculate the dose in **mg/kg** and check the answer with a second nurse, use an oral syringe for liquids, never call medicine "sweets", give each drug by the five rights, and watch the drip rate of IV fluid (use a burette or pump). Check the maximum dose per day (for example paracetamol 75 mg/kg/day, maximum 4 g). IV cannulas are inserted at the dorsum of the hand or foot, taped with a clear dressing so that the site can be seen, and flushed gently. Check for **infiltration** (swelling, coolness, pain) every hour.

### Seven points of care of the hospitalised child

1. **Welcome and orient.** Greet the child by name, show the bed, toilet and call bell, and let the child keep a toy or blanket. A friendly start lowers fear and builds trust. Speak in the child's own language at his own level.
2. **Keep the mother or carer close.** Allow rooming-in and encourage the mother to feed, bathe and comfort the child. Separation is the worst stress for young children. A calm mother makes a calm child.
3. **Prepare for every procedure.** Explain in simple, honest words, show the equipment on a doll and let the child touch it. Do this just before for young children and the day before for school-age children. Prepared children cry less and cooperate more.
4. **Assess and treat pain.** Use FLACC or FACES, give the ordered drug on time, add comfort measures and reassess. Untreated pain makes the child more afraid of the next procedure. Believe what the child says.
5. **Provide play and learning.** Set up a play corner, allow drawing, stories and school work, and involve a play volunteer. Play keeps development going and makes the ward less frightening.
6. **Ensure safety.** Use cot sides or bed rails, keep medicines locked, check ID bands, and supervise the child near hot liquids and water. Children cannot judge danger. Ward falls and drug errors are preventable.
7. **Prepare for discharge from day one.** Teach the mother medicines, diet, signs of danger and follow-up with a written plan, and link her with the FCHV. A child who goes home well-taught seldom returns sicker.

## 5.4 Child rights, abuse and protection

The **UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989)**, which Nepal ratified in 1990, groups rights into **survival, development, protection and participation**. **Nepal's Act Relating to Children 2075 (2018)** defines a child as a person under 18 years, guarantees the rights to life, name, education, health and protection, and **prohibits physical and mental punishment** at home, school and elsewhere. The **Central Child Welfare Board (CCWB)** runs the **Childline helpline 1098**. Nepal's **Child Labour (Prohibition and Regulation) Act 2056** bars the work of children under 14 and hazardous work under 18, and the legal age of marriage is **20 years**. Hospitals in Nepal have **One-Stop Crisis Management Centres (OCMC)** that give medical, psychological, police and legal help for survivors of violence.

**Child abuse** is any act or neglect that harms a child. Types are **physical** (burns, bruises, fractures of different ages, shaken-baby injury), **sexual**, **emotional** and **neglect** (poor weight, dirty clothes, missed vaccines). Red flags are an **injury that does not fit the story, delay in seeking care, injuries to different stages of healing, bruises on the trunk, ears or neck, a frightened or withdrawn child, and a changing story**. Child labour, trafficking and child marriage are also forms of abuse in Nepal.

### Seven points of nursing response to child abuse

1. **Be alert for the signs.** Look at every injury and ask whether the story and the age of the child fit the injury. Check weight, hygiene, behaviour and the vaccine card. Abuse is often missed because nobody looks for it.
2. **Treat the injury and make the child safe.** Give first aid and medical care, and do not discharge a child to an unsafe place. A child's safety comes before any other task.
3. **Speak gently and listen.** Sit at the child's level, use open questions ("Tell me what happened") and never lead or force. Write the child's own words in quotation marks. A kind first response helps healing and keeps evidence clean.
4. **Document carefully.** Record date, time, injuries (with body diagram and measurements), the story and who was present, and take photographs only with consent. Clear notes help the court and protect the child.
5. **Report and refer.** Tell the doctor and the hospital social worker, and refer to the OCMC, Childline 1098 or CCWB and the police as the Act requires. Reporting is a duty and protects the child from further harm.
6. **Support the child and non-offending family.** Offer counselling and psychosocial care, and treat the parents without blame while keeping the child's safety first. Many abusers are family members; judgment can make families hide the child.
7. **Prevent through education.** Teach positive discipline, safe touch, the ban on corporal punishment, the harm of child marriage and child labour, and where to seek help. Work with schools, women's groups and FCHVs. Prevention starts in the community.

## 5.5 Calculations

**Estimated weight (1-10 years) = 2 × (age + 4) kg.** Emergency doses are by weight: **adrenaline 0.01 mg/kg = 0.1 mL/kg of 1:10,000 (0.1 mg/mL)**, **fluid bolus 20 mL/kg**, **10% dextrose 2 mL/kg**, **defibrillation 4 J/kg**. **Uncuffed tube size = age ÷ 4 + 4; cuffed = age ÷ 4 + 3.5.** **Parkland = 4 mL × kg × % burn**, half in the first 8 hours. **Holliday-Segar** gives maintenance fluid. **Paracetamol** is toxic above 150 mg/kg as a single ingestion; the daily maximum is 75 mg/kg (4 g).

## Formula and fact sheet

- **ETAT emergency signs:** airway, cyanosis, severe respiratory distress, shock, coma or convulsion, severe dehydration. **Weight** 2 × (age + 4). **Shock** 20 mL/kg Ringer's (10 mL/kg if SAM).
- **BLS:** compressions 100-120/min, depth 1/3 (infant 4 cm, child 5 cm), 30:2 (15:2 two rescuers); adrenaline 0.01 mg/kg; shock 4 J/kg; choking infant 5 back blows + 5 chest thrusts; child over 1 year abdominal thrusts.
- **Poisoning:** no induced vomiting; charcoal 1 g/kg (not hydrocarbons); atropine in organophosphate; NAC 150 mg/kg for paracetamol over 150 mg/kg; observe kerosene 6 hours.
- **Burns:** cool 20 minutes; Lund and Browder; Parkland 4 mL × kg × % half in 8 h; urine 1 mL/kg/h.
- **Pain:** FLACC under 3 years; FACES from 3 years; paracetamol 15 mg/kg, ibuprofen 10 mg/kg; EMLA 1 hour.
- **Rights:** UNCRC 1989 (ratified 1990); Act Relating to Children 2075 (child under 18, bans corporal punishment); Childline 1098; OCMC; age of marriage 20 years.

## Chapter summary

- The sick child is triaged for emergency signs and managed by ABCDE; BLS, shock fluids, choking and drowning care are life skills.
- Poisoning care avoids vomiting, uses the correct antidote and observes closely; burns need cooling, cover and weight-based fluids.
- Injury prevention (car seats, safe storage, water safety, stove safety) is the nurse's best long-term work.
- Hospitalised children need family-centred, atraumatic care, play, pain relief and safe weight-based medicines.
- Child rights and the Act Relating to Children 2075 guide the response to abuse, neglect, child labour and child marriage through OCMC and Childline 1098.

**MCQs (with reasons)**

5. Ibuprofen 10 mg/kg for a 12 kg child using 100 mg/5 mL syrup is: (a) 3 mL (b) 6 mL (c) 12 mL (d) 24 mL.
6. A 10 kg child swallows 2,000 mg of paracetamol. The dose is: (a) 20 mg/kg (b) 75 mg/kg (c) 150 mg/kg (d) 200 mg/kg.
7. A toddler scores Face 1, Legs 1, Activity 2, Cry 1 and Consolability 1 on the FLACC scale. The total and the meaning are: (a) 4, mild (b) 6, moderate (c) 8, severe (d) 10, severe.
8. The correct first aid for a choking infant who cannot cry is: (a) blind finger sweep (b) abdominal thrusts (c) 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts (d) give water.

::: note Answers and rationales
**1. (c).** 2 × (5 + 4) = 18 kg. 14 kg is the estimate for a 3-year-old and 16 kg for a 4-year-old.
**2. (b).** 0.1 mL/kg × 8 = 0.8 mL of the 1:10,000 solution. 8 mL would be a ten-fold overdose.

**Short answer (3 marks): State the first aid of a burn in a child.** Stop the burning and remove the child from the heat. Cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes, remove clothing and jewellery that is not stuck, and cover loosely with clean cling film or a clean cloth. Keep the child warm and give paracetamol for pain. Do not apply ice, butter, toothpaste or ash, and do not burst blisters. Send for medical help at once for burns over 10%, burns of the face, hands, feet or genitals, and electrical or inhalation burns.

**Short answer (3 marks): Define atraumatic care and list its three principles.** Atraumatic care is the delivery of therapeutic care through settings, personnel and interventions that remove or reduce the physical and psychological distress of children and families. Its three principles are: (1) prevent or reduce separation of the child from the family (rooming-in), (2) promote a sense of control (choices, explanation, play) and (3) prevent or minimise bodily injury and pain (EMLA, distraction, skilled and gentle procedures).

**Medium answer (5 marks): Describe the steps of paediatric basic life support (Figure 5.1).** First check that the scene is **safe**, then **tap and shout** to see if the child responds. **Shout for help** and send someone for the AED. **Open the airway** by head tilt and chin lift (neutral position in an infant) and **look, listen and feel for breathing for no more than 10 seconds**. If the child is not breathing normally, give **5 rescue breaths** (2 in some protocols), each of 1 second, and check for signs of life. If none, start **chest compressions** at the centre of the chest at **100-120 per minute**, to **one third of the chest depth** (4 cm in infants with two fingers or two thumbs, 5 cm in a child with one or two hands), at a ratio of **30:2** (15:2 with two rescuers). Attach the **AED** as soon as it arrives, using paediatric pads, and follow its voice instructions. Continue until the child recovers, the team takes over or the doctor stops. Give adrenaline 0.01 mg/kg as the team directs.

**Long answer (10 marks): Sumit Gurung, 3 years, found with an open kerosene bottle, is coughing, has vomited once and has a respiratory rate of 44, pulse 128 and SpO₂ 91%. *Calculations.* Sumit's **estimated weight is 2 × (3 + 4) = 14 kg**. *Management.* The nurse tells the mother to stop and not to induce vomiting, wipes any kerosene from his skin and removes wet clothes. She checks airway, breathing and circulation, gives **oxygen to keep SpO₂ at 94% or more**, sits him upright and suctions only visible vomit. The nurse keeps the child nil by mouth until the cough settles, then starts small sips. She comforts the mother without blame, and before discharge teaches **locked storage and original labelled containers** for kerosene and pesticides and reports the event for injury surveillance.
