## 1.1 The female reproductive organs (review)

The **external genitalia** (vulva) are the mons pubis, labia majora and minora, clitoris, vestibule, Bartholin's glands and the vaginal opening. The **vagina** is a 7-9 cm muscular canal; its acid pH (3.8-4.5, from lactobacilli) protects against infection. The **uterus** is a pear-shaped muscle, about 7.5 cm long and 50-60 g in the non-pregnant woman, with a **fundus**, **body** and **cervix**; its wall has three layers: **perimetrium**, **myometrium** (three muscle layers that squeeze blood vessels shut after birth, the "living ligatures") and **endometrium**, which thickens each cycle. The two **fallopian tubes** (about 10 cm) have four parts: interstitial, isthmus, **ampulla** (where fertilisation happens) and infundibulum with fimbriae. The two **ovaries** hold the follicles and make oestrogen and progesterone. Plate 1.1 (a) shows these parts on a hysterosalpingogram, in which dye outlines the cavity and tubes.

**Support of the uterus.** The uterus is held by **ligaments** (broad, round, uterosacral, cardinal or Mackenrodt's, pubocervical) and by the **pelvic floor muscles** (levator ani). Weak support after many births causes prolapse, which is common among Nepali women who return to heavy load-carrying soon after delivery. **Blood supply:** uterine arteries (from the internal iliac) and ovarian arteries (from the aorta). **Nerve supply:** the sympathetic and parasympathetic plexuses (pelvic or Frankenhauser's plexus), which carry labour pain to T10-L1.

**Menstrual cycle.** A cycle of 28 days (range 21-35) has a **follicular phase** (FSH ripens a follicle; oestrogen builds the endometrium), **ovulation** (an LH surge, about 36 hours before the egg is released) and a **luteal phase** (the corpus luteum makes progesterone, a fixed 14 days). If the egg is not fertilised the corpus luteum fails and menstruation follows. **Menopause** is the last period, confirmed after 12 months without one (median age near 49-50 years). The **breast** has 15-20 lobes, each draining by a lactiferous duct to the nipple; **prolactin** makes milk and **oxytocin** ejects it (Unit 5).

## 1.2 The bony pelvis: parts, landmarks, joints and ligaments

The pelvis is a bony ring made of **two innominate (hip) bones**, the **sacrum** and the **coccyx**. Each hip bone is the fusion of the **ilium, ischium and pubis**. The ring is divided by the **linea terminalis (pelvic brim)**, running from the sacral promontory along the iliopectineal line to the upper edge of the pubic symphysis. Above the brim is the **false pelvis** (supports the pregnant uterus and has no obstetric limit); below it is the **true pelvis**, the bony birth canal, with an **inlet** (brim), a **cavity** (midpelvis) and an **outlet**.

**Joints and ligaments.** There are four joints: two **sacroiliac** joints, the **symphysis pubis** (fibrocartilage) and the **sacrococcygeal** joint. In late pregnancy **relaxin and progesterone** soften the ligaments so the joints give a little; the coccyx can move back by about 1-2 cm in labour, increasing the outlet. Strong sacrotuberous and sacrospinous ligaments bind the sacrum to the ischial tuberosity and ischial spine. The **ischial spines** are the key midpelvic landmarks: they are felt on vaginal examination and **station** is measured from them.

**Diameters of the true pelvis.** Figure 1.1 shows the inlet. The inlet is a transverse oval (kidney or round in other types); the cavity is nearly circular; the outlet is a diamond shaped from front to back. The fetal head meets a **different widest dimension at each level**, so it must turn (rotate) as it descends, which is the reason for the cardinal movements of labour (Unit 4).

**Pelvic types (Caldwell and Moloy).** **Gynaecoid** (about 50% of women): round inlet, wide subpubic arch, best for vaginal birth. **Android** (male type, about 20%): heart-shaped, narrow forepelvis, prominent spines; the head gets stuck at the midpelvis (deep transverse arrest). **Anthropoid** (about 25%): long oval in the AP direction; head enters occipito-posterior or direct. **Platypelloid** (about 5%): flat, oval transversely; the head enters sideways and may stop at the brim. Causes of contraction include **poor childhood nutrition and rickets** (vitamin D and calcium shortage), early pregnancy before the pelvis is mature (under 18 years) and old fractures. Short stature under 145 cm, unengaged head in a primigravida at term, and a history of obstructed labour are the alert signs in the field.

**Moulding, caput and cephalhaematoma.** *Moulding* is overlapping of the vault bones: mild is normal, severe moulding with bones that do not separate on pressure signals **cephalopelvic disproportion**. *Caput succedaneum* is oedema of the scalp over the presenting part, crosses suture lines, is present at birth and vanishes within 2-3 days. *Cephalhaematoma* is bleeding under the periosteum, is limited by the suture line, appears 12-24 hours after birth and takes weeks to clear; it can raise jaundice. The **anterior fontanelle** also gives a clinical sign: sunken in dehydration and bulging in raised intracranial pressure, for example meningitis.

## 1.4 Fertilisation, implantation and early development

After ovulation the egg lives about **12-24 hours** and sperm up to **5 days**, so the **fertile window** is the five days before ovulation plus the day itself. Fertilisation takes place in the **ampulla** of the tube. The **zygote** divides (cleavage) into a 16-cell **morula** (day 3-4) and then a **blastocyst** (day 5) with an inner cell mass (the embryo) and an outer **trophoblast** (the placenta and membranes). **Implantation** begins about **6-7 days after fertilisation**, when the trophoblast burrows into the secretory endometrium, which is now called the **decidua**. The trophoblast makes **hCG**, which keeps the corpus luteum alive; hCG is what pregnancy tests detect, about 10-14 days after fertilisation. The **embryonic period** (to 8 weeks) forms every organ, so this is the time of greatest teratogenic risk: folic acid, rubella immunity and avoiding alcohol and unprescribed drugs must be advised **before** conception (Unit 2). The **fetal period** (9 weeks to birth) is growth and maturation.

```python
cycle = 32                                  # days; luteal phase is a fixed 14 days
ovulation = cycle - 14
window = (ovulation - 5, ovulation)         # fertile window, 5 days before to ovulation day
implant = ovulation + 7                     # about 6-7 days after fertilisation
missed = cycle + 1
dc = 12.0                                   # diagonal conjugate measured on examination, cm
oc = dc - 1.5                               # obstetric conjugate = DC minus 1.5 to 2 cm
afi = round(4.2 + 3.8 + 5.1 + 4.9, 1)       # four quadrants of amniotic fluid
baby, placenta = 3000, 500                  # grams at term
ratio = baby // placenta
print(f"Ovulation day {ovulation}; fertile window days {window[0]}-{window[1]}; implantation about day {implant}; first missed period day {missed}")
print(f"Diagonal conjugate {dc} cm -> obstetric conjugate {oc} cm ({'adequate' if oc >= 10 else 'contracted'}); AFI {afi} cm (normal 5-25); placenta : baby = 1 : {ratio}")
```

A woman with a 32-day cycle ovulates on **day 18** (32 - 14), her fertile window is **days 13-18**, implantation is expected near **day 25**, and her missed period falls on **day 33**. A diagonal conjugate of 12.0 cm gives an **obstetric conjugate of 10.5 cm**, which is adequate (at least 10 cm). An amniotic fluid index of **18.0 cm** is normal (5-25 cm), and a 500 g placenta for a 3,000 g baby is the usual ratio of **1 : 6**.

## 1.5 The placenta, membranes, liquor and cord

The **placenta** forms from the trophoblast (fetal part, the **chorionic villi**) and the decidua basalis (maternal part). Maternal blood from the **spiral arteries** bathes the villi in the intervillous space; fetal and maternal blood **never mix** and are separated by the thin placental membrane. At term the placenta is round, **15-20 cm** across, **2.5 cm** thick, weighs about **500 g** (one-sixth of the baby) and has **15-20 lobes (cotyledons)** on the maternal side (Plate 1.2 a). The **fetal surface** is smooth and shiny, covered by the amnion, with the cord attached near the centre. After delivery the midwife **always inspects the placenta, membranes and cord** for completeness: a missing lobe or a torn vessel running to the edge (succenturiate lobe) can cause postpartum haemorrhage.

**Membranes and liquor.** The **amnion** (inner, strong) and **chorion** (outer) enclose the **amniotic fluid (liquor)**, about **800-1,000 mL at term** (peak 1 L at 36-37 weeks). It cushions, keeps temperature steady, lets the baby move and develop the lungs and gut, and gives a clean flow in labour. **Polyhydramnios** (AFI above 25 cm; diabetes, oesophageal atresia) and **oligohydramnios** (AFI below 5 cm; growth restriction, ruptured membranes, post-term) both signal risk. **Meconium-stained liquor** (green) in labour warns of fetal distress.

**The umbilical cord** is 50-60 cm long and about 2 cm thick, with **two arteries** (carry deoxygenated blood *to* the placenta) and **one vein** (carries oxygenated blood *to* the baby) embedded in **Wharton's jelly**, which protects them from kinking. A single umbilical artery is linked with other anomalies, so the midwife counts the cord vessels at every birth.

## 1.6 Fetal circulation

The baby's lungs are filled with fluid and do not work, so the placenta is the lung, gut and kidney. Three **shunts** send blood past the liver and lungs (Figure 1.3). **Umbilical vein** blood, about **80% saturated**, goes through the **ductus venosus** to the inferior vena cava and right atrium; most passes through the **foramen ovale** to the left atrium, left ventricle and aorta, so the **brain and heart get the best-oxygenated blood**. Blood from the head returns through the superior vena cava to the right ventricle, then the pulmonary trunk; because lung resistance is high, most crosses the **ductus arteriosus** to the descending aorta. The two **umbilical arteries** return blood to the placenta.

**At birth.** The first breath expands the lungs, resistance falls, pulmonary blood flow rises and left atrial pressure exceeds right, **closing the foramen ovale**; higher oxygen constricts the **ductus arteriosus**. Clamping the cord removes the low-resistance placenta and raises systemic pressure. A persistent ductus arteriosus gives a machine-like murmur in a preterm baby, and a baby with a heart defect may become blue as the ductus closes (Child Health Nursing).

## 1.7 Eight functions of the placenta

1. **Respiration.** Oxygen passes from the mother's blood to the baby by simple diffusion, and carbon dioxide passes back. Fetal haemoglobin binds oxygen more tightly than adult haemoglobin, which pulls oxygen across. Reduced blood flow in pre-eclampsia or smoking starves the baby of oxygen.
2. **Nutrition.** Glucose crosses by facilitated diffusion, amino acids and calcium by active transport, and fatty acids and vitamins also cross. This is why the mother's diet, iron and folic acid matter. A very thin or anaemic mother gives a small placenta and a small baby.
3. **Excretion.** Urea, uric acid, creatinine and bilirubin travel from the baby to the mother's blood and leave through her kidneys and liver. The unborn baby therefore does not need working kidneys to stay clean. After birth the newborn's own organs must take over.
4. **Protection.** The placenta passes **IgG** antibodies from about 28 weeks, giving the newborn passive immunity against tetanus (from maternal Td), measles and others. It also blocks many bacteria. Preterm babies miss most of this gift.
5. **Hormone production.** The placenta makes **hCG** (maintains the corpus luteum), **oestrogen and progesterone** (take over from the corpus luteum at 8-12 weeks), **human placental lactogen** (hPL, raises maternal glucose) and relaxin. hPL is the reason pregnancy is diabetogenic, so gestational diabetes is screened at 24-28 weeks.
6. **Storage and metabolism.** It stores glycogen, iron and calcium and makes glycogen, cholesterol and fatty acids for the baby. It also breaks down some drugs and hormones with enzymes. The store is used when the mother's intake falls briefly.
7. **A leaky barrier.** The placenta stops large molecules but lets alcohol, nicotine, many drugs and viruses (**rubella, CMV, HIV, Zika**) and *Treponema* cross. Small amounts of fetal red cells can leak into the mother's blood, so an **Rh-negative** mother with an Rh-positive baby needs anti-D within 72 hours. Never call it a complete barrier.
8. **Immune tolerance.** The trophoblast hides from the mother's immune cells and is not rejected, though half of the baby's genes come from the father. Decidual cells shape this tolerance. Failure of placental development is linked to recurrent miscarriage, pre-eclampsia and growth restriction.

## 1.8 Eight effects of the pelvis, joints and muscles on labour

1. **Inlet shape.** A round (gynaecoid) inlet lets the head engage easily, but a flat or narrow inlet blocks engagement. In a primigravida the head normally engages by 36-38 weeks; **a head that is still high at term needs assessment**. The obstetric conjugate of at least 10 cm is the number to remember.
2. **Midpelvis and ischial spines.** The narrowest part is between the ischial spines (10.5 cm). Prominent spines in an android pelvis arrest the head here. This is why a labour that stops at station 0 to +1 is examined carefully.
3. **Outlet and subpubic arch.** A wide arch (90 degrees or more) lets the head extend under the symphysis; a narrow arch pushes the head back onto the perineum and increases tears. The bi-tuberous diameter below 8 cm suggests a narrow outlet.
4. **Joint softening.** Relaxin and progesterone loosen the sacroiliac joints and symphysis, adding 1-2 cm to some diameters. This helps labour but also causes **symphysis pain and a waddling gait**, a minor disorder of pregnancy (Unit 3). Squatting and the lateral position widen the outlet further.
5. **Coccygeal movement.** The coccyx moves backward during the second stage, increasing the AP outlet by 1-2 cm. A woman lying flat on her back loses this gain. Upright and side-lying positions are better and are advised in WHO's 2018 intrapartum guideline.
6. **Inclination and axis.** The brim is tilted about **55-60 degrees**, and the birth canal curves forward (the curve of Carus). The head first moves down and back, then forward under the pubic arch. The midwife guides the baby along this curve.
7. **Levator ani and rotation.** The levator ani muscles form a funnel; the descending head meets them and **rotates** so that the occiput comes forward. Weak or scarred muscles delay rotation. After birth they must be rebuilt with **pelvic floor (Kegel) exercises**, taught from the first postnatal day.
8. **Perineum and long-term effect.** The perineal body and the muscles stretch up to 3-4 times their length. Fast delivery of the head, a large baby, and lack of support cause tears. Obstetric fistula after obstructed labour is still reported in Nepal; early referral prevents it.

## Formula and fact sheet

- Pelvic inlet: AP (obstetric conjugate) at least 10 cm; transverse 13 cm; oblique 12 cm. **OC = diagonal conjugate - 1.5 to 2 cm.** Interspinous 10.5 cm; outlet AP 12.5 cm, bi-tuberous 11 cm. Types: gynaecoid 50%, android 20%, anthropoid 25%, platypelloid 5%.
- Fetal skull: SOB 9.5 cm (vertex flexed), OF 11.5, MV 14 (brow), submentobregmatic 9.5 (face), BPD 9.5; anterior fontanelle closes at 18 months, posterior at 6-8 weeks.
- Ovulation = cycle length minus 14; implantation 6-7 days after fertilisation; hCG detectable 10-14 days after fertilisation.
- Placenta: 500 g, 15-20 cm, 15-20 lobes; cord 50-60 cm, 2 arteries + 1 vein; liquor 800-1,000 mL, AFI 5-25 cm.
- Fetal shunts: ductus venosus, foramen ovale, ductus arteriosus.

## Chapter summary

- The true pelvis is the bony birth canal; the obstetric conjugate (at least 10 cm), the interspinous diameter (10.5 cm) and the subpubic arch decide whether the head can pass.
- The fetal head is the part that must pass; flexion presents the smallest (SOB 9.5 cm) diameter, and moulding is limited by the pelvis.
- Fertilisation occurs in the ampulla; implantation 6-7 days later; hCG makes the pregnancy test positive.
- The placenta breathes, feeds, excretes, protects, secretes hormones, stores and tolerates; it is a leaky barrier. Always inspect it after delivery.
- Fetal blood bypasses the lungs and liver through three shunts that close after birth.

**Short answer (3 marks): List the functions of the placenta.** The placenta carries out **respiration** (gas exchange), **nutrition**, **excretion** of fetal waste, **protection** (IgG antibodies from 28 weeks), **hormone production** (hCG, oestrogen, progesterone, hPL), **storage** of glycogen and iron, and **immune tolerance**. It is only a partial barrier: alcohol, nicotine, many drugs and viruses cross it, and so does fetal blood, which is why anti-D is given to Rh-negative mothers.

**Medium answer (5 marks): Describe the fetal skull and its diameters, with a labelled diagram (Figure 1.2).** The skull has a vault of two frontal, two parietal and one occipital bones, joined by the **sagittal, coronal, frontal and lambdoid sutures**; at their junctions are the **anterior fontanelle** (diamond, 3-4 cm, closes at 18 months) and the **posterior fontanelle** (triangle, closes at 6-8 weeks). The vault bones overlap in labour (moulding). The **vertex** lies between the fontanelles and the parietal eminences. The important diameters are the **suboccipitobregmatic 9.5 cm** (well-flexed vertex, the smallest presented), **occipitofrontal 11.5 cm** (deflexed, occipito-posterior), **submentobregmatic 9.5 cm** (face), **mentovertical 14 cm** (brow, the largest, cannot deliver) and **biparietal 9.5 cm** (the widest transverse). The midwife uses the fontanelles on vaginal examination to find the **position** of the head. Mild moulding is normal; severe moulding with a high head suggests disproportion. Caput is oedema that crosses sutures; cephalhaematoma is bleeding limited by one bone.

**Long answer (10 marks): Sunita, 19, a primigravida, 143 cm tall, comes to a hill-district birthing centre at 38 weeks. The fundal height is 37 cm and the head is not engaged (5/5 palpable). Her diagonal conjugate is 11.5 cm. Calculate the obstetric conjugate, explain the significance and write the nursing care plan.**

*Calculation and meaning.* The obstetric conjugate is DC - 1.5 = 11.5 - 1.5 = **10.0 cm**, at the lower limit of normal (10 cm or more); with short height (under 145 cm), a young age (the pelvis may not be fully grown), and a head that is **not engaged at 38 weeks in a primigravida**, she has a **high risk of cephalopelvic disproportion and obstructed labour**. Her amniotic fluid index of 13.0 cm (3.0 + 2.5 + 4.0 + 3.5) is normal. The finding is *not* a diagnosis of contracted pelvis; it is a signal that **the birth must happen in a facility able to do a caesarean section (CEONC)**, such as the district hospital or Paropakar Maternity Hospital in Thapathali, under the Aama programme, which pays transport and covers the delivery. A trial of labour under skilled supervision with a Labour Care Guide is acceptable only if the fetal head descends and labour progresses.

*Care.* The midwife explains the finding gently and in Nepali, involves the mother-in-law and husband, and agrees a **birth preparedness plan** (place, transport, money, blood donor, escort). She checks vital signs, fetal heart rate and fetal movements, gives iron and calcium, teaches danger signs (bleeding, leaking, reduced movements, headache, blurred vision, severe pain), arranges transfer at about 37-38 weeks to wait near the hospital, and documents the pelvic assessment. In labour she records the head's descent and moulding at each examination, and refers at once if the head fails to descend, labour is prolonged or there is fetal distress.
