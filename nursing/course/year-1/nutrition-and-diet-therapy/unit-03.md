The nutritional needs of a person change at every stage of life, and the first 1,000 days from conception to the child's second birthday decide growth, brain and adult health. In Nepal, NDHS 2022 found that only **56%** of infants under six months are exclusively breastfed, that 25% of children are stunted and 8% wasted, and that the national Integrated Management of Acute Malnutrition (IMAM) programme, together with Baal Vita sprinkles and the Multi-Sector Nutrition Plan III, tries to close these gaps. This unit follows the life cycle, then turns to breastfeeding, complementary feeding and the nurse's management of severe acute malnutrition, with all doses and volumes computed by program.

## 3.1 Nutrition in pregnancy and lactation

A pregnant woman needs about **350 kcal extra a day in the second trimester and 450 kcal in the third**, with extra protein, iron, folate, calcium, iodine and vitamin A (not in high doses). The **recommended weight gain** depends on the pre-pregnancy BMI (Institute of Medicine): underweight 12.5-18 kg, **normal 11.5-16 kg**, overweight 7-11.5 kg and obese 5-9 kg. A woman who weighed 48 kg before pregnancy and is 154 cm tall has a BMI of 48 ÷ 1.54² = **20.2**, which is normal, so her target gain is 11.5-16 kg, about 0.4 kg a week in the second and third trimesters. In Nepal the safe motherhood package gives **180 IFA tablets, calcium, albendazole after the first trimester and iodised salt advice**, and a woman is told to eat one extra meal a day. Low weight gain, short height under 145 cm and anaemia predict low birth weight (below 2,500 g).

A **lactating woman** needs about **500 kcal extra** and 20-25 g more protein, with extra fluids (about 700 mL of milk is made daily). The best food is the family's food made richer: pulses, egg, milk, green vegetables, ghee and fruit, and not the harmful taboo foods that some communities enforce after childbirth. She should be told that **the more the baby suckles, the more milk she makes**, and that a mother of any size can breastfeed.

## 3.2 Breastfeeding and complementary feeding

**Breast milk** is the ideal first food: it gives the right energy and protein, antibodies (IgA), lactoferrin, growth factors and enough water. It lowers the risk of diarrhoea, pneumonia, otitis, necrotising enterocolitis, later obesity and diabetes, and for the mother it reduces bleeding, breast and ovarian cancer and gives lactational amenorrhoea. **Colostrum**, the thick yellow first milk, is the baby's first vaccine and must never be discarded. The **Mother's Milk Substitutes (Control of Sale and Distribution) Act** of Nepal protects breastfeeding from advertising of formula, bottles and teats.

Nepal follows the WHO and UNICEF recommendation: **start within one hour of birth, exclusive breastfeeding for six months** (no water, honey or *nwaran* feeds), then continue with complementary food to **two years or more**. A good **latch** has the mouth wide open, chin touching the breast, more areola visible above than below, lips turned out, and slow deep sucks with swallowing. Feed on demand, 8-12 times in 24 hours. Cracked nipples, engorgement and mastitis are managed by correcting the latch, frequent feeding and warm compresses.

**Complementary feeding** starts at **6 months**, because milk alone no longer gives enough energy and iron. At 6-8 months the food must supply about **202 kcal a day**, at 9-11 months about **307 kcal** and at 12-23 months about **548 kcal**; these are the differences between total energy needs (615, 686 and 894 kcal) and the energy that breast milk supplies (413, 379 and 346 kcal). Give thick *lito* or *jaulo* with a spoon of ghee or oil, mashed dal, egg, vegetable and fruit, 2-3 meals at 6-8 months, 3-4 meals at 9-11 months and 3-4 meals plus snacks at 12-23 months. *Sarbottam pitho*, a flour of 50% cereal and 50% pulses, and **Baal Vita** micronutrient powder sachets (one a day for 60 days, three cycles between 6 and 23 months) are promoted by the MoHP to improve iron and vitamin intake.

```

A 4.8-kg infant needs about 150 mL/kg/day, which is **720 mL in a day or 90 mL per feed** over 8 feeds; breastfed infants take what they need on demand. The three complementary energy gaps are 202, 307 and 548 kcal, so the amount and energy density of food must rise with age. A **rule of thumb** for a thick porridge is 2-3 teaspoons of oil or ghee in each bowl, a fist of cereal and a spoon of mashed dal.

## 3.3 Childhood, adolescence and old age

A **preschool child** (1-5 years) grows slowly, eats small amounts and needs 3 meals and 2 snacks of family food, 1,000-1,400 kcal and about 1 g protein per kg. Growth is checked monthly on the card up to two years and every three months to five years. **School children** need a breakfast and midday meal; the national school meal programme gives a cooked or snack meal in many districts. **Adolescents**, with a growth spurt and (in girls) menstrual loss, have a high need for iron, calcium and protein, and in Nepal girls aged 10-19 receive **weekly iron-folic acid tablets** through schools. Early marriage and early pregnancy sharply raise the risk of anaemia and low birth weight. The **elderly** have a lower BMR and lower activity so need fewer calories but the same protein and micronutrients (0.8-1.0 g/kg), with attention to chewing, taste, loneliness, constipation and calcium and vitamin D for bone.

## 3.4 Protein-energy malnutrition and its management

**Protein-energy malnutrition (PEM)** is a spectrum from mild underweight to severe disease. **Severe acute malnutrition (SAM)** is defined by any of: **weight-for-height Z-score below −3, mid-upper-arm circumference below 115 mm in a child 6-59 months, or bilateral pitting oedema**. **Kwashiorkor** shows oedema, a swollen face, flaky skin, thin hair and a fatty liver, with a relatively preserved weight because of fluid; **marasmus** shows severe wasting with a wizened face and ribs visible; **marasmic-kwashiorkor** shows both. The **Gomez classification** uses weight-for-age as a percentage of the reference median: 75-89% mild, 60-74% moderate, below 60% severe; the **Waterlow classification** separates **wasting** (low weight-for-height, recent) from **stunting** (low height-for-age, long-standing).

The cause is rarely one thing: poor feeding, repeated diarrhoea, measles and infection, poverty, a short birth interval, maternal illiteracy and food insecurity all feed in. The body adapts by lowering metabolism, so a child with SAM has **hypothermia, hypoglycaemia, hypokalaemia, infection with few signs, dehydration that is hard to judge and a weak heart**; these are the reasons for the careful WHO 10 steps. Under **Nepal's IMAM guideline** a child with SAM who has a **good appetite and no complications** (passes the appetite test) is treated at home through the **outpatient therapeutic care (OTC)** programme with **RUTF**, weekly weighing and routine antibiotics; a child with **oedema of ++ or +++, poor appetite, vomiting, pneumonia, fever, hypothermia or dehydration** goes to **inpatient stabilisation care** with **F-75**, then **F-100** or RUTF.

For an 8-kg child the home RUTF dose is **1,600 kcal a day, 3.2 sachets a day, 23 sachets a week**; in hospital, F-75 130 mL/kg/day is **1,040 mL/day or 130 mL every 3 hours**, and in the rehabilitation phase F-100 at 200 mL/kg is **1,600 mL/day**. The goal is a gain of **40 g a day** (5 g/kg/day). A child of 6.2 kg whose reference median is 9.6 kg is at **64.6%** of the median, moderate by the Gomez grade (but SAM by MUAC or oedema, which override). Give **vitamin A** on day 1, **amoxicillin** for 5 days, **folic acid**, a **multivitamin and mineral mix** without iron at first, **zinc**, and keep the child warm; feed 8 times a day including at night; avoid IV fluids unless shock, because they overload a weak heart. **Refeeding** must be gradual, and the child is discharged when the weight-for-height reaches −2 Z or the MUAC is 125 mm or more, with oedema gone for two weeks.

## 3.5 The nurse's role in infant feeding and the care of malnourished children: eight points

1. **Support early and exclusive breastfeeding.** Help the mother put the baby to the breast within one hour of birth, with skin contact. Teach the latch and positions, and say no water or other feeds in the first six months. Praise good practice, because confidence is the greatest help to a new mother.
2. **Teach correct complementary feeding.** From six months teach thick foods with oil, dal, egg and vegetables in the right number of meals for age. Show how to prepare *jaulo* or *sarbottam pitho* in a demonstration. Advise active feeding: sit with the child, use a clean spoon and never force.
3. **Monitor growth regularly.** Weigh each child on schedule, plot it on the growth card and see the line. A flat or falling line means counselling and a search for illness, even when the child looks well. Share the curve with the mother in simple words.
4. **Screen for malnutrition at every contact.** Use MUAC, oedema check and weight-for-height. Classify as SAM, MAM or normal and act the same day. Early detection prevents admissions, and a MUAC tape costs little.
5. **Manage SAM by protocol.** Do the appetite test, check for complications and decide OTC or inpatient care. Give RUTF in weighed amounts, routine antibiotics and vitamin A. Keep the child warm and feed frequently.
6. **Prevent and treat hypoglycaemia and hypothermia.** Feed every 2-3 hours day and night, check temperature and blood sugar on admission, and cover the child and use kangaroo care. These two problems cause many early deaths and are easily preventable.
7. **Counsel the family and treat the causes.** Look for diarrhoea, TB, HIV, measles and poverty, and link the family to social support and agriculture programmes. Teach handwashing, safe water, immunisation and spacing of births. Treat the household, not only the child.
8. **Record, report and follow up.** Fill in the IMAM register, weight chart and HMIS report, and follow absent children by an FCHV visit. Discharge only after the criteria are met and keep the child under growth monitoring for months. Good records show whether the programme works.

## Formula and fact sheet

- Pregnancy extra energy 350 kcal (2nd trimester), 450 kcal (3rd); lactation 500 kcal; weight gain 11.5-16 kg for normal BMI.
- Exclusive breastfeeding 6 months, complementary food from 6 months, continue to 2 years; food energy gap 202, 307 and 548 kcal.
- SAM: WHZ below −3, MUAC below 115 mm or bilateral oedema. RUTF 200 kcal/kg/day (500 kcal per 92 g sachet); F-75 130 mL/kg/day; F-100 200 mL/kg/day; gain 5 g/kg/day.
- Gomez: 75-89% mild, 60-74% moderate, below 60% severe. Nepal: stunting 25%, wasting 8%, EBF 56% (NDHS 2022).

## Chapter summary

- Needs rise in pregnancy and lactation and fall in energy but not in nutrients in old age.
- Breast milk alone for six months, then thick complementary food with continued breastfeeding, is the Nepal message.
- SAM is found by MUAC, oedema or WHZ; the appetite test and complications decide between RUTF at home and F-75 in hospital.
- The nurse promotes feeding, monitors growth, manages SAM and treats the causes.

## Practice

**MCQs (with reasons)**

1. A 6-kg child with SAM treated at home needs RUTF of 200 kcal/kg/day. The number of 500-kcal sachets daily is: (a) 1.2 (b) 2.4 (c) 3.0 (d) 4.8.
2. F-75 at 130 mL/kg/day for a 5-kg child gives: (a) 325 mL (b) 500 mL (c) 650 mL (d) 1,000 mL a day.
3. A child of 5.0 kg whose reference median is 8.0 kg has a weight-for-age of: (a) 37.5% (b) 62.5% (c) 75% (d) 160%.
4. The most important sign that separates kwashiorkor from marasmus is: (a) wasting (b) bilateral pitting oedema (c) low weight (d) a poor appetite.
5. The correct age to start complementary feeding is: (a) 2 months (b) 4 months (c) 6 months (d) 12 months.

::: note Answers and rationales
**1. (b).** 200 × 6 = 1,200 kcal ÷ 500 = 2.4 sachets; (c) is for a 7.5-kg child. Dose is always calculated from the present weight.
**2. (c).** 130 × 5 = 650 mL/day, given in 8 feeds of about 81 mL; this is the stabilisation volume, not the catch-up volume.
**3. (b).** 5.0 ÷ 8.0 × 100 = 62.5%, moderate by Gomez (60-74%); the 37.5% answer wrongly divides the median by the weight.
**4. (b).** Bilateral pitting oedema defines kwashiorkor; wasting and low weight occur in both and a poor appetite is common to both.
**5. (c).** At six months energy and iron needs exceed what milk gives, and the gut and kidneys can handle other foods; before 6 months it risks infection and shortens breastfeeding.
:::

**Short answer (3 marks): Advantages of breastfeeding.** Breast milk has ideal nutrients and enough water, and gives immunity through IgA, lactoferrin and white cells, so infants have fewer diarrhoea, chest and ear infections. It is clean, free, always warm and available. Suckling releases oxytocin that reduces postpartum bleeding and builds bonding, and exclusive feeding delays the return of ovulation. It lowers later obesity and diabetes in the child and breast and ovarian cancer in the mother.

**Medium answer (5 marks): Differentiate kwashiorkor from marasmus (Figure 3.1d) and state the nurse's role.** **Kwashiorkor** is mainly protein deficiency with enough energy: the child has pitting oedema, a moon-shaped face, flaky pigmented skin, thin light hair, an enlarged fatty liver, apathy and irritability, and the weight can look near-normal because of fluid. **Marasmus** is overall deficiency of energy and protein: severe wasting, loss of fat and muscle, an old man's face, visible ribs, no oedema, but the child is alert and hungry. Marasmic-kwashiorkor shows both. The nurse measures MUAC and weight, checks oedema, does the appetite test, gives RUTF or F-75 by protocol, treats infection, hypoglycaemia and hypothermia, and counsels the family on feeding.

**Long answer (10 marks): Rohit, an 18-month-old boy, is brought to the health post with weight 7.2 kg, MUAC 109 mm and visible wasting. He has no oedema, refuses the RUTF taste test, has had loose stools for 5 days, temperature 35.8 °C and is lethargic. Classify and manage him, and write the care plan.**

*Classification.* MUAC 109 mm is below 115 mm, so he has **severe acute malnutrition (marasmus)**. He **fails the appetite test**, is **lethargic and hypothermic**, and has diarrhoea, so he has **complicated SAM** and needs **inpatient stabilisation care** (and urgent referral from the health post to a hospital with an SC unit, with the child kept warm during transport).

*Management.* **Step 1-3:** treat and prevent hypoglycaemia (feed or give 10% sugar water 50 mL if awake, glucose 10% IV if unconscious), hypothermia (skin-to-skin or kangaroo care, cover) and dehydration (ReSoMal 5 mL/kg every 30 minutes for 2 hours orally or by NG tube; avoid IV unless shock). **Step 4-6:** treat infection with antibiotics, correct micronutrients (vitamin A, folic acid, zinc, potassium and magnesium through the mix; **no iron** at first) and start cautious feeding. **F-75: 130 mL/kg/day = 7.2 × 130 = 936 mL/day, i.e. 117 mL every 3 hours (8 feeds)**, including at night. **Step 7-10:** when appetite returns and complications settle (2-7 days), change to F-100 or RUTF; RUTF at 200 kcal/kg = **1,440 kcal = 2.9 sachets a day**; aim at **36 g/day gain** (5 g/kg/day), give sensory stimulation and play, immunise, and prepare for discharge when WHZ is −2 or MUAC 125 mm. Teach the mother to give thick food, to practise handwashing and safe water, and arrange follow-up through the health post and the FCHV.
