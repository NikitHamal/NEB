## 1.1 Concept of health

The **WHO (1948)** defines health as *a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity*. Critics say the word "complete" makes health impossible to reach, so modern nursing thinks of health as a **dynamic process**: a person adapts to changes inside and outside the body and keeps function and meaning in life. **Halbert Dunn (1959)** called the top of this process **high-level wellness**: a person who uses all their abilities to move towards the best potential. The **Constitution of Nepal 2072 (Article 35)** gives every citizen the right to free basic health services, and the **Public Health Service Act 2075** turns that right into law.

Health has many sides: **physical, mental, social, spiritual, emotional and environmental**. A Nepali farmer with a healed leg but no field, no family support and no hope is not healthy by this wider view. Factors that decide health are called **determinants**: biology and genes, lifestyle (tobacco, alcohol, diet, exercise), physical environment (water, air, housing), income, education, social support and the health system itself. In Nepal, tobacco and alcohol, air pollution, unsafe water, poverty and long distance to a health post matter more than any single hospital machine.

**Levels of prevention (Leavell and Clark).** **Primary** prevention stops disease before it starts (immunisation, hand washing, iodised salt, health education). **Secondary** prevention finds disease early and treats it (BP screening, Pap smear, sputum test for TB). **Tertiary** prevention limits disability and helps rehabilitation (physiotherapy after stroke, foot care in diabetes). Every nursing action in the hospital or in the community belongs to one of these three levels.

## 1.2 Eight ways a nurse promotes and maintains health

Health promotion is *the process of enabling people to increase control over their health and improve it* (**Ottawa Charter, 1986**). The **Nepal Health Sector Strategy 2023-2030** and the Basic Health Service Package place promotion beside treatment. The eight points below are written for a ward, a health post and a community.

1. **Assess the person, not only the disease.** Ask about food, sleep, work, habits, family, beliefs and worries. A full picture shows risks that tests miss. The nurse uses this to plan care that fits the person's life.
2. **Teach in simple words.** Use Nepali or the client's mother tongue, pictures and demonstration. Check understanding with "teach-back": ask the client to explain it again. People change habits only when they understand why.
3. **Support healthy habits.** Help clients to stop tobacco, reduce alcohol, eat dal-bhat with vegetables, walk daily and sleep enough. Praise small steps. Change is slow, and shame makes it slower.
4. **Prevent disease through immunisation and screening.** Follow the National Immunisation Programme schedule and refer for BP, sugar, cervical and breast checks. Early action is cheaper and safer than late treatment. Record every contact in HMIS.
5. **Create a safe environment.** Promote safe water, toilets, clean cooking fuel, safe sharps and fall-proof homes. Environment is a silent determinant. FCHVs and ward committees can help the nurse here.
6. **Strengthen family and community.** Involve mothers' groups, FCHVs, schools and local government. A health message told by a neighbour is trusted more than one told by a stranger. Community action keeps the effect after the nurse leaves.
7. **Respect culture and belief.** Many families visit a *dhami*, *baidya* or temple before the health post. Do not mock them. Work with the belief, add the safe medicine, and ask the family to return.
8. **Reorient care towards prevention and rehabilitation.** Plan discharge, home care and follow-up from the first day. Link the client with a health post, a self-help group or a rehabilitation centre. Health is kept at home, not in the bed.

## 1.3 Illness: stages and the road back

**Illness** is the person's own experience of being unwell, while **disease** is the medical diagnosis. A person may have disease without feeling ill (early hypertension), and may feel ill without a found disease (stress). **Acute** illness is short and severe, **chronic** illness lasts months or years, and **terminal** illness ends in death. **Illness behaviour** is how a person reacts: some ignore symptoms until late (common among men in Nepal), others visit many healers.

**Suchman's five stages of illness** give a clinical path: (1) **symptom experience**: the person feels something is wrong; (2) **assumption of the sick role**: accepts the illness, seeks permission to rest and advice from family; (3) **medical care contact**: seeks a diagnosis and treatment; (4) **dependent-patient role**: gives control to the health team and follows treatment; (5) **recovery and rehabilitation**: gives up the sick role and returns to normal life. **Parsons' sick role** adds that a sick person is excused from normal duties and is not blamed for being ill, but must try to get well and co-operate with care.

**Convalescence** is the recovery period. It has three **tasks**: (a) **reassessment of life's meaning**: "Why did this happen and what matters now?"; (b) **reintegration of body image**: accepting a changed body such as a scar or an amputation; (c) **resolution of role changes**: a mother who cannot lift her child or a breadwinner who cannot work must adjust family roles. The nurse supports all three through listening, teaching, step-by-step independence and referral.

## 1.4 Stress and the General Adaptation Syndrome

**Stress** is the body's non-specific response to any demand made on it. A **stressor** is the demand: it may be physical (burns, cold), psychological (exam, fear), social (debt, separation) or developmental (puberty, ageing). **Hans Selye** (1936) described the **General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)**, a whole-body response in three stages (Figure 1.2): **alarm** (adrenaline, noradrenaline and cortisol rise; heart rate, BP and blood sugar go up), **resistance** (the body adapts; hormones stay high but symptoms ease) and **exhaustion** (adaptation fails; illness, collapse, even death). The **Local Adaptation Syndrome (LAS)** is the same idea in one organ: the inflammation, swelling and repair around a cut finger.

**Lazarus's transactional model** says stress is not in the event alone but in the person's *appraisal*: **primary appraisal** ("Is this a threat to me?") and **secondary appraisal** ("Can I cope?"). The same exam is a challenge for one student and a threat for another. **Coping** is the effort to manage the demand: **problem-focused** coping changes the situation (asks for help, makes a plan), while **emotion-focused** coping manages feelings (talks, prays, breathes slowly). Harmful coping includes smoking, alcohol and withdrawal. Signs of stress: fast pulse, sweating, headache, poor sleep, irritability, poor concentration, loss of appetite.

**Measuring life stress: the Holmes-Rahe scale (1967).** Each life event carries **life-change units (LCU)**: death of a spouse 100, divorce 73, marital separation 65, death of a close family member 63, personal injury or illness 53, change in financial state 38, change in responsibilities at work 29, change in residence 20, change in sleeping habits 16. A year total **below 150** carries a low (about 30%) chance of stress-related illness, **150-299** a moderate (about 50%) chance and **300 or more** a high (about 80%) chance. The scale is a screening aid, not a diagnosis; it was built on Western samples, so use it with judgement.

## 1.5 Crisis

A **crisis** is a **temporary state of upset and disorganisation** when a person meets a problem that cannot be solved with the usual coping skills. **Stress** is a common, daily demand that the person can usually manage, while **crisis** is an overload: the person's balance is lost, thinking is confused and ordinary methods fail. Stress can lead to crisis, but not every stress becomes a crisis. A crisis is **time-limited**: it usually lasts **four to six weeks** (Caplan) and ends either in growth or in damage.

**Types of crisis.** (1) **Maturational (developmental) crisis**: predictable life changes such as puberty, marriage, first childbirth, retirement. (2) **Situational crisis**: sudden outside events such as job loss, divorce, serious illness, a death or migration. (3) **Adventitious (social) crisis**: disaster not part of normal life and not predictable, such as earthquake, flood, landslide, fire, war or a bus accident; many people are affected together.

**Phases of crisis (Caplan).** **Phase 1:** anxiety rises as the stressor appears and the person uses usual coping. **Phase 2:** coping fails, anxiety rises further, thinking becomes disorganised and the person tries new methods. **Phase 3:** all methods tried; anxiety is severe, the person may withdraw, panic or accept help. **Phase 4:** the crisis becomes acute; if unresolved, it ends in **major disorganisation**: depression, violence or suicide. Aguilera describes the path in four steps (Figure 1.4).

**Eight nursing actions in crisis resolution.** Crisis intervention is short, focused and active, usually within six weeks.

1. **Make the person safe first.** Check for injury, suicidal thought, violence and basic needs such as food, warmth and sleep. Nothing else works if safety is missing. Stay with a person who is in danger.
2. **Assess quickly and clearly.** Ask what happened, how the person understands it, who is helping and what has been tried. Check the three balancing factors. Use a short checklist so that no key point is missed.
3. **Listen actively.** Sit near, keep eye contact that suits the culture, nod, and let the person tell the story and cry. Do not hurry to advise. Being heard is the first treatment.
4. **Stay calm and give simple, true information.** Speak slowly with short sentences, because a person in crisis cannot absorb much. Do not give false promises. Repeat important points.
5. **Mobilise support.** Call family, neighbours, FCHV, ward member, teacher or religious leader. Social support is the strongest protector. Link the person to a social worker, a helpline or a psychosocial counsellor.
6. **Help the person decide small steps.** Make a plan for today and for this week: who will cook, who will care for the children, who will go to the office. Small successes restore control. The person, not the nurse, makes the final choices.
7. **Teach coping skills.** Teach slow breathing, regular meals and sleep, light exercise, talking, prayer and writing. Warn against alcohol and sleeping tablets without advice. New skills help future crises.
8. **Follow up and refer.** Visit or call at fixed times, for example at 48 hours then weekly. Refer for suicide risk, severe depression, psychosis or panic that does not settle. Record every contact.

## 1.6 Loss, grief and mourning

**Loss** is the state of being without something one valued: a person, a limb, a job, a home, health. **Grief** is the inner emotional response to loss; **mourning** is the outward, cultural expression of grief; **bereavement** is the period after a death. **Elisabeth Kübler-Ross (1969)** described five common responses of people facing death: **denial** ("It cannot be true"), **anger** ("Why me?"), **bargaining** ("If I pray, will God give me more time?"), **depression** (sadness, withdrawal) and **acceptance** (peace). The stages are **not a fixed sequence**; people move back and forth and may skip stages. The same pattern is seen in family members. **Types of grief:** normal, anticipatory (before the loss), complicated or prolonged (more than 12 months with severe function loss), delayed and disenfranchised (not publicly acknowledged, such as grief after miscarriage).

**Mourning in Nepal.** Hindu families (the majority) usually cremate the body within a day at a ghat on a river, and the chief mourner, often the eldest son, observes **13 days** of mourning (*kriya*), with simple vegetarian food, white clothes and rites that end with the *terhau* ceremony. A widow may have restrictions, such as giving up coloured clothes and tika, which can add isolation. Buddhist communities hold prayers over 49 days, and Muslim and Christian families have their own rites. The nurse should know and respect these practices, allow time and privacy for the rites, and **never judge**.

**Factors that influence mourning:** (1) closeness of the relationship; (2) manner of death (sudden or violent deaths are harder); (3) age of the dead and of the mourner; (4) earlier losses; (5) personality and coping; (6) support from family and community; (7) culture and religion; (8) other stresses such as money or migration. After the 2015 earthquake many people lost home, income and family together, so multiple losses piled up.

**Nurse's role in grief.** Be present, let the family see and touch the body if they wish, allow rites, use the dead person's name, give simple explanations of what to expect (crying, poor sleep, guilt, seeing the person in dreams), and refer for suicidal thinking or grief that stops daily life.

## Formula and fact sheet

- Health (WHO 1948): physical, mental and social well-being; prevention is primary, secondary or tertiary; Suchman has 5 stages; convalescence has 3 tasks.
- GAS (Selye 1936): alarm (shock and counter-shock), resistance, exhaustion; LAS is local.
- Holmes-Rahe: below 150 low, 150-299 moderate, 300 or more high; separation 65, family death 63, spouse death 100.
- Crisis lasts about 4-6 weeks; three balancing factors; Kübler-Ross stages: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, acceptance; Hindu mourning 13 days in Nepal.

## Chapter summary

- Health is a dynamic state and not only absence of disease; nurses move clients along the wellness continuum by prevention and promotion.
- Illness passes through stages; convalescence has three tasks: meaning, body image and roles.
- Stress produces the GAS in three stages; the Holmes-Rahe score is a screening aid; appraisal and coping decide the outcome.
- A crisis is a short, overwhelming disruption; three balancing factors decide the outcome; the nurse gives safety, listening, support, coping skills and follow-up.
- Grief is natural and culture shapes mourning; nursing care respects rites and watches for complicated grief.

**Short answer (3 marks): Differentiate stress from crisis.** Stress is a normal demand that the person can manage with usual coping and may even be useful; crisis is an overload in which usual coping fails and the person is disorganised. Stress can last long; crisis is time-limited, usually 4-6 weeks. In stress, the balance is strained; in crisis it is lost. Crisis needs active intervention and is an opportunity for growth if resolved well.

**Medium answer (5 marks): Describe the General Adaptation Syndrome (Figure 1.2).** Hans Selye described the GAS as the body's non-specific response to any stressor. **Alarm:** the hypothalamus triggers the sympathetic system and the adrenal glands, so adrenaline, noradrenaline and cortisol rise; heart rate, BP, breathing and blood sugar rise; the first shock phase is followed by counter-shock. **Resistance:** the body adapts and resistance is above normal, symptoms ease but cortisol stays high; if the stressor ends the body recovers. **Exhaustion:** if the stress continues, the body's reserves are used up, resistance falls and illness such as ulcer, hypertension, infection, depression or death can follow. The local adaptation syndrome is the same response in one organ, as in inflammation. Nurses reduce stressors, teach coping and watch for exhaustion.

**Long answer (10 marks): Mrs Sita Rai, 34 years, from Sindhupalchok, is brought to the health post with her mother-in-law one week after her husband died in a construction accident in Malaysia. Calculate her Holmes-Rahe score and write the nursing care plan.**

Her crisis window is **42 days** (6 weeks), so the nurse plans **7 contacts** (48 h, then weekly). She sits quietly with Sita, accepts her denial without arguing, and gently repeats the facts ("Your husband has died; I am sorry") so that reality can be faced at her own pace. She arranges **support**: the FCHV, the ward member and the Foreign Employment Board contact for compensation and repatriation of the body and insurance. She teaches simple coping: three small meals, warm drink at night, slow breathing, talking with her sister. She plans follow-up, refers to the district psychosocial counsellor if the crisis continues beyond 6 weeks or if suicidal thought appears, and records every visit.
