## 3.1 What communication is

**Communication** is the exchange of information, thoughts and feelings between two or more people by words, writing, signs or behaviour. The nurse spends most of her day communicating: taking a history, teaching, giving handover, recording, comforting and advocating. The process has **six elements** (Figure 3.1): the **sender** (who has an idea), the **message** (what is said), the **channel** (voice, writing, touch, picture), the **receiver**, **feedback** (the reply that shows whether the message arrived) and the **context** (setting, culture, noise, emotion). The sender must **encode** the idea into words, and the receiver must **decode** them; at each step meaning can be lost.

## 3.2 Tools of communication

**Language** is the main tool. Nepal has **124 languages** (Census 2021) and Nepali is the mother tongue of about 45% of people, so in a ward the nurse often must use simple words, short sentences, a Nepali-Maithili-Tharu-Tamang word list, pictures and an interpreter (a family member is not always a good interpreter for private matters). Avoid medical jargon: say "sugar in the blood", not "hyperglycaemia".

**Observation** is looking with purpose: face colour, posture, gait, breathing, grooming, eye contact, skin and the way the person sits. **Perception** is how people interpret what they see and hear, which depends on past experience, culture and mood; two people can hear the same sentence differently, so the nurse **checks** her interpretation ("You look worried. Is that right?"). **Listening** is active, not passive. The mnemonic **SOLER** gives the posture of attention: **S**quarely face the person, **O**pen posture (no crossed arms), **L**ean slightly forward, **E**ye contact (as culturally suitable), **R**elaxed manner.

**Non-verbal behaviour** includes facial expression, gesture, posture, touch, distance (**personal space** about 0.5-1.2 m), tone, pace and silence. A popular statement says only 7% of a message is words, 38% tone and 55% body language (Mehrabian), but this applies only to **feelings and attitudes when words and body disagree**, not to all speech. When words and body disagree, believe the body. Touch must be culturally appropriate: in Nepal a gentle hand on a hand or shoulder is often welcome between same-sex people but may not be between a male nurse and a woman patient, and the head is never touched casually.

## 3.3 Eight barriers to communication

1. **Language and jargon.** The nurse and patient may not share a language, or the nurse uses words such as "NPO" and "catheter" that the patient does not know. The message is lost without any sign of confusion. Use plain words and an interpreter, and always confirm by teach-back.
2. **Hearing, vision and speech problems.** Older patients and those with stroke may not hear, see or speak clearly. Face the person, reduce noise, check hearing aids and glasses, write, draw or use signs. Never shout.
3. **Pain, fear and anxiety.** A person in pain or in panic cannot take in long explanations. Give short messages, stay calm, and repeat later. Treat the pain first.
4. **Noise and lack of privacy.** A crowded ward with a loud radio and no curtain makes patients silent about private matters. Use a curtain, a corner, a quiet room, and lower your voice. Privacy is a patient right.
5. **Culture, beliefs and gender.** Eye contact, touch, age and caste may carry meanings that the nurse does not know, and some women cannot speak openly with a male nurse. Learn the culture and ask. Never assume that silence means agreement.
6. **Attitudes and prejudice.** Judging a patient as "poor", "drunk" or "low caste" closes the conversation. Patients feel the attitude even when the words are polite. Practise respect and the principle of equal care.
7. **Nurse's workload and haste.** Standing at the door, looking at the chart or answering the phone tells the patient "I have no time". With a ratio of 1 nurse to 35 patients this barrier is real. Use short, focused, undivided time: even two minutes seated is effective.
8. **Wrong responses and poor feedback.** False reassurance, advice-giving, changing the subject and never asking "Did I explain clearly?" block the conversation. Learn the therapeutic replies in section 3.4. Always close the loop.

**Eight principles of effective communication (the "7 Cs" plus one).** Be **clear** (simple words), **concise** (short), **correct** (true and accurate), **complete** (all needed information), **courteous** (respectful), **concrete** (specific, with examples), **considerate** (suit the person's age, culture and emotion) and **culturally** careful, with feedback at the end. Add: choose the right time and place, greet by name with "namaste" and the proper title (*didi*, *dai*, *buwa*, *aama*), explain every procedure before doing it, and document.

## 3.4 The nurse-patient relationship

A **nurse-patient relationship** is a professional, purposeful and time-limited relationship focused on the **patient's needs**. A **therapeutic relationship** has five features: **trust, respect, empathy, genuineness and boundaries**. It follows **Peplau's four phases** (Figure 3.2). **Empathy** is understanding the patient's feelings and showing it ("This sounds very frightening for you"), while **sympathy** is feeling sorry for the person and is less helpful. **Boundaries** keep the relationship professional: no gifts, no private contact, no secrets, no favours; confidentiality is kept except where safety requires sharing.

**Therapeutic techniques:** **active listening**, **open questions** ("How are you feeling today?"), **reflection** (repeating feeling), **clarification** ("Do you mean the pain comes at night?"), **silence**, **offering self** ("I will stay with you for ten minutes"), **summarising** and **giving information**. **Anti-therapeutic** (blocking) responses include **false reassurance** ("Don't worry, everything will be fine"), **giving advice** ("You should..."), **asking "why"** (sounds like blame), **changing the subject**, **judging** ("That was foolish"), **defending** the hospital or colleagues, **stereotyped replies** ("Everyone feels this way") and **minimising**. Replace "Don't worry" with "I can see you are worried. Tell me more."

## 3.5 Handover and documentation: SBAR

Poor handover is a leading cause of errors. **SBAR** is a short structure used for telephone calls, shift handover and referrals (Figure 3.4): **S**ituation (who and what is wrong now), **B**ackground (history, drugs, allergies, what happened), **A**ssessment (vital signs, what you think the problem is) and **R**ecommendation (what you need and by when). Record in the chart in clear, time-ordered, objective, signed, legible notes; write what was seen and done, never opinions such as "patient is difficult", and never erase (a single line and signature correct a mistake).

## 3.6 Physical examination

**Physical examination** is the systematic collection of objective data from the body, done after the health history and followed by vital signs and nursing diagnosis. **Preparation:** wash hands, explain, get consent, ensure **privacy** and a warm, well-lit room, empty the bladder, position the patient, expose only the part examined, collect equipment (thermometer, BP cuff, stethoscope, torch, tongue depressor, reflex hammer, tape measure, otoscope, ophthalmoscope, weighing scale) and keep a chaperone for intimate examinations.

**The four techniques, always in this order, except the abdomen:**

1. **Inspection:** look at colour, size, shape, symmetry, movement and skin with good light; compare sides.
2. **Palpation:** touch with warm hands: light palpation (1 cm) first for tenderness and surface, then deep palpation (4-5 cm) for organs and masses; use the fingertips for texture, the palm for vibration and the back of the hand for temperature; examine painful areas last.
3. **Percussion:** tap a finger laid on the surface and listen: **resonance** (normal lung), **hyper-resonance** (emphysema, pneumothorax), **tympany** (air-filled gut, gastric bubble), **dullness** (liver, heart, fluid or consolidation) and **flatness** (thigh muscle, bone).
4. **Auscultation:** listen with the **diaphragm** for high-pitched sounds (lungs, S1 and S2, bowel sounds) and the **bell** for low-pitched sounds (S3, murmurs); use a quiet room and a stethoscope placed directly on skin.

**Abdomen:** **inspect, auscultate, percuss, then palpate**, because palpation can change bowel sounds. **General sequence (head to toe):** general survey and vital signs; skin, hair and nails; head, face, eyes, ears, nose, mouth and throat; neck and thyroid; chest and lungs; heart and pulses; breasts; abdomen; musculoskeletal; neurological (GCS, reflexes, sensation). Always compare right and left and record in clear words.

## 3.7 Eight nursing responsibilities during examination

1. **Prepare the patient and the place.** Explain, take consent, and ensure privacy with a curtain and a warm room. A patient who is cold and exposed is tense and the findings are less reliable. Offer to empty the bladder first.
2. **Prepare equipment and yourself.** Check that all instruments work and are clean, wash hands, warm the stethoscope and your hands. Cleaning between patients prevents cross-infection. Keep a chaperone for intimate areas.
3. **Use the right order and technique.** Follow inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation (abdomen: auscultation before palpation). Touch painful areas last. Correct technique gives reliable data.
4. **Compare sides and use landmarks.** Examine both sides, the normal side first. Record findings with landmarks such as "right upper quadrant" and with measurements. This makes later comparison possible.
5. **Observe the patient's face and comfort.** Watch for grimace, guarding and fear during palpation and ask "Does this hurt?". Stop when pain is severe. Respect modesty and culture.
6. **Preserve dignity and respect gender and culture.** A female patient should be examined with a female nurse or chaperone present where possible. Cover the body, speak calmly, and never comment on the body. Explain each step before touching.
7. **Record findings objectively.** Write exactly what you found and measured, with the time and your signature; avoid words such as "normal" without detail. Report abnormal findings, such as BP above 180/110 or SpO2 below 90%, at once. Objective records protect the patient and the nurse.
8. **Teach and follow up.** Tell the patient what was found in simple words, answer questions and explain the next step. Teach home measures such as BP and weight monitoring. Plan re-examination at a set time.

## Formula and fact sheet

- Communication: sender, message, channel, receiver, feedback, context; SOLER; Peplau 4 phases; SBAR; teach-back.
- Percussion notes: resonance (lung), hyper-resonance (emphysema), tympany (gut), dullness (liver, fluid), flatness (muscle, bone).
- MAP = (SBP + 2 × DBP) ÷ 3; pulse pressure = SBP − DBP; pulse deficit = apical − radial; BMI = kg ÷ m²; waist-hip ratio = waist ÷ hip; respiratory rate = breaths in 30 s × 2.
- Asia-Pacific BMI: 23-24.9 overweight, 25 or more obese; WHR above 0.90 (men) or 0.85 (women) is high.

## Chapter summary

- Communication is a loop; the nurse closes it with feedback (teach-back) and removes barriers of language, noise, pain and attitude.
- The therapeutic relationship has trust, respect, empathy, genuineness and boundaries, and passes through four phases; anti-therapeutic replies block it.
- SBAR gives a safe, structured handover and call.
- Physical examination uses inspection, palpation, percussion and auscultation in order (abdomen: inspect, auscultate, percuss, palpate) with privacy and objective records.
- Simple calculations (MAP, pulse pressure, BMI, WHR, respiratory rate) turn observations into clinical data.

::: note Answers and rationales
**1. (a).** (120 + 2 × 80) ÷ 3 = 93.3; 100 uses a simple average; 40 is the pulse pressure.
**2. (b).** 14 × 2 = 28 breaths a minute, which is fast; 14 is only a half-minute count.
**3. (b).** 60 ÷ 2.25 = 26.7; 20.0 forgets to square the height.
**4. (b).** Auscultation comes before percussion and palpation in the abdomen because touch can change bowel sounds.
**5. (c).** A nod is not understanding; teach-back and clear channels confirm it. Louder speech does not help a language gap.
:::

**Medium answer (5 marks): Describe the phases of the nurse-patient relationship (Figure 3.2).** Peplau described four phases. **Orientation:** the nurse introduces herself, builds trust and helps the patient to name the problem. **Identification:** the patient responds to those who can help, states needs and agrees a plan. **Exploitation:** the patient uses the services offered, learns, takes part in care and sets goals. **Resolution:** needs are met, the relationship is ended and the patient becomes independent.
