## 6.1 Rehabilitation: meaning, goals and process

**Rehabilitation** is the process of helping a person with disability to reach and keep the best possible **physical, sensory, intellectual, psychological and social function**, and to return to a meaningful life (WHO). **Disability** is the umbrella term for impairment, activity limitation and participation restriction (WHO ICF); the Nepal **Act Relating to the Rights of Persons with Disabilities 2074 (2017)** defines it as long-term physical, mental, intellectual or sensory impairment that, with barriers, prevents equal participation. **Goals:** prevent further disability, restore function, teach compensation (use of the other hand, an aid), promote independence and dignity, support return to family, school or work, and change the community's attitude. **Types:** medical, vocational, social and educational rehabilitation. The **team** includes the doctor, nurse, physiotherapist, occupational therapist, speech therapist, prosthetist, psychologist, social worker, and above all the patient and family.

The **process** is a cycle (Figure 6.1): **assess** abilities and needs (Barthel Index, mobility, speech, mood), **set goals** with the patient (SMART), **plan and treat** (exercise, aids, teaching), **evaluate** and **discharge with a follow-up plan**. The nurse is on the ward 24 hours a day and so is the **co-ordinator** who practises therapy tasks with the patient and links hospital and home.

## 6.2 Eight nursing approaches in rehabilitation

The syllabus asks for four problem areas: mobility, communication, elimination and body image. The eight points below cover them with the general principles.

1. **Prevent complications first.** Turn every 2 hours, use pressure-relieving mattresses, position joints in the functional position, and do passive range-of-motion exercises. Contractures, pressure injuries and deep-vein thrombosis begin in the first days. Prevention is cheaper than treatment.
2. **Mobility: teach safe movement.** Teach bed mobility, sitting balance, transfers and walking with the correct aid. A **cane** is held on the stronger side with its top at the **greater trochanter** and the elbow bent 20-30 degrees; **crutches** are **the person's height minus 40 cm** with the hand-grip at wrist level when the arm hangs; a walker suits poor balance. Use a gait belt and stay on the weak side.
3. **Communication: use every channel.** For **aphasia** speak in short, simple sentences, ask yes/no questions, use gestures, pictures and writing, give time without finishing sentences, reduce noise and never talk about the patient as if absent. For **hearing loss** face the person; for **visual loss** announce yourself and describe the place. Work with the speech therapist.
4. **Elimination: set up a routine.** For neurogenic bladder use timed voiding or intermittent catheterisation, adequate fluids and prompt toileting; for the bowel use a regular time after a meal, fibre, fluids and a suppository if ordered. Keep skin clean and dry. Avoid indwelling catheters when possible to prevent infection.
5. **Body image: allow grief, then rebuild.** Loss of a limb or function causes shock, denial, anger and slowly acceptance. Let the person look at and touch the changed part, listen, use positive words, introduce a peer who has coped (for example in the Spinal Injury Rehabilitation Centre or a self-help group), and encourage grooming and clothes. Refer for severe depression.
6. **Self-care and ADLs.** Promote independence in feeding, washing, dressing and toileting using adaptive tools such as a built-up spoon, long-handled sponge and Velcro clothing. Let the patient do all that he can, even if it is slow. Praise every gain. Use a Barthel chart to track progress.
7. **Family and caregiver training.** Teach the family safe lifting, positioning, skin checks, exercises and warning signs, and watch for caregiver stress. Rehabilitation continues at home for months. Give written and picture instructions.
8. **Discharge and community follow-up.** Assess the home (steps, bathroom, floor), plan aids (walking stick, ramp, commode), arrange follow-up, link with the local health post, ward office, disability organisation and CBR worker, and explain rights and allowances. A good discharge plan prevents readmission.

**Community responsibility in Nepal.** The **2021 Census** counted **654,782** people with disability among **29,164,578** residents; the proportion is calculated below (other surveys, using the Washington Group method, give a higher figure of about 6.7%, so the true number is probably larger). The **Constitution of Nepal** forbids discrimination on disability grounds (Article 18) and grants free education (Article 31); the **2074 Act** gives rights to health, education, employment, accessibility and a **disability identity card** in four classes: **A (total, red), B (severe, blue), C (moderate, yellow) and D (mild, white)**; allowances are paid to classes A and B. **Community-based rehabilitation (CBR)**, local governments and organisations of persons with disabilities share the duty to make roads, buildings, schools and services accessible.

## 6.3 Ethics and law

**Ethics** is the study of right and wrong conduct; **nursing ethics** are the principles that guide a nurse's choices. **Law** is the set of rules made by the State and enforced by courts. **Differences:** ethics are moral standards (what *ought* to be done), set by the profession, with penalties such as reprimand or loss of registration; law is a minimum standard of what *must* be done, set by Parliament, with penalties such as fine, prison or compensation. An act can be legal but unethical (telling a family member a patient's diagnosis without consent where no law forbids it) or ethical but illegal. **Ethical principles:** **autonomy** (the patient decides), **beneficence** (do good), **non-maleficence** (do no harm), **justice** (fairness), **fidelity** (keep promises), **veracity** (tell the truth), **confidentiality** and **accountability**. The **ICN Code of Ethics (2021)** has four elements (nurses and people, practice, profession, global health); the **Nepal Nursing Council** issues the *Code of Ethics and Professional Code of Conduct for Registered Nurses and Midwives* (2019). **Nightingale Pledge** is a promise to keep the profession pure, protect patient confidences and avoid harm.

**Legal terms.** **Consent** is permission to touch or treat. **Informed consent** needs five elements: **capacity** (understanding), **information** (purpose, benefits, risks, alternatives), **voluntariness**, **understanding** (check by teach-back) and **decision**, recorded in writing. **Tort** is a civil wrong; **liability** is legal responsibility for harm; **negligence** is failure to give the care that a reasonable nurse would give, with four elements all proved (Figure 6.2); **malpractice** is professional negligence; **assault** is the threat of unwanted touch and **battery** is the touch itself (treating without consent); **defamation** is harm to reputation by false statements; **suit** is a case filed in court. **Vicarious liability** makes the employer responsible for staff acts, but the nurse stays personally responsible.

**Legal control of nursing practice in Nepal.** The **Nepal Nursing Council Act 2052 (1996)** created the **Nepal Nursing Council (NNC)** as an autonomous body to set standards, approve nursing programmes, register nurses and ANMs, hold the **National Licensure Examination for Nurses (NLEN)** and discipline misconduct; no unregistered person may practise as a nurse. Graduates pass the NLEN (pass mark 50%), register, and **renew** periodically; the NNC has introduced a **continuing professional development (CPD)** rule of **60 credit hours in six years with at least 10 in every year** from Magh 1, 2082 (check the current notice because validity periods were reported differently in different sources). Under the **Muluki Criminal Code 2074 (Chapter 19)** it is an offence to treat patients without a licence (**section 230**: up to 3 years' prison and a fine up to Rs 30,000, rising to life imprisonment if the death results), to treat maliciously (**231**) or to treat recklessly or negligently (**232**); the **Public Health Service Act 2075**, the **Consumer Protection Act 2075** and the **Right to Safe Motherhood and Reproductive Health Act 2075** also protect patients. The right to health is in the Constitution (Article 35).

**Eight legal and ethical responsibilities of the nurse.**

1. **Hold a valid registration and work within scope.** Keep the NNC licence current and complete CPD. Do not perform procedures you are not trained or authorised to do. Practising without a licence is an offence.
2. **Obtain informed consent.** Make sure the patient has capacity, has understood the procedure and agrees freely. Witness the doctor's consent and never force or trick a patient. For children and incapacitated adults obtain consent from the guardian.
3. **Maintain confidentiality.** Share information only with the care team and with consent, never in corridors, on social media or by phone to unknown callers. Exceptions are legal orders and serious risk of harm. Keep records secure.
4. **Follow the five rights and verify orders.** Check patient, drug, dose, route and time; question unclear orders. Do not give an order that you believe is unsafe. Report errors at once; honest reporting protects patients.
5. **Document carefully.** Write timely, accurate, signed and objective notes, and never alter, erase or backdate. Records are the main legal evidence. If it was not recorded, it is hard to prove it was done.
6. **Protect patient rights and dignity.** Respect privacy, equal care regardless of caste, gender, religion or disability, the right to refuse treatment and the right to complain. Act as advocate for the vulnerable.
7. **Act on unsafe practice and keep standards.** Report unsafe colleagues or equipment through the right channel and refuse unsafe assignments in writing. Maintain competence through learning. Know the hospital's policies.
8. **Respond in emergencies and keep insurance.** Give first aid within your competence, call for help and document. Consider professional indemnity cover and join the Nepal Nurses Association. Seek legal advice when a case is filed.

::: note Answers and rationales
**1. (b).** 180 − 40 = 140 cm; 120 cm would be too short and 220 cm is absurdly long.
**2. (d).** Negligence needs duty, breach, causation and damage; intention to harm is a different crime, not an element.
**3. (b).** The NNC Act 2052 (1996) created the Council; the other laws regulate other matters.
**4. (b).** Short sentences, gestures and time respect dignity and reduce frustration; shouting and finishing sentences do not help.
**5. (b).** Capacity, information, voluntariness and understanding are required; blank forms and relative signatures for capable adults are invalid.
:::

**Short answer (3 marks): State the four elements of negligence.** The four elements are duty of care owed by the nurse to the patient, breach of that duty by failing to meet the standard of a reasonable nurse, causation (the breach caused the harm) and damage (actual injury or loss). All four must be proved. Example: a double dose of insulin given without checking, causing brain injury.

**Medium answer (5 marks): Describe the nursing care of a client with a mobility problem in rehabilitation.** Prevent complications by turning 2-hourly, using pressure-relieving mattresses, correct limb positions and range-of-motion exercises. Assess strength and balance, teach bed mobility and transfers, and train in the correct use of a cane (top at the greater trochanter, held on the stronger side), crutches (height minus 40 cm) or walker with a gait belt. Provide an anti-slip environment, encourage active participation and praise progress, and use the Barthel Index to track gains. Teach the family safe handling, plan home modification (ramp, commode) and arrange follow-up with the physiotherapist and community rehabilitation worker.

**Long answer (10 marks): Mr Bhim Bahadur Gurung, 55 years, from Lamjung, has right hemiplegia and expressive aphasia 3 weeks after a stroke. His Barthel Index was 35 at admission and 65 at four weeks. He is incontinent of urine, sad about being "useless", and the family asks about going home. Calculate and write the rehabilitation care plan.**

*Calculations.* The Barthel gain is **30 points** (86% above baseline), from **severe** to **moderate** dependence; he needs **26 more points** to reach the 91 mark of slight dependence. Turning every 2 hours means **12 turns a day** while he is bed-bound.

*Care.* The nurse prevents complications (2-hourly turning, positioning of the right arm on a pillow, range-of-motion exercises, skin checks, deep breathing). She practises the physiotherapist's exercises with him daily, teaches transfers to the stronger left side, and begins walking with a quad cane. For aphasia she uses short sentences, yes/no questions, picture cards and time, and works with the speech therapist. She starts timed voiding every 2-3 hours and reviews fluids, to avoid a catheter. She acknowledges his grief, involves him in decisions and links him with a stroke survivor. She teaches his wife safe lifting, skin care, exercises and warning signs (new weakness, speech change), plans home changes (ramp, a raised toilet seat, a handrail) and arranges follow-up at the health post and a rehabilitation centre. She also explains his rights under the Act 2074 (identity card, allowance if class A or B) and obtains informed consent for each plan, keeps his information confidential and records every session.
