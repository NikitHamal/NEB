## 1.1 What is sociology?

**Sociology** (Latin *socius*, companion; Greek *logos*, study) studies how people live together in groups, how groups shape individuals and how individuals change groups. The word was coined by **Auguste Comte** in 1838. The **nature** of sociology: it is a **social science** (it uses evidence, not opinion), **empirical** (based on observation, surveys and records), **theoretical** (it builds explanations), **general** (it looks for patterns, not single cases) and **objective** (it tries to put personal bias aside). Its **scope** covers social institutions (family, religion, economy, education, health), social processes (socialization, cooperation, conflict, change), social structure (groups, classes, castes) and social problems.

**Sociology and its neighbours.** **Anthropology** studies human culture, usually through long fieldwork in one community. **Psychology** studies the mind and behaviour of the individual. **Social work** applies sociological knowledge to help people and communities. **Sociology** sits between them: it studies the group and the structure around the individual.

**Eight reasons sociology matters in nursing.**

1. **It explains the patient as a social person.** A patient is a daughter, a farmer, a Dalit, a migrant's wife, not only a diagnosis. Her family and community decide when she seeks care and who pays. The nurse who sees this plans realistic care.
2. **It shows how society shapes health.** Poverty, education, caste, gender and place of birth change who falls ill and who recovers. In Nepal, women in the poorest households are far less likely to have a skilled birth attendant. Knowing this guides outreach.
3. **It improves communication.** Words, gestures and silence carry meaning from one's culture. Touching an elder's feet, avoiding eye contact or addressing a woman through her husband are messages. A nurse who reads them builds trust.
4. **It builds cultural competence.** Nepal has 142 caste and ethnic groups and over 120 languages. Food rules, birth customs and death rites differ by group. Respectful care starts with asking, not assuming.
5. **It helps in health education.** People change behaviour when the change fits their roles, beliefs and group norms. A message that ignores the mother-in-law will fail. Sociology tells us to teach the decision-makers too.
6. **It reveals barriers to care.** Distance, cost, stigma, language, fear of hospitals and gender rules keep people away. Examples: a woman who needs her husband's permission to travel, or a person with leprosy who hides. Once seen, barriers can be reduced.
7. **It supports patient rights and ethics.** Equal care for every caste, religion and gender is required by the Constitution of Nepal (2015) and the NNC Code of Ethics. Sociology explains why discrimination happens and how to stop it.
8. **It prepares nurses for leadership and community work.** Ward teams, committees and health facility operation and management committees are small societies. Understanding groups, roles and power helps a nurse lead, resolve conflict and mobilise Female Community Health Volunteers (FCHVs).

- **Auguste Comte (France):** coined "sociology" (1838); called it the queen of sciences; proposed the **law of three stages** (theological, metaphysical, positive); divided the field into **social statics** (order) and **social dynamics** (change).
- **Emile Durkheim (France):** social facts exist outside the individual and constrain him; studied **suicide** (1897) and showed that rates differ by group (egoistic, altruistic, anomic, fatalistic); described **mechanical** and **organic solidarity** and the idea of **anomie** (normlessness).
- **Karl Marx (Germany):** history is shaped by **class struggle** between the owners of production (bourgeoisie) and workers (proletariat); material conditions shape ideas; **alienation** of the worker.
- **Max Weber (Germany):** insisted on **verstehen** (interpretive understanding of meaning), described **class, status and power**, ideal-type **bureaucracy** (rules, hierarchy, records, which hospitals still follow) and the **Protestant ethic** and capitalism.

## 1.3 Three perspectives, one hospital

**Functionalism** sees society as a living body whose parts (family, school, health system) work together to keep it stable; each part has a **function** (manifest, intended; latent, unintended). **Conflict theory** sees society as a field of competition for scarce resources between groups (rich and poor, castes, genders), where order is kept by power and inequality is the root of many problems. **Symbolic interactionism** (Mead, Blumer, Goffman) studies face-to-face meaning: people act according to the **symbols** and labels they give to things, and the self is built in interaction (a label such as "TB patient" changes how others treat the person).

## 1.4 Culture, norms, values, status and role

**Culture** is the learned, shared way of life of a group: its knowledge, beliefs, values, customs, language, art, food and tools (Tylor, 1871). **Material culture** is what can be seen and touched (dress, tools, houses); **non-material culture** is ideas and norms. The features of culture: it is **learned** (not inherited), **shared**, **cumulative**, **dynamic** (changes with time), **symbolic** (language) and **integrated** (parts fit). A **subculture** is a group within a culture with its own pattern (the nursing profession, the Newar community, adolescents). **Cultural lag** (Ogburn) is the delay of non-material culture behind material change: mobile phones spread faster than attitudes to women using them. **Ethnocentrism** judges other cultures by one's own; **cultural relativism** understands each culture on its own terms; nurses practise relativism but never accept harm.

**Norms** are rules of behaviour. **Folkways** are everyday customs (greeting with *namaste*; removing shoes before entering a home), breaking them brings mild disapproval. **Mores** are strong norms with moral weight (respect for the dead, incest taboo), breaking them brings strong disapproval. **Laws** are norms enforced by the state (the Criminal Code 2074 bans caste discrimination and *chhaupadi*). **Values** are shared ideas of what is good (hospitality, respect for elders, non-violence). **Sanctions** are rewards or punishments that enforce norms.

**Status** is a position in society and **role** is the behaviour expected from it. **Ascribed status** is given at birth (sex, caste, age); **achieved status** is earned (nurse, graduate). **Role strain** is stress inside one role (a ward nurse with too many patients); **role conflict** is a clash between two roles (a nurse who is also a mother on night duty); **role set** is the group of roles linked to one status. The **sick role** (Parsons, 1951) gives the patient four rights and duties: exemption from normal duties, no blame for illness, a duty to want to get well, and a duty to seek and follow medical help.

## 1.5 Nepal's social picture in numbers

The 2021 National Population and Housing Census counted **29,164,578** people in Nepal, in **142 caste and ethnic groups** speaking **124 mother tongues** (as reported by the National Statistics Office; some secondary sources differ by a few groups because of grouping). Hindus are **81.19%**, Buddhists **8.21%**, Muslims **5.09%**, Kirat **3.17%** and Christians **1.76%**. The largest caste and ethnic groups are Chhetri 16.45%, Hill Brahmin 11.29%, Magar 6.90%, Tharu 6.20% and Tamang 5.62%. A **diversity index** (Simpson) is 1 minus the sum of squared shares: 0 means everyone belongs to one group, values near 1 mean many equal groups.

## 1.6 Socialization: becoming a member of society

**Socialization** is the lifelong process by which a person learns the language, norms, values and roles of a society and develops a self. **Primary socialization** happens in early childhood in the family; **secondary socialization** continues in school, peer group, workplace, religion and media; **anticipatory socialization** is rehearsal for a future role (a nursing student learning to behave as a nurse); **resocialization** is learning a new identity after a big change (a soldier, a prisoner, a patient on long dialysis). **Cooley's looking-glass self** says we see ourselves as others see us; **Mead's theory** says the self grows through play and imitation (the "I", spontaneous, and the "me", the social image); **Freud** described id, ego and superego; **Piaget** and **Erikson** described stages of thinking and of identity.

The **agents of socialization** in Nepal: the **family** (the first teacher of food, language and gender roles); the **peer group** (smoking and alcohol are often learnt here); the **school** (hand washing, discipline, citizenship); **religion** (festivals, fasting, purity rules); **mass media and mobile phones** (health messages and misinformation); and the **workplace** and **health institution** (the hidden curriculum of a hospital teaches a new nurse how to speak to patients). When socialization is poor, deviance and poor health behaviour follow; when it is good, norms such as hand washing become habits.

## 1.7 Culture and health in Nepal: eight points

1. **Beliefs about the cause of illness.** Many families first think of **fate, evil spirits, the evil eye or a god's anger**, and call a **dhami or jhankri** (traditional healer) before the health post. This is a cultural model of illness, not stupidity. The nurse should ask, "What do you think caused this?" and then explain the biomedical cause without ridicule, and may allow harmless rituals.
2. **Menstruation and chhaupadi.** In parts of Karnali and Sudurpashchim a menstruating or postpartum woman is sent to a hut or shed. The hut is cold, smoky and unsafe: deaths from cold, smoke inhalation, snakebite and assault are reported. **Chhaupadi has been a crime since August 2018 (Criminal Code 2074, section 168: up to three months' imprisonment and/or a fine of Rs 3,000)**, but the practice persists in places. Nurses educate families and report cases with the ward and the local government.
3. **Pregnancy, birth and the postnatal period.** Families may restrict food (the "hot and cold" idea), keep a mother in a separate room for several days, delay colostrum, or prefer home birth. Some practices help, such as warm *jwano* soup and rest; others harm, such as delayed breastfeeding, cord care with ash, and delayed referral. Respect the first kind and negotiate the second kind.
4. **Food rules and fasting.** Hindus, Buddhists, Muslims and Kirat have different food rules; fasting at Teej, Dashain, Ekadashi or Ramadan changes insulin, tablet and meal timing. A patient may refuse beef, pork or eggs. Ask about dietary rules at admission, and plan diabetic doses around fasts with the doctor.
5. **Caste, purity and untouchability.** The Constitution (2015) and the Caste-Based Discrimination and Untouchability (Offence and Punishment) Act 2068 (2011) prohibit discrimination, yet Dalit patients sometimes report being made to sit apart or being treated last. The nurse must give the same care to all, and never ask caste as a condition of treatment.
6. **Gender roles and decision-making.** In many homes the husband or mother-in-law decides whether a woman goes to a facility, how much is spent and when. A woman may not speak openly in front of her husband. Offer a private examination room and include decision-makers in teaching.
7. **Death and mourning.** Hindus cremate and observe 13 days of mourning (kriya); Buddhists chant and observe rites; Muslims bury and wash the body; Kirat bury. Families want time with the body, and specific people to handle it. Nurses follow the family's wishes within the hospital's policy and guide next of kin kindly.
8. **Language and literacy.** About 124 mother tongues mean a patient may not understand Nepali well, and about one in four adults cannot read. Use simple words, an interpreter (an FCHV or relative, with consent), drawings and the "teach-back" method. A signature or thumbprint is valid consent only when the patient understood.

## 1.8 Culturally competent nursing: the LEARN model

**Cultural competence** is the ability to give safe, respectful, effective care to people of different cultures. Its elements are **cultural awareness** (of my own bias), **knowledge**, **skill** (assessment, communication), **encounters** and **desire**. **Transcultural nursing** (Madeleine Leininger) and the **Purnell model** are the theories behind it. The **LEARN** model (Berlin and Fowkes) gives a bedside method, shown in Figure 1.5.

**Eight nursing responsibilities in culturally sensitive care.**

1. **Assess culture at admission.** Ask about language, religion, diet, healers consulted, who makes decisions and any rituals needed. A short checklist in the file is enough. This prevents conflict later.
2. **Examine your own bias.** Every nurse has views about caste, gender and religion. Awareness stops them from reaching the patient. Supervisors and peer discussion help.
3. **Communicate respectfully.** Greet with *namaste*, use the right title (*didi*, *bhai*, *ama*, *buwa*), speak slowly and avoid slang. Use teach-back. Respect silence and shyness.
4. **Protect privacy and modesty.** Provide screens, a female nurse for a female patient when possible and gowns that cover. Many patients delay care because of embarrassment. Respect is care.
5. **Include the family and healers.** Family support is a major strength in Nepal. Invite the decision-maker to teaching and, where safe, allow a harmless ritual. This builds trust and compliance.
6. **Adapt diet and routine.** Offer vegetarian and halal options, plan insulin around fasts and keep prayer times in mind. Small adaptations improve intake and satisfaction.
7. **Challenge harmful practices gently.** Use facts, stories and local leaders (FCHVs, female teachers, religious leaders). Never mock. Report crimes (chhaupadi, violence) according to the law.
8. **Document and advocate.** Record the cultural needs and what was agreed, and speak for patients who are poor, lower caste or silent. The NNC Code of Ethics requires equal care without discrimination.

**Short answer (3 marks): Define culture and list its features.** Culture is the learned and shared way of life of a group, including knowledge, beliefs, values, customs, language, art and tools (Tylor). Its features are that it is learned, shared, cumulative, dynamic, symbolic and integrated. It has material parts (dress, tools) and non-material parts (norms, values). A nurse studies it because it shapes health beliefs, food rules and care-seeking.

**Medium answer (5 marks): Describe the three main sociological perspectives and give a nursing application of each.** **Functionalism** (Durkheim, Parsons) sees society as a system of interdependent parts that keep stability; in nursing it explains teamwork, the referral chain and the sick role (Figure 1.2). **Conflict theory** (Marx, Weber) sees society as competition for scarce resources between classes, castes and genders; it explains why poor, remote and Dalit patients reach care late, and it supports advocacy and equity. **Symbolic interactionism** (Mead, Blumer, Goffman) studies meanings and labels made in daily interaction; it explains stigma (TB, leprosy, HIV, mental illness) and is the base of therapeutic communication. Functionalism and conflict theory are macro views, whereas symbolic interactionism is a micro view. For example, to reduce delayed referral, a nurse can repair the ambulance link (functionalism), work with the municipality to fund transport for poor families (conflict) and speak with the family to change the meaning of the hospital from "place of death" to "place of cure" (interactionism). A good nurse uses all three lenses together.

**Long answer (10 marks): Mrs Sunita Tharu, 24 years, from Banke, G2P2, is on postnatal day 1 after a normal birth in a district hospital. Her mother-in-law wants her to stay in a separate dark room for 11 days and says the first milk is "dirty", so the baby has not been breastfed. Birth weight 3,000 g; on day 3 the weight is 2,850 g. Explain the cultural factors and write a nursing care plan.**

*Cultural analysis.* The mother-in-law holds authority in the household (role and status), and her belief is a **mores-level norm** passed through socialization: the postnatal mother is "impure" and colostrum is wrong. Sociologically this is **cultural relativism**: the practice makes sense inside her world, but it can harm. The nurse uses the **LEARN** model (Figure 1.5) and the **conflict** and **interactionist** views: Sunita has low power to decide, and the meaning of colostrum must be changed. The baby lost **(3,000 - 2,850) / 3,000 = 5.0%** of birth weight, which is within the usual limit of about 7-10% in the first week, so there is no emergency, but delay of three days at 8 feeds a day would lose **24 feeds**, and feeds should come every **3 hours** (8-12 times in 24 hours). Early, exclusive breastfeeding with colostrum is the baby's first vaccine and lowers neonatal infection and death; the Nepal postnatal protocol asks for visits at 24 hours, day 3, day 7 and 6 weeks.
