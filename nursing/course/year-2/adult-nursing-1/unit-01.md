Adult nursing is the care of people from about 18 years onward who have a medical or surgical illness, in the ward, the theatre, the clinic and the home. The wards of Bir, TUTH, Patan, BPKIHS and the provincial hospitals are full of adults with chronic disease: **non-communicable diseases (NCDs) cause about 71% of all deaths in Nepal** (WHO estimate), and NDHS 2022 found that **20% of adults aged 15 and over have raised blood pressure**. Many of these patients need an operation at some point. This unit teaches the **perioperative continuum**: the care before, during and after surgery, with pain, fluid balance and wound healing. All arithmetic is checked by program.

## 1.1 Adult nursing in the Nepal health system

Adults come to the hospital through the outpatient department (OPD), the emergency room or by referral from a health post. At the health post the **Package of Essential NCD interventions (PEN)** of the Ministry of Health and Population (MoHP) lets a health worker measure blood pressure, glucose and BMI, start simple treatment and refer. The **Nepal Nursing Council (NNC)** licenses the nurse and its Code of Ethics (NNC Act 2052) requires consent, privacy and honest records. In every ward the nurse uses the **nursing process** (assess, diagnose, plan, implement, evaluate) and writes the care in **NANDA-I** language, as in Year 1.

The three levels of prevention frame adult care. **Primary prevention** stops disease (tobacco and alcohol control, salt reduction, exercise, vaccination). **Secondary prevention** finds disease early (BP and glucose screening, Pap smear, sputum test). **Tertiary prevention** limits disability (rehabilitation, stoma care, home care after stroke). A good adult nurse works at all three levels each day: while changing a dressing she also teaches the patient to stop smoking.

## 1.2 The perioperative continuum

Surgery is described in three phases (Figure 1.1). The **pre-operative phase** begins when the decision to operate is made and ends when the patient reaches the theatre. The **intra-operative phase** lasts from entry to the theatre until transfer to the recovery room. The **post-operative phase** runs from recovery until the patient is healed and follows up. Surgery is also classified by urgency: **emergency** (immediate, life-threatening, such as perforation), **urgent** (within 24-48 hours), **elective** (planned, such as hernia repair) and **optional**. By purpose it can be diagnostic (biopsy), curative (appendicectomy), palliative (bypass of a blocked bowel), reconstructive or cosmetic.

### Pre-operative nursing responsibilities: eight points

1. **Assess the whole patient.** Take the history of allergies, drugs, smoking, alcohol, past anaesthetics and chronic disease, and check weight, vital signs, Hb, blood sugar and renal tests. Poorly controlled diabetes, hypertension or anaemia raise the risk of surgery. Report any abnormal result before the operation list, not at the theatre door.
2. **Know the ASA grade.** The anaesthetist gives an **ASA physical status** class: I healthy; II mild systemic disease (controlled hypertension); III severe disease limiting activity (poorly controlled diabetes); IV disease that is a constant threat to life; V moribund; VI brain-dead donor. The nurse uses the grade to plan monitoring and the ward to which the patient returns. Higher grade means closer watching.
3. **Informed consent.** The surgeon explains the operation, benefits, risks and alternatives; the nurse confirms that the patient understood and signed willingly, with a witness. For a minor or an unconscious patient the guardian signs. Consent given under pressure or without understanding is not valid and the nurse must report this.
4. **Teach and reduce fear.** Show deep breathing, coughing while splinting the wound with a pillow, leg exercises and turning in bed. Explain tubes, drains and the pain plan in Nepali. A patient who knows what to expect has less fear, less pain and fewer chest and clot complications.
5. **Fasting.** Standard guidance is **6 hours for solids and 2 hours for clear fluids** (up to 4 hours before for breast milk or a light meal depending on local protocol). Fasting prevents aspiration of stomach contents during anaesthesia. Diabetic patients need an insulin plan and frequent glucose checks.
6. **Prepare the skin and bowel.** Ask the patient to bathe the evening before, remove hair only if needed and use clippers, not razors, which cause micro-cuts and infection. Give a bowel preparation only if ordered. Remove jewellery, dentures, nail polish and contact lenses.
7. **Give pre-medication and keep records.** Give the ordered antibiotic prophylaxis (usually within 60 minutes before the incision), sedatives or anti-reflux drugs exactly on time. Check name band, blood group and cross-match, and place the chart, X-rays and consent in the file. Document the last oral intake.
8. **Safe handover to theatre.** Use the pre-operative checklist: identity, site marking, consent, allergies, fasting, implants, valuables, last void and vital signs. The nurse accompanies the patient and gives a clear verbal handover. A missed item at this point is the root of wrong-site and wrong-patient errors.

## 1.3 The operating theatre and the WHO Surgical Safety Checklist

The theatre is divided into zones: **unrestricted** (entry, changing), **semi-restricted** (corridors, scrub area) and **restricted** (the operating room itself) with positive-pressure ventilation, a mask and cap, and limited traffic. The **scrub nurse** keeps the sterile field, hands the instruments and counts; the **circulating nurse** is outside the field, opens the packs, manages the records and the checklist. Instruments are sorted by use: cutting (scalpel, scissors), grasping (forceps, clamps), retracting (retractors), suturing (needle holders) and suction. **Sterilisation** is by steam autoclave (121 °C for 15-20 minutes at 15 psi, or 134 °C for 3-4 minutes), with a chemical indicator in each pack.

The **WHO Surgical Safety Checklist** has three pauses. **Sign in** (before anaesthesia): identity, site, consent, allergy, airway risk, blood loss risk. **Time out** (before skin incision): everyone introduces themselves, the team confirms patient, procedure and site aloud, antibiotic prophylaxis given, and critical events are anticipated. **Sign out** (before the patient leaves): the nurse confirms the procedure recorded, **instrument, swab and needle counts correct**, specimens labelled, equipment problems noted and the key recovery concerns. Nepal's hospitals have adopted the checklist, and the nurse who calls "time out" and will not allow the incision until every item is confirmed prevents wrong-site surgery and retained swabs.

## 1.4 Post-operative care, fluids and the eight complications

On return from theatre the patient is first watched in the recovery room, where the nurse uses the **Aldrete score** (activity, respiration, circulation, consciousness and oxygen saturation, each scored 0-2): a total of **9 or more** allows return to the ward. On the ward the order of care is **ABC** (airway, breathing, circulation), then vital signs (every 15 minutes at first, then 30-minute, hourly, 4-hourly), pain, nausea, the wound and drains, intravenous fluid and urine output. A track-and-trigger tool such as **NEWS2** gives a total from the six physiological signs and consciousness: **7 or more is an emergency call**.

A 60-kg adult needs about **1,800 mL of maintenance fluid a day (30 mL/kg), which is 75 mL/h**, and must pass at least **30 mL of urine an hour**; less than this (oliguria) signals hypovolaemia or kidney injury. One litre in 8 hours through a macro-drip set (20 gtt/mL) runs at **42 gtt/min**. For a patient of 60 kg (blood volume 4,200 mL) whose haematocrit falls from 40 to 30, the **allowable blood loss is 1,050 mL**. The sample patient scores **NEWS2 7** (emergency), and a recovery score of **9** passes. A 5 mg morphine order from a 10 mg/mL ampoule is **0.5 mL**.

### Eight post-operative complications: what the nurse watches and does

1. **Airway obstruction and hypoxia.** The tongue falls back or secretions block the airway in a patient not fully awake. Look for noisy breathing, falling SpO2 and restlessness. Position on the side or sit up, suction gently, give oxygen and call the anaesthetist.
2. **Haemorrhage and shock.** Bleeding may be visible (soaked dressing, drain) or hidden (abdominal swelling). Early signs are a fast pulse, cold clammy skin, low urine and then low blood pressure. Raise the legs, run IV fluid, send blood, inform the surgeon and keep the patient nil by mouth.
3. **Pain and post-operative nausea and vomiting.** Unrelieved pain raises heart rate, stops deep breathing and delays walking. Assess with a scale, give analgesia before the pain peaks and use non-drug aids such as position and splinting. Give anti-emetics for nausea and watch for aspiration.
4. **Atelectasis and pneumonia.** Pain, anaesthesia and bed rest collapse the small airways, and fever with cough appears on days 1-3. Use the incentive spirometer ten times an hour while awake, teach deep breathing, coughing with a pillow, sit up and walk early. Stop smoking before surgery lowers the risk.
5. **Urinary retention and infection.** Anaesthesia and pain can stop the bladder emptying; a full bladder is felt as lower-abdominal pain. Check for voiding within 6-8 hours, use privacy and a sitting position, and catheterise if ordered. Remove a urinary catheter as early as possible to avoid infection.
6. **Deep-vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism.** Blood pools in the calf veins when the patient lies still, causing a swollen, warm, painful leg and, if the clot travels, sudden breathlessness and chest pain. Use anti-embolism stockings, calf exercises, early walking, fluids and ordered heparin. Report sudden breathlessness as an emergency.
7. **Surgical site infection (SSI) and dehiscence.** Redness, heat, pus, swelling, fever and rising pain at the wound on days 3-7 suggest infection; a gush of pink fluid suggests the wound is opening. Keep the dressing dry, use strict hand hygiene and aseptic technique, send a swab, and give antibiotics. If wound edges split, cover with moist saline gauze and call the surgeon.
8. **Paralytic ileus and delirium.** The bowel may stop moving after abdominal surgery, giving distension, vomiting and no flatus. Keep fasting, pass a nasogastric tube if ordered, record fluids and correct potassium. In older adults watch for sudden confusion and treat the cause: pain, infection, hypoxia, retention or drugs.

## 1.5 Pain, drains and wound healing

**Pain assessment** uses the **numeric rating scale (NRS 0-10)**: 1-3 mild, 4-6 moderate, 7-10 severe. For children and non-verbal adults use faces or **FLACC**. The **WHO analgesic ladder** has three steps: **step 1** non-opioids (paracetamol, NSAIDs); **step 2** weak opioids (tramadol, codeine) with a non-opioid; **step 3** strong opioids (morphine) with a non-opioid. Give drugs by the clock, by the mouth if possible, and always give laxatives with opioids. Check the respiratory rate and sedation before each opioid dose; hold the dose if the rate is below 10 a minute and call the doctor.

**Wound healing** passes through four overlapping phases (Figure 1.4): haemostasis (minutes), inflammation (days 1-4), proliferation (days 4-21, collagen and granulation tissue) and remodelling (21 days to 2 years). **Primary intention** means clean edges joined by sutures or staples; **secondary intention** is an open wound filling from the base with granulation tissue (a pressure ulcer, an infected wound); **tertiary intention** is delayed closure. Healing is delayed by diabetes, smoking, malnutrition, steroids, infection, poor blood supply and pressure. Protein, vitamin C and zinc help; tell the patient to eat dal, egg, fish, green leaves and citrus fruit.

## Formula and fact sheet

- Maintenance fluid 30-35 mL/kg/day; minimum urine 0.5 mL/kg/h; drip rate (gtt/min) = volume (mL) × drop factor ÷ time (min); macro set 15-20 gtt/mL, micro set 60 gtt/mL.
- Dose volume = desired ÷ have × vehicle; allowable blood loss = blood volume × (Hi - Hf) ÷ Hi, with 70 mL/kg for an adult.
- Fasting 6 h solids, 2 h clear fluids; prophylactic antibiotic within 60 minutes before the incision; NEWS2 of 7 or more is an emergency; Aldrete 9 or more to leave recovery.
- WHO ladder: non-opioid, weak opioid, strong opioid; NRS 1-3 mild, 4-6 moderate, 7-10 severe.

## Chapter summary

- The perioperative continuum has three phases and the nurse works in each: prepares, protects and watches.
- The ASA grade, consent, fasting, safe handover and the WHO checklist prevent most serious surgical errors.
- Post-operative care follows ABC, vital signs, pain, fluids, urine, wound and drains; the five Ws guide the search for complications.
- Healing has four phases, is helped by protein and vitamin C and delayed by smoking, diabetes and infection.

**3. (c).** 0.5 × 80 = 40 mL/h; below this the kidneys are poorly perfused.

**Short answer (3 marks): The WHO Surgical Safety Checklist.** It is a 19-item tool in three pauses. **Medium answer (5 marks): Describe the nursing care after major abdominal surgery (Figures 1.1 and 1.3).** On return keep the airway open and give oxygen, record vital signs at 15-minute then longer intervals and use NEWS2. Control pain by the NRS and the WHO ladder, give anti-emetics, and keep the patient comfortable with the head up. Maintain IV fluid at about 30 mL/kg/day and record intake and urine (target at least 0.5 mL/kg/h). Check the dressing and the drain volume and colour; empty the drain bulb and record. Prevent chest complications with the incentive spirometer and coughing against a pillow, and prevent DVT with stockings, leg exercises and early walking. Start oral fluids as the surgeon orders, keep the wound clean and dry, and teach the patient the danger signs before discharge.
