The final unit covers the money that circulates in the economy, the institutions that create and control it, the price changes it can cause (inflation), and trade with other countries, with attention to Nepal.

::: key What this unit gives you
- Concept and functions of money, the value of money, money supply (M1, M2).
- Inflation: types, causes, effects and control.
- Commercial banks and the central bank (Nepal Rastra Bank).
- Internal versus international trade; balance of trade and balance of payments.
:::

## 7.1 Concept and functions of money

**Money** is anything generally accepted as a means of payment and settlement of debts. It solves the **double coincidence of wants** problem of barter. **Functions:** *primary*: **medium of exchange** and **unit of account** (a common measure of value); *secondary*: **store of value** and **standard of deferred payment**; *contingent*: transfer of value, liquidity. **Forms:** commodity money, **fiat money** (legal tender with value by government decree: banknotes), bank (deposit) money, and digital payments. **Qualities:** acceptability, durability, portability, divisibility, uniformity, scarcity. Nepal's rupee is a fiat currency, **pegged to the Indian rupee** (NPR 1.60 = INR 1 since 1993).

### Value of money

The **value (purchasing power) of money is the inverse of the price level**: value = 1/P. If the **consumer price index (CPI)** rises from 100 to 125, one rupee buys 100/125 = **0.8** of what it did. The **quantity theory** says MV = PT (money supply × velocity = price level × output): with V and output stable, prices rise in proportion to money supply.

### Money supply

The **money supply** is the total money held by the public. Nepal Rastra Bank defines, in the usual pattern: **M1** (narrow money) = currency in the hands of the public + demand (current) deposits; **M2** (broad money) = M1 + savings and fixed (time) deposits of banks and financial institutions (and foreign-currency deposits, depending on the definition). Banks **create deposits** through lending: with a reserve ratio r, the **money multiplier = 1/r**.

::: example Worked example: deposit creation
Initial deposit NPR 1,000; required reserve ratio 10%. The bank lends 900, which is deposited elsewhere and 810 is lent again, and so on. Maximum total deposits = 1,000 × (1/0.10) = **10,000**, so **9,000** of new deposit money is created. With a 20% ratio the multiplier is 5 and total deposits 5,000 (4,000 created). A **higher reserve ratio restricts** money creation, which is how a central bank tightens credit.
:::

## 7.2 Inflation

**Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level (equivalently, a fall in the value of money), measured by the **percentage change in a price index** such as the CPI: inflation = (CPI₁ − CPI₀)/CPI₀ × 100.

::: example Worked example
A basket costs NPR 5,000 in the base year and 5,900 now: **CPI = 5,900/5,000 × 100 = 118**. If last year's CPI was 112, inflation = (118 − 112)/112 = **5.36%**.
:::

**Types:** by speed: **creeping** (< 3%), **walking/trotting** (3–10%), **galloping/running** (10–100%+), **hyperinflation** (extreme, like Zimbabwe 2008); by cause: **demand-pull**, **cost-push**, **structural**.

**Causes:** **demand-pull** (too much money chasing too few goods: money supply growth, government deficit spending, rising exports or remittances raising spending, easy credit); **cost-push** (higher wages, fuel and raw materials, imported inflation, taxes, supply disruptions, monopoly pricing); **structural** (bottlenecks, poor infrastructure and supply chains); expectations feed back (wage–price spiral). In Nepal, prices are strongly influenced by India's prices through the open border and fixed exchange rate and by fuel imports.

**Effects:** **debtors gain and creditors lose** (loans repaid with cheaper money); **fixed-income earners** (pensioners, salaried workers) lose purchasing power; savers lose if interest is below inflation (negative real interest); uncertainty reduces investment; exports lose competitiveness; income is redistributed toward those with real assets (land, gold). Mild inflation (2–5%) is usually considered consistent with growth.

**Control:** **monetary measures** (raise policy interest rates, raise reserve requirements, sell government securities, credit ceilings); **fiscal measures** (cut public spending, raise taxes, budget surplus); **supply-side** measures (increase production, improve supply chains, import essentials, buffer stocks) and price and wage controls (temporary).

## 7.3 Banking: commercial banks and the central bank

**Commercial banks** accept deposits and lend. Functions: **primary**: accepting deposits (current, savings, fixed, recurring), granting loans and advances (overdraft, term loans, discounting bills); **secondary/agency**: remittance and money transfer, issuing cheques, cards and letters of credit, collecting bills, utility payments, locker and advisory services, foreign-exchange dealing, and digital banking; **credit creation** (7.1). Banks earn from the spread between lending and deposit rates and from fees; they hold reserves and keep a balance between **liquidity, safety and profitability**.

**Central bank (Nepal Rastra Bank, NRB)**, established in 1956 (2013 BS; governed now by the Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058), is the apex monetary authority. **Functions:**

- **Issues currency** (sole right to issue notes).
- **Banker, agent and adviser to the government** (keeps its accounts, manages public debt).
- **Banker to banks and lender of last resort**; holds banks' reserves, settles interbank payments.
- **Formulates and implements monetary policy** to control money supply, interest rates, inflation and the exchange rate.
- **Regulates and supervises** banks and financial institutions (licensing, capital adequacy, directives).
- **Manages foreign-exchange reserves** and the exchange rate regime.

**Instruments of credit control:** *quantitative*: **cash reserve ratio (CRR)**, **statutory liquidity ratio (SLR)**, **bank rate**, **repo and reverse repo/standing liquidity facility** (policy rate corridor), **open market operations** (buy/sell securities); *qualitative*: moral suasion, credit rationing, margin requirements, directives on sector lending limits. Tight policy (higher rates and CRR) lowers inflation but slows growth; loose policy does the reverse.

## 7.4 International trade, balance of trade and balance of payments

**Internal (domestic) trade** is between regions of one country; **international trade** between countries. Differences: **factor immobility** across borders (labour and capital move less freely), **different currencies** and exchange-rate risk, **different laws, tariffs, quotas and regulations**, differing languages and customs, and more transport, documentation and political risk. **Theory:** countries gain from trade by **comparative advantage**: specialise in goods with the lowest opportunity cost and trade (Ricardo). Barriers: tariffs, quotas, subsidies, non-tariff barriers; Nepal is a **landlocked** country, depending on transit through India, which raises trade costs; the largest trading partner is India, and imports greatly exceed exports.

**Balance of trade (BoT)** = value of **merchandise (visible) exports − imports**. A **deficit** means imports exceed exports. **Balance of payments (BoP)** is the systematic record of **all** economic transactions between residents and the rest of the world in a period:

| Account | Includes |
|---|---|
| **Current account** | trade in goods (BoT), trade in services (tourism, transport, IT exports), primary income (wages, investment income), **secondary income (remittances, grants)** |
| **Capital account** | capital transfers, debt forgiveness, acquisition of non-produced assets |
| **Financial account** | foreign direct investment, portfolio investment, loans, changes in **reserve assets** |

Because it is a double-entry record the BoP **always balances in total** (with errors and omissions): a current-account deficit is financed by a surplus in the capital and financial accounts or by drawing down reserves. A **deficit/surplus** refers to a particular part (usually the current account or the overall change in reserves).

::: example Worked example
Merchandise exports 100 and imports 450 (units of NPR billion): **BoT = −350** (deficit). Net services and income +10; remittances and other transfers +300. **Current account** = −350 + 10 + 300 = **−40**. Capital and financial account = +30 (loans and investment inflows). **Overall balance** = −40 + 30 = **−10**, financed by a fall in foreign exchange reserves of 10. This pattern, a large trade deficit offset by remittance inflows, is typical of Nepal.
:::

## 7.6 Gains from trade, tariffs, the terms of trade and the quantity theory of money

Section 7.4 stated the principle of comparative advantage and the balance of payments. This section works the numbers, shows what a tariff does to the market of section 1.7, and gives the standard equation linking money and prices.

### Comparative advantage with numbers

Compare Nepal and India producing two goods, **ginger** and **handicrafts**. Each country has 120 labour hours. The table gives the **hours needed per unit**.

| | Ginger (hours per unit) | Handicraft (hours per unit) | Opportunity cost of 1 ginger | Opportunity cost of 1 handicraft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Nepal | 2 | 3 | 2/3 handicraft | 1.5 ginger |
| India | 1 | 2 | 1/2 handicraft | 2 ginger |

India is better at **both** goods (absolute advantage), but trade still pays because the **opportunity costs differ**. Nepal gives up less ginger to make a handicraft (1.5 against 2), so Nepal has the **comparative advantage in handicrafts**; India gives up less to make ginger (0.5 against 0.667), so it has it in ginger.

::: example Worked example: gains from trade
**Without trade**, each country splits its 120 hours equally. Nepal: 60 h gives 30 ginger and 20 handicrafts. India: 60 h gives 60 ginger and 30 handicrafts.
**With specialisation**, Nepal makes only handicrafts: 120/3 = **40**. India makes only ginger: 120/1 = **120**.
They trade at **1 handicraft = 1.75 ginger**, a rate between the two opportunity costs (1.5 and 2), so both sides gain. Nepal sells 20 handicrafts and receives 35 ginger.
**Nepal ends with** 20 handicrafts and 35 ginger, against 20 and 30 before: **5 more ginger** with no loss. **India ends with** 85 ginger and 20 handicrafts, against 60 and 30 before: it has 25 more ginger and 10 fewer handicrafts, and 10 handicrafts are worth 20 ginger at its own opportunity cost, so India is better off by **5 ginger equivalents**.
World output rises from 90 ginger + 50 handicrafts to 120 ginger + 40 handicrafts. At the trading rate (1 handicraft = 1.75 ginger) that is worth 90 + 87.5 = 177.5 ginger before and 120 + 70 = 190 ginger after: the world package is larger.
:::

The gains disappear if the trading rate lies **outside** the range of the two opportunity costs, because one country would then do better at home. Real trade has transport costs, tariffs and quotas, and specialisation has risks (Nepal's trade with a single dominant partner exposes it to that partner's shocks); the theory explains why trade is useful, not that every deal is.

### The terms of trade

The **terms of trade** show how many imports one unit of exports buys: **terms of trade index = (export price index / import price index) × 100**. If export prices are 120 and import prices 150 (base year 100), the index is 120/150 × 100 = **80**: each unit of exports now buys only 80 % of the imports it bought in the base year. The terms of trade have **deteriorated**. A country exporting primary products and importing fuel and machines often sees this.

### A tariff on imports

A **tariff** is a tax on imports. Take a small country whose domestic demand is **Qd = 120 − P** and domestic supply **Qs = P − 20**, with a world price of **40**.

::: example Worked example: a tariff of 10
**Free trade at P = 40:** Qd = 80, Qs = 20, so imports = **60**.
**Tariff of 10 raises the domestic price to 50:** Qd = 70, Qs = 30, so imports = **40**.
**Consumers lose** the area under demand between P = 40 and 50: 10 × 70 + ½ × 10 × 10 = **750**.
**Producers gain**: 10 × 20 + ½ × 10 × 10 = **250**. **Government collects** 10 × 40 = **400**.
**Net loss** = 750 − 250 − 400 = **100**, two triangles of 50: **½ × 10 × (30 − 20)** from producing units that could be bought cheaper abroad, and **½ × 10 × (80 − 70)** from consumers who now buy less.
:::

Arguments for tariffs include protecting an **infant industry** until it can compete, raising revenue (an important source of government income in Nepal) and national security. The arguments against are the deadweight loss above, higher prices for users of imported inputs and the risk of **retaliation**. **Dumping** means selling abroad below the home price or below cost; a country may answer it with an **anti-dumping duty**, but the claim must be proved. A **quota** limits quantity instead of price; unlike a tariff its price gain goes to whoever holds the import licences.

### The quantity theory of money

The **equation of exchange** is **MV = PY**: money supply M times its **velocity** V (how many times a unit of money is spent in a year) equals the price level P times real output Y. If V and Y are roughly constant, **P is proportional to M**.

::: example Worked example: money and prices
M = 1,500, V = 4, Y = 2,000 gives P = 1,500 × 4/2,000 = **3.0**. If M rises by 10 % to 1,650 with V and Y unchanged, **P = 3.3**: prices also rise 10 %. If output grows 5 % at the same time, P = 6,600/2,100 = 3.14, a rise of only 4.8 %.
:::

Real life is less tidy: V changes with interest rates and payment habits (digital wallets raise velocity), and output responds in the short run. The theory is best used for the **long-run** link between money growth and inflation, as in section 7.2.

**Fisher equation.** The **real interest rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation**. A deposit paying 9 % while prices rise 6 % earns a real rate of about **3 %** (exactly 1.09/1.06 − 1 = 2.83 %). When inflation exceeds the deposit rate, the real return is negative.

## 7.7 Eight roles of money, banks and trade in everyday Nepali life

1. **Money makes exchange simple.** Without money a farmer who wants a phone must find a phone seller who wants rice. A common medium of exchange removes this double coincidence of wants. Notes, cards and QR payments such as Fonepay all do the same job.
2. **Money is a store of value and a unit of account.** Savings kept as money can be spent later, but they lose value when prices rise. Every price in a Nepali shop is quoted in rupees, so goods can be compared quickly. Inflation reduces this value, which is why real assets are sometimes preferred.
3. **Banks turn savings into loans.** A bank lends most of its deposits, and the loan is deposited again, so deposits multiply. This lets a small saving finance a farm loan, a shop or a house. The reserve ratio limits how far the process can go.
4. **Inflation changes who gains and who loses.** Borrowers repay in cheaper money and gain, while pensioners and salaried people lose buying power. Prices of fuel, rice and vegetables matter most to poor families. This is why governments watch the consumer price index every month.
5. **The central bank guards stability.** The Nepal Rastra Bank issues notes, supervises banks and sets policy rates to keep prices and the financial system stable. When it raises rates, loans become costlier and spending cools. It also acts as the lender of last resort in a crisis.
6. **Digital banking cuts transaction costs.** ConnectIPS, eSewa and mobile banking let people send money without travelling to a branch. A migrant worker's family in Gorkha can receive money in minutes. Lower costs help small firms and bring more people into the formal system.
7. **Trade lets Nepal specialise.** By the principle of comparative advantage, a country gains by making what it produces at lowest opportunity cost and importing the rest. Nepal exports items such as cardamom, tea and handicrafts, and imports fuel, machinery and vehicles. The gains come through lower prices and wider choice.
8. **The balance of payments shows dependence on remittance.** A big trade deficit is paid for by money sent home by workers abroad, as in the worked example. This keeps reserves safe but ties the economy to foreign labour markets. Reading the BoP tells whether this pattern can last.

## Formula and fact sheet

- Money: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value, standard of deferred payment.
- Value of money = 1/price level. Quantity theory MV = PT. Money multiplier = 1/reserve ratio.
- M1 = currency + demand deposits; M2 = M1 + time/savings deposits (NRB definition varies slightly).
- Inflation = (CPI₁ − CPI₀)/CPI₀ × 100; demand-pull, cost-push, structural.
- NRB tools: CRR, SLR, bank rate, repo/SLF, open market operations.
- BoT = exports − imports (goods). BoP = current + capital + financial account (= 0 overall).
- Terms of trade = (export price index / import price index) × 100; tariff loss = two triangles; MV = PY; real rate ≈ nominal − inflation.

## Chapter summary

- Money serves four functions; its value falls as the price level rises; the money supply (M1, M2) is created by banks via the multiplier and controlled by the central bank.
- Inflation, caused by demand, costs or structure, redistributes income and is fought with monetary, fiscal and supply measures.
- Commercial banks take deposits and lend; the NRB issues currency, regulates banks and conducts monetary policy.
- International trade differs from domestic trade by frictions and currencies; BoT covers goods, while the BoP records all transactions and balances in total.

## Model answers: medium (5 marks) and long (10 marks) questions

**Q1 (5 marks). Define money and explain its functions. How do banks create deposits? Show with an example of NPR 1,000 and a 10% reserve ratio.**

*Introduction.* **Money** is anything generally accepted as a means of payment and for settling debts. It removes the **double coincidence of wants** of barter. Forms include commodity money, fiat notes, bank deposits and digital payments.

*Functions.* (1) **Medium of exchange:** buyers and sellers accept it for any good. (2) **Unit of account:** all prices are quoted in rupees, so values can be compared. (3) **Store of value:** purchasing power can be saved for later. (4) **Standard of deferred payment:** loans and wages are fixed in money.

*Deposit creation (Figure 7.1).* Banks keep a fraction r of deposits as reserve and lend the rest, and the loan returns as a new deposit. With an initial deposit of 1,000 and r = 10%, the bank lends 900, then 810, then 729, and so on. The **money multiplier** is 1/r = 10, so total deposits can reach 1,000 × 10 = **10,000**, of which **9,000** is newly created. With r = 20% the multiplier is 5 and total deposits are 5,000.

*Conclusion.* A higher reserve ratio limits credit creation; this is the lever the central bank uses to tighten or loosen money.

**Q2 (5 marks). Distinguish between balance of trade and balance of payments. From the data, calculate the current account and the overall balance.**

*Introduction.* International transactions are recorded in two ways: a narrow account for goods and a wide account for everything.

| Point | Balance of trade | Balance of payments |
|---|---|---|
| Covers | visible goods only | goods, services, income, transfers, capital and finance |
| Formula | exports − imports | current + capital + financial account |
| Balances? | rarely | always, as a double-entry record |
| Nepal | large deficit | cushioned by remittance |

*Structure.* The **current account** has trade in goods, services, primary income and secondary income (remittances and grants). The **capital account** has capital transfers. The **financial account** has foreign investment, loans and changes in reserves.

*Calculation (Figure 7.4), NPR billion.* BoT = 100 − 450 = **−350**. Current account = −350 + 10 + 300 = **−40**. Capital and financial account = +30. Overall balance = −40 + 30 = **−10**, financed by a fall of 10 in foreign-exchange reserves.

*Conclusion.* A large trade deficit offset by remittances is typical of Nepal; the BoP shows how it is financed.

**Q3 (10 marks). Define inflation. Explain its types, causes, effects and the measures to control it. A basket costs NPR 5,000 in the base year and 5,900 now; last year's CPI was 112.**

*Introduction.* **Inflation** is a sustained rise in the general price level, equal to a fall in the value of money. It is measured by the percentage change in a price index such as the consumer price index (CPI).

*Measurement.* CPI = 5,900/5,000 × 100 = **118**. Inflation = (118 − 112)/112 × 100 = **5.36%**. One rupee now buys 100/118 = 0.85 of what it bought in the base year.

*Types.* By speed: **creeping** (below 3%), **walking** (3 to 10%), **galloping** (10% and more) and **hyperinflation**. By cause: demand-pull, cost-push and structural.

*Causes (Figure 7.2).*

1. **Demand-pull:** too much money chasing too few goods, through money growth, deficit spending, rising remittance spending and easy credit. AD shifts right.
2. **Cost-push:** higher wages, fuel and raw material prices, imported inflation, taxes, monopoly pricing. AS shifts up and output falls (stagflation).
3. **Structural:** poor roads, weak supply chains and bottlenecks. In Nepal prices also follow India's through the open border and the peg.

*Effects.* Debtors gain and creditors lose; pensioners and salaried people lose real income; savers lose if the interest rate is below inflation; uncertainty reduces investment; exports become less competitive; real assets such as land and gold gain.

*Control.*

| Measure | Examples | Works against |
|---|---|---|
| Monetary | higher policy rate, higher CRR, selling securities | demand-pull |
| Fiscal | lower spending, higher taxes, surplus budget | demand-pull |
| Supply-side | more production, better supply chain, buffer stocks | cost-push, structural |
| Controls | temporary price and wage limits | short-term relief |

*Conclusion.* Mild inflation of 2 to 5% is consistent with growth; the right cure depends on whether demand or supply is the cause.

**Q4 (10 marks). Explain the functions of the Nepal Rastra Bank and the instruments it uses to control credit.**

*Introduction.* The **Nepal Rastra Bank (NRB)** is Nepal's central bank. It was established in 1956 (2013 BS) and now works under the Nepal Rastra Bank Act, 2058. It is the apex monetary authority and, unlike a commercial bank, it does not work for profit or deal with the public.

*Functions.*

1. **Issuer of currency:** it holds the sole right to issue notes.
2. **Banker, agent and adviser to the government:** it keeps government accounts and manages public debt.
3. **Banker to banks:** it holds their reserves and settles payments between them.
4. **Lender of last resort:** it lends to a sound bank in a liquidity crisis, which protects depositors.
5. **Monetary policy:** it controls the money supply, interest rates, inflation and the exchange rate.
6. **Regulation and supervision:** it licenses banks and sets capital adequacy and lending directives.
7. **Reserve manager:** it holds and manages foreign-exchange reserves and the rate regime.

*Instruments of credit control (Figure 7.3).*

| Type | Instrument | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Quantitative | **Cash reserve ratio (CRR)** | a higher ratio leaves banks less to lend |
| Quantitative | **Statutory liquidity ratio (SLR)** | banks must hold a share in liquid assets |
| Quantitative | **Bank rate, repo and standing liquidity facility** | a higher rate makes borrowing from NRB dearer |
| Quantitative | **Open market operations** | selling securities drains money; buying adds it |
| Qualitative | Moral suasion, credit rationing, margin rules, sector limits | direct guidance of lending |

*Effect.* A tight policy (higher rates and CRR) cuts borrowing and spending and lowers inflation, but it slows growth and jobs. A loose policy does the opposite. With a reserve ratio change from 10% to 20% the multiplier halves from 10 to 5, which shows how strong a lever the CRR is.

*Conclusion.* The NRB keeps prices, the banking system and the external position stable, and trades off inflation against growth in choosing its instruments.
