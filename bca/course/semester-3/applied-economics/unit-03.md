Why does the demand curve slope downward? **Consumer theory** explains it from the way a consumer with a limited income allocates spending to get the greatest satisfaction. Two approaches exist: the older **cardinal** utility analysis and the modern **ordinal** indifference-curve analysis.

::: key What this unit gives you
- Cardinal utility: total and marginal utility, diminishing marginal utility, consumer's equilibrium, derivation of demand.
- Ordinal analysis: indifference curves, MRS, budget line, equilibrium.
- Price, income and substitution effects; PCC and ICC; the Hicksian demand curve, with numbers.
:::

## 3.1 Cardinal utility analysis

**Utility** is the want-satisfying power of a good. The cardinal approach (Marshall) assumes utility can be measured in units ("utils"). **Total utility (TU)** is the total satisfaction from all units consumed; **marginal utility (MU)** is the extra utility from one more unit: MUₙ = TUₙ − TUₙ₋₁. **Law of diminishing marginal utility:** as a consumer takes more units of a good, MU falls; TU rises at a decreasing rate, reaches a maximum when MU = 0, and then falls (MU negative).

| Units of apples | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TU | 10 | 18 | 24 | 28 | 30 |
| MU | 10 | 8 | 6 | 4 | 2 |

**Assumptions:** utility is measurable in cardinal numbers; the consumer is rational and wants to maximise utility; the marginal utility of money is constant; MU diminishes; utilities of goods are independent; income and prices are given; consumption is in standard units.

### Consumer's equilibrium

With one good, the consumer buys until **MU of the good = price** (in units of the MU of money). With two or more goods, the **equi-marginal principle**: spend so that the **marginal utility per rupee is equal across goods**: **MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y = … = MU of money**, with all income spent.

::: example Worked example: two goods
Income NPR 20; P_x = 4, P_y = 2. MU of X for units 1–4: 16, 12, 8, 4 (MU/P: 4, 3, 2, 1). MU of Y for units 1–5: 10, 8, 6, 4, 2 (MU/P: 5, 4, 3, 2, 1).
Buy in descending order of MU per rupee within the budget: Y₁ (5, cost 2), Y₂ (4, 2), X₁ (4, 4), Y₃ (3, 2), X₂ (3, 4), Y₄ (2, 2), X₃ (2, 4): total cost 2 + 2 + 4 + 2 + 4 + 2 + 4 = **20**, so the budget is exhausted. Equilibrium: **3 units of X and 4 of Y**; MU_x/P_x = 8/4 = 2 = MU_y/P_y = 4/2 ✓. Total utility = (16 + 12 + 8) + (10 + 8 + 6 + 4) = 36 + 28 = **64**. Any other affordable bundle gives less.
:::

**Derivation of the demand curve (cardinal):** with one good and the MU of money fixed, the consumer buys where MU = P. If the price falls from 8 to 6, he buys more units until MU falls to 6, because MU diminishes: price and quantity are negatively related, which is the demand curve. **Criticisms:** utility is not truly measurable; the constant MU of money is unrealistic for big price changes; it cannot separate the income and substitution effects, and it neglects inferior and Giffen goods.

## 3.2 Ordinal utility: indifference curves

The ordinal approach (Hicks and Allen) needs only that the consumer can **rank** bundles. An **indifference curve (IC)** joins all combinations of two goods giving the **same satisfaction**; an **indifference map** is a set of ICs, higher = preferred.

**Properties:** (1) ICs **slope downward** (more of one good needs less of the other); (2) **convex to the origin** (diminishing MRS); (3) **higher ICs mean higher satisfaction**; (4) ICs **never intersect** (it would contradict transitivity); (5) they do not touch the axes in the basic two-goods model.

**Marginal rate of substitution:** MRS_xy = −ΔY/ΔX along an IC = **MU_x/MU_y**: the amount of Y given up for one more X. It **diminishes** as the consumer has more X.

**Budget line:** all bundles costing exactly the income M: **P_x·X + P_y·Y = M**; intercepts M/P_x and M/P_y; **slope = −P_x/P_y**. An income rise shifts it out in parallel; a price change rotates it around the intercept of the unchanged good.

**Consumer's equilibrium:** the highest reachable IC touches the budget line: **tangency** with **MRS = P_x/P_y** (equivalently MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y, matching the cardinal rule), and the IC convex at that point (second-order condition).

## 3.3 Price effect, income effect and substitution effect

Varying one price and joining the equilibria gives the **price consumption curve (PCC)**; its points of (price, quantity) give the **demand curve**. Varying income and joining equilibria gives the **income consumption curve (ICC)**: upward sloping for normal goods, bending back for inferior goods; the **Engel curve** plots quantity against income.

When P_x rises the **price effect** (total change in X) splits into:

- **Substitution effect:** X is now relatively dearer, so substitute Y; always **negative** (moves opposite to price). Hicks measures it by compensating income to keep the consumer on the **original IC**.
- **Income effect:** the price rise cuts real income; for a **normal** good this lowers consumption (reinforcing); for an **inferior** good it raises it (offsetting). If an inferior good's income effect is stronger than its substitution effect we get the rare **Giffen good**.

::: example Worked example: Hicksian decomposition (utility U = X·Y)
Income M = 120, P_x = 4, P_y = 2. For U = XY the optimum is X = M/(2P_x) = **15**, Y = M/(2P_y) = **30**, U = **450**.
**P_x rises to 8:** new optimum X = 120/16 = **7.5**, Y = 30, U = 225. Price effect on X = 7.5 − 15 = **−7.5**.
**Hicks compensation:** the income needed at the new prices to stay on U = 450: X = √(450 × 2/8) = **10.61**, Y = √(450 × 8/2) = **42.43**; expenditure 8 × 10.61 + 2 × 42.43 = **169.71** (so compensating income is 49.71 more than 120).
**Substitution effect** = 10.61 − 15 = **−4.39**. **Income effect** = 7.5 − 10.61 = **−3.11**. Sum = −7.5 ✓. Both effects are negative because X is a normal good.
:::

**Derivation of the demand curve (Hicksian approach):** the demand curve is the locus of price–quantity points from the PCC (the **Marshallian** demand includes both effects); the **Hicksian (compensated) demand** holds utility constant and includes only the substitution effect, so it is steeper for normal goods. The ordinal approach needs fewer assumptions and explains inferior and Giffen goods.

## Formula and fact sheet

- MUₙ = TUₙ − TUₙ₋₁. Equilibrium: MU_x/P_x = MU_y/P_y = MU_money (equi-marginal rule).
- MRS_xy = MU_x/MU_y. Budget line: P_xX + P_yY = M, slope −P_x/P_y.
- Ordinal equilibrium: tangency, MRS = P_x/P_y.
- Price effect = substitution effect + income effect. Giffen: inferior with dominant income effect.

## Chapter summary

- Cardinal analysis explains demand through diminishing marginal utility and the equi-marginal principle.
- Ordinal analysis uses indifference curves and the budget line; equilibrium is tangency (MRS = price ratio).
- Price effects decompose into substitution and income effects; PCC gives demand and ICC gives Engel curves.

## Practice

1. From TU = 12, 22, 30, 36, 40, find the MU column and the unit at which MU is lowest.
2. State the law of diminishing marginal utility and its assumptions.
3. P_x = 5, P_y = 10, income 100. Draw the budget line (intercepts, slope). If MU_x/MU_y = 3, is the consumer in equilibrium? What should she do?
4. List the properties of indifference curves and say why they cannot cross.
5. Explain the income and substitution effects of a price fall for a normal good.
6. For U = XY, M = 80, P_x = 2, P_y = 4, find the optimal bundle and utility.
7. What are the criticisms of cardinal utility?

::: note Answers and worked solutions
**1.** MU = 12, 10, 8, 6, 4; MU is lowest (4) at the fifth unit.
**2.** As consumption of a good increases, the extra satisfaction from each additional unit falls. Assumptions: standard units, consistent tastes, continuous consumption, constant MU of money, rational consumer.
**3.** Intercepts: X = 100/5 = 20, Y = 100/10 = 10; slope = −P_x/P_y = −0.5. MRS = 3 versus price ratio 0.5: not in equilibrium; X is worth 3 units of Y to her but costs only 0.5, so she should **buy more X and less Y** until MRS falls to 0.5.
**4.** See 3.2. If two ICs crossed, the crossing bundle would lie on both, implying that two different satisfaction levels are equal, violating transitivity.
**5.** A fall in P_x makes X relatively cheaper (substitution towards X) and raises real income (income effect also raises X for a normal good): both increase consumption.
**6.** X = M/(2P_x) = 80/4 = **20**, Y = 80/8 = **10**, U = **200**.
**7.** Utility cannot be measured in numbers; MU of money is not constant; it ignores income and substitution effects; cannot explain Giffen goods.
:::
