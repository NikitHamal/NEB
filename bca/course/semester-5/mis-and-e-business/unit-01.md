A student in Dharan opens a food-delivery app, sees a restaurant in Itahari that she has never visited, pays with a wallet, and follows the rider on a map. Nobody at the restaurant met her; no cash changed hands; a phone told the rider where to go. That small evening errand uses most of the ideas in this unit: buying and selling over a network, a marketplace that matches strangers, a social network that told her which restaurant was good, and a phone that knows where she is. This unit is the first of the Tribhuvan University CACS301 syllabus and its text is Turban's *Electronic Commerce: A Managerial and Social Networks Perspective*.

## 1.1 Definitions and concepts

**Electronic commerce (EC)** is the process of buying, selling, transferring or exchanging products, services or information through computer networks, including the Internet. Three words matter in this definition. *Exchanging* includes more than money: a university that gives students a video lecture for free is still engaged in electronic exchange. *Information* is itself a product: an e-book, a stock quote, an online map. *Computer networks* means that the Internet is the main channel today, but private networks (a bank's inter-branch network, an airline's reservation network) are older forms of EC.

Turban also describes EC from several **perspectives**, which are useful because an exam question often asks "define e-commerce" and a good answer lists them:

| Perspective | EC is... | Plain meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Business process | doing business electronically by completing business processes over electronic networks | invoices, orders and payments without paper |
| Service | a tool that lets firms, consumers and managers cut service cost while improving quality and delivery speed | cheaper and faster service |
| Learning | enabling online training and education | e-learning for staff and students |
| Collaboration | a framework for inter- and intra-organisational collaboration | teams and partners working on a shared system |
| Community | a gathering place for community members to learn, transact and collaborate | forums, groups, marketplaces |
| Communication | delivery of goods, services, information or payments over networks | the network as the channel |

### 1.1.1 Defining e-business

**e-Business** is a broader term. It includes buying and selling, but also servicing customers, collaborating with business partners, conducting e-learning, and running internal electronic transactions such as e-recruitment, ERP and payroll. A simple way to remember: *all e-commerce is e-business, but not all e-business is e-commerce.* When a bank lets staff apply for leave in a web portal, that is e-business (an internal process). When a customer buys a mobile top-up from the same bank's app, that is e-commerce.

### 1.1.2 Pure versus partial e-commerce

An EC transaction has three dimensions: the **product** (physical or digital), the **process** of ordering, paying and so on (physical or digital), and the **delivery agent** or delivery method (physical or digital). If all three are digital, the EC is **pure**. If at least one is physical, it is **partial**.

| Case | Product | Process (order, payment) | Delivery agent | Type |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Buy a paperback from an online shop and pay at the door | physical | partly digital | physical rider | partial |
| Order a pair of shoes online, pay by wallet, rider delivers | physical | digital | physical | partial |
| Buy a software licence, pay by card, download it | digital | digital | digital (download) | pure |
| Buy an e-book and read it on your phone | digital | digital | digital | pure |
| Pay an electricity bill through an app | digital (a payment) | digital | digital | pure |

Most Nepali online shopping today is partial EC, because products are physical and delivery is by rider or courier.

### 1.1.3 Brick-and-mortar, virtual and click-and-mortar organisations

A **brick-and-mortar** (pure physical) organisation performs all its activities in the physical world: a neighbourhood grocery. A **virtual** (pure-play) organisation performs its activities online only: a firm that sells digital services and has no shop. A **click-and-mortar** (click-and-brick, partial EC) organisation does some business online but also keeps physical operations: a bank with branches, ATMs and a mobile app. The trend among established firms is to become click-and-mortar, because customers move between both channels: they inspect a phone in a shop and buy it online, or order online and collect in store.

## 1.2 The EC framework

Turban's **EC framework** is a picture that places the things an EC activity needs. At the top are the **EC applications**: stores and malls, auctions, banking, brokerage, e-learning, e-government, directories and so on. They stand on **five support areas** and a **base of infrastructure**.

| Layer | What it contains | Example |
|---|---|---|
| EC applications | online shops, marketplaces, e-banking, e-government, e-learning, e-health | an online bus-ticket shop |
| People | buyers, sellers, intermediaries, information-systems staff, management | the shop owner, customers, the delivery rider |
| Public policy | laws, taxes, privacy, regulation, standards | the Electronic Transactions Act of Nepal, NRB rules |
| Marketing and advertising | market research, promotion, customer service | a Facebook ad campaign |
| Support services | payment, logistics, content, security, knowledge | a wallet, a courier, an SSL certificate |
| Business partnerships | joint ventures, affiliate programmes, exchanges | a shop and a courier firm sharing data |
| Infrastructure | the Internet, intranets, extranets, networks, hardware, software | mobile data, servers, the cloud |

The lesson of the framework is that *a website alone is not an e-commerce business*. A shop with a beautiful site but no payment option, no delivery partner and no legal clarity cannot trade. Management coordinates all the layers, which is why the book is called "a managerial" perspective.

::: note Nepal context
The legal base for EC in Nepal is the **Electronic Transactions Act 2063 (2008)**, which recognises electronic records and digital signatures and creates offences for cyber crime. Payment services are regulated by Nepal Rastra Bank under the **Payment and Settlement Act 2075 (2019)**. State these as "the Act on electronic transactions" and "the payment and settlement law" if you are unsure of the exact year in an exam; the idea (law recognises electronic records and licenses payment operators) is what the examiner wants.
:::

## 1.3 Classification of e-commerce

EC is usually classified by **who the transacting parties are**. The first letter names the seller (or originator) and the last the buyer.

| Type | Parties | Typical example | Nepali illustration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **B2C** | business to consumer | online shop sells to individuals | a clothing store with a web shop and wallet payment |
| **B2B** | business to business | wholesaler's ordering portal, e-procurement, supplier exchange | a distributor's portal where shops reorder stock |
| **C2C** | consumer to consumer | classified ads, auctions, peer marketplaces | second-hand phones on a classifieds site |
| **B2B2C** | business to business to consumer | a firm sells through another business to reach end customers | a brand sells through a delivery marketplace to its customers |
| **C2B** | consumer to business | freelancers offer services, customers name a price | a graphic designer bidding on an overseas job |
| **G2C** | government to citizens | tax filing, licence renewal, bill payment | online driving-licence and passport appointment |
| **G2B** | government to business | tenders, company registration, customs | an e-bidding portal for public procurement |
| **G2G** | government to government | data exchange between ministries or with other states | shared citizen data between offices |
| **B2E** | business to its employees (intrabusiness) | staff portal, payroll, training | leave application and payslips online |
| **Collaborative commerce** | partners plan and design together | shared forecasts between maker and supplier | a factory and a supplier share demand data |
| **e-learning, e-government, e-health** | service sectors | online degree, online tax, tele-medicine | a college LMS |
| **m-commerce, l-commerce** | any of the above on a mobile or by location | app ordering, nearby offers | wallet shopping, ride-hailing |

The same transaction can fit more than one cell. If a farmer sells potatoes through a marketplace to a restaurant, the farmer is a producer (B2B or C2B) and the platform is B2B2C if the restaurant resells to a diner. In an examination, state your assumption and classify it.

### 1.3.1 Business models in brief

A **business model** is the plan by which a company makes revenue. Turban lists common EC models: *online direct marketing* (selling directly), *electronic tendering* (reverse auction for buyers), *name-your-price*, *find the best price*, *affiliate marketing* (a commission for sending buyers to another site), *viral marketing* (customers pass on the message), *group purchasing* (many buyers join to get a volume discount), *online auctions*, *product customisation* (build-to-order), *electronic marketplaces and exchanges*, and *bartering*. A **revenue model** describes how the money is earned: sales, subscription, advertising, transaction fee, affiliate fee or licensing.

## 1.4 Benefits of e-commerce

| To organisations | To consumers | To society |
|---|---|---|
| Global reach; the market is not limited to one town | Ubiquity: shop any time and from anywhere | Telecommuting and work from home reduce travel and pollution |
| Lower cost of creating, processing, distributing and storing paper information | More choice and easy price comparison | A higher standard of living when goods are cheaper and more available |
| Supports customisation: build-to-order | Customisation and personalised offers | Public services reach remote areas (e-government, e-health, e-learning) |
| Reduces inventory and overhead: a pull-type supply chain | Cheaper products through competition and discounts | Fewer cash transactions: more transparency and less leakage |
| Faster time to market and 24-hour service | Instant delivery of digital products, 24 by 7 information | New jobs (digital marketing, delivery, development) |
| Lower communication and telecommunication cost | Easy access to reviews and the experience of other buyers | Services for the elderly and disabled who cannot travel |

## 1.5 Electronic markets

A **marketplace** is a place where buyers and sellers meet to exchange goods and services. An **electronic market (e-market)** is a network of interactions and relationships over which information, products, services and payments flow. An e-market has three main functions:

1. **Matching** buyers and sellers (search, catalogues, comparison, auctions).
2. **Facilitating transactions** (logistics, payment, settlement, trust and reviews).
3. **Providing institutional infrastructure** (a legal and regulatory framework, standards, rules for disputes).

The **players** in an e-market are customers, sellers, intermediaries (brokers who match and charge a fee), and support services (payment, delivery, security). The market place, the **space** that connects them, may be a web site, an app or a private network.

| Type of electronic market | Owner | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sell-side marketplace** | one seller | sell to many customers | a company's own web shop |
| **Buy-side marketplace** | one buyer | buy from many suppliers by reverse auction or e-tender | a bank buying stationery by tender |
| **Public e-marketplace or exchange** | a third party (an independent operator) | many buyers meet many sellers | a general marketplace or classifieds portal |
| **Private exchange** | one firm with its partners | share demand and orders with chosen suppliers | a manufacturer's supplier portal |

**Disintermediation** is the removal of intermediaries (a farmer sells directly to the consumer). **Reintermediation** is the arrival of new intermediaries that add value in the digital world, such as price-comparison sites, payment gateways and delivery marketplaces. Both changes happen at once: the middlemen of the old chain disappear, and different middlemen appear.

## 1.6 The role of the Internet and the Web in e-commerce

The **Internet** is a global network of networks that carries data by the TCP/IP protocol suite. The **World Wide Web** is a service on the Internet: pages linked by hypertext and fetched by HTTP and shown in a browser. For EC they play different roles:

- The Internet is the **transport**: it connects buyer, seller, bank and courier in seconds and at a tiny cost per message.
- The Web is the **shop window and the counter**: product catalogues, forms, carts, and reviews; it hides the technical detail behind a simple page.
- Search engines, email, messaging and social networks are **marketing and service channels**.
- Intranets (a firm's private network) and extranets (partners' access) carry the internal and B2B parts.
- The Internet supports **new business models** (search advertising, marketplaces) and shortens the distance between producer and consumer, which is why the EC framework counts infrastructure as the base layer.

The Web is also a **medium of exchange for information goods**: the Internet reduces the marginal cost of copying and delivering a digital product to almost zero, which is why software, music, news and courses moved online first.

## 1.7 Limitations and barriers of e-commerce

Turban divides the limits into **technological** and **non-technological**. Many shrink every year as technology improves, but each one explains why a business plan must be tested before it is launched.

| Group | Limitation | What it means in practice |
|---|---|---|
| Technological | No universal standards for quality, security and reliability | different shops, gateways and apps behave differently |
| Technological | Insufficient telecommunication bandwidth and slow access | pages that fail on a weak mobile connection |
| Technological | Software tools are still evolving; integration with existing systems is hard | the new shop and the old accounting software do not talk to each other |
| Technological | Special web servers and network equipment needed in addition to the network server | extra cost for scalability |
| Non-technological | Cost and justification: a big investment with uncertain return | a start-up does not know its conversion rate until it trades |
| Non-technological | Security and privacy: fear of fraud and of data misuse | customers avoid online payment |
| Non-technological | Lack of trust and user resistance: customers want to touch and see the product | return and refund disputes |
| Non-technological | Many legal and regulatory issues are unresolved or differ by country | cross-border sales and tax |
| Non-technological | Sales and delivery: address systems, logistics and cash-on-delivery habits | failed deliveries and returned goods |
| Non-technological | Digital divide: not everyone has a device, data or skill | rural and elderly users are left out |

::: key Why "Nepal's barriers" is a common exam question
Group your answer: *infrastructure* (connectivity and electricity), *payments* (limited card use, cash-on-delivery, wallet interoperability), *trust and legal* (fraud, weak consumer remedies), *logistics* (informal addressing, difficult terrain, returns) and *skills* (digital literacy). Give one example for each and one improvement.
:::

## 1.8 Social networks and social network services

A **social network** is a map of relationships among individuals, groups or organisations. A **social network service (SNS)** is an online platform that lets members create a profile, connect with others, share content and talk. Turban distinguishes services by purpose: *general* networks (Facebook, Instagram, TikTok), *professional* networks (LinkedIn), *messaging* (Viber, WhatsApp, Messenger), *media sharing* (YouTube), *microblogging* (X) and *question-and-answer or interest communities* (Reddit, Quora).

For business, an SNS plays four roles:

1. **Advertising and brand building.** Pages, groups and paid ads reach targeted audiences by age, place and interest.
2. **Customer service and listening.** Complaints and compliments appear in public, so a fast reply protects reputation.
3. **Word-of-mouth and trust.** Reviews and recommendations from friends influence purchase more than a banner does.
4. **Direct selling: social commerce.** Shops and live-selling inside the platform, shopping tags, and orders by chat.

**Social commerce** is the delivery of EC activities and transactions through social media, using Web 2.0 tools and the social context of the buyer. Typical activities are *ratings and reviews*, *recommendations and referrals*, *social shopping* (friends shop together or share carts), *forums and communities* and *group buying*. In Nepal many small sellers start with a Facebook or Instagram page, take orders by chat, and settle by wallet or cash on delivery. That approach has a low cost of entry but weak records, so growth usually requires a proper web shop.

::: note Risks
Fake reviews and fake accounts, privacy leaks, rumour that spreads faster than correction, and dependence on one platform's rules (an account ban can end a business). A firm should keep its customer list and its own domain, not only a social page.
:::

## 1.9 m-Commerce: concept, scope, attributes and benefits

**Mobile commerce (m-commerce)** is any EC transaction, or any part of one, that is carried out through a wireless device and a wireless network. The phone is personal, always carried and always connected, so m-commerce is not simply a smaller screen of the same shop.

**Scope.** The main application areas are:

| Area | Examples |
|---|---|
| Mobile financial services | wallet payments, QR payments, mobile banking, top-ups, remittance |
| Mobile shopping and ordering | apps for food, groceries, fashion, tickets |
| Mobile advertising | SMS offers, in-app ads, push notifications |
| Mobile entertainment | games, streaming, ringback tones |
| Mobile enterprise | field sales, staff attendance, delivery tracking |
| Mobile information services | news, weather, train and bus information, market prices for farmers |

**Attributes (what is special about the mobile channel).** Turban lists these:

- **Mobility**: users carry the device and can act anywhere.
- **Broad reach**: a user can be reached at any time (the phone number is the address).
- **Ubiquity**: access to information and transactions in real time regardless of the place.
- **Convenience**: the device is small, always at hand and already holds payment credentials.
- **Instant connectivity**: quick connection to the Internet and other users.
- **Personalisation**: the phone belongs to one person, so offers and messages can be tailored.
- **Localisation of products and services**: the network knows where the user is and can offer what is nearby.

**Benefits.** Customers get faster service and the ability to buy at the moment of need. Businesses reach customers who have a phone but no computer (the majority in Nepal), capture precise data, and take payment instantly. Society gains financial inclusion when a phone replaces a branch.

**Limitations.** Small screens and input, battery life, bandwidth and coverage (a difficulty in hills), device and platform fragmentation, and security (lost phones, malicious apps, SIM swap).

## 1.10 Location-based commerce (l-commerce)

**Location-based commerce (l-commerce)** is m-commerce transactions targeted to individuals in a specific location at a specific time. Its building block is the **location-based service (LBS)**, a service that uses the user's position to give information or perform an action.

### 1.10.1 Infrastructure of l-commerce

Five components work together:

| Component | Role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile devices | carry the user and the application | a smartphone or a vehicle tracker |
| Communication network | transmits the data between the device and the provider | the mobile data network |
| Positioning component | finds where the user is | GPS satellites, cell-tower and Wi-Fi positioning, Bluetooth beacons |
| Service or application provider | processes the request and provides the service | a ride-hailing or maps app |
| Data or content provider | supplies the maps, restaurant lists, traffic and weather | a digital map database |

### 1.10.2 Location-based services and applications

- **Navigation and maps**: find the route and the traffic on the way.
- **Ride hailing and delivery tracking**: the rider and customer see each other on a map and the fare is computed from distance.
- **Location-based advertising and offers**: a coupon is sent when a user is near a shop (geo-fencing).
- **Emergency services**: send the caller's location to an ambulance or police.
- **Tracking assets and people**: buses, cargo, field staff, and a family-sharing function.
- **Location-based games and social tagging**: games tied to places and check-ins.
- **Tourism**: nearby sights, hotels and a guide in the trekker's language.

**Positioning methods compared.**

| Method | How it works | Accuracy (typical) | Where it fails |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPS | trilateration from satellites | a few metres outdoors | indoors, deep valleys, tall buildings |
| Cell-tower (cell ID) | position of the serving tower | hundreds of metres to some kilometres | sparse towers in rural areas |
| Wi-Fi positioning | known positions of nearby access points | tens of metres | few access points |
| Bluetooth beacon | short-range signal from a fixed tag | one to a few metres | needs installed beacons |

**Privacy.** Location is highly sensitive data: it reveals home, work and habits. Ethical l-commerce uses *consent (opt-in)*, collects only what is needed, tells the user how the data is used, and lets the user delete it. Tracking without permission is a legal and reputational risk.

### 1.10.3 Eight lessons from this unit for a Nepali business

1. **Decide pure or partial first.** A seller of digital courses can be pure EC; a seller of momo is partial. The logistics plan differs completely, so choose before building.
2. **Be click-and-mortar when trust matters.** A visible shop and a phone number convert hesitant customers; the web shop supplies reach.
3. **Use the framework as a checklist.** Before launch, tick payment, delivery, law, marketing, partners and infrastructure, not only design.
4. **Choose the market type deliberately.** Selling on a public marketplace gives traffic and takes commission; your own sell-side shop gives control and needs marketing.
5. **Expect disintermediation and reintermediation.** Selling direct removes a wholesaler, but you will pay a delivery or payment intermediary. Count both in the price.
6. **Treat the phone as the primary screen.** Design for a small screen, weak data and wallet payment; a site that is perfect on a laptop but slow on a phone loses most buyers.
7. **Use social networks for trust, not only for ads.** Reviews, replies and live demonstrations convert; keep your own customer list.
8. **Handle location data with care.** Ask permission, store little, delete on request; one privacy scandal destroys the trust that location services need.

## Chapter summary

- EC is buying, selling, transferring or exchanging products, services and information over computer networks. e-business is wider and includes internal processes and collaboration.
- EC is pure when product, process and delivery are all digital; otherwise it is partial. Organisations are brick-and-mortar, virtual or click-and-mortar.
- The EC framework places applications on five support areas and an infrastructure base.
- Classification is by parties: B2C, B2B, C2C, B2B2C, C2B, G2C, G2B, G2G, B2E, plus collaborative commerce and m- and l-commerce.
- Benefits go to organisations, consumers and society; limits are technological and non-technological.
- An e-market matches buyers and sellers, facilitates transactions and gives institutional infrastructure; sell-side, buy-side, public and private types exist.
- Social commerce uses social networks and their trust; m-commerce uses a personal, mobile, always-on device; l-commerce adds the user's location, which needs five components and careful privacy.

## Model answers: medium (5 marks) and long (10 marks) questions

**Q1 (5 marks). What is pure and partial e-commerce? Illustrate with examples.**

*Introduction.* EC is classed by how much of a transaction is digital (section 1.1).

1. **Three dimensions.** The product, the process (order and payment) and the delivery agent can each be physical or digital.
2. **Pure EC.** All three digital: a paid software download, an e-book, an online bill payment.
3. **Partial EC.** At least one physical: shoes ordered online and delivered by a rider.
4. **Why it matters.** Partial EC needs logistics, stock and returns; pure EC needs only bandwidth and licensing.

**Q2 (10 marks). Describe the classification of e-commerce with an example for each type, and discuss its benefits and limitations.**

*Introduction.* EC is classified by the parties to the transaction (section 1.3), and its value must be set against its limits (sections 1.4 and 1.7).

1. **B2C**: an online shop sells to a person. **B2B**: a distributor portal serves shops. **C2C**: used items on a classifieds site. **B2B2C**: a brand sells through a marketplace.
2. **Government types**: G2C (online licence renewal), G2B (tenders) and G2G (data sharing between offices).
3. **Intrabusiness (B2E)**: staff portal, payroll. **Collaborative commerce**: shared forecasts. **m-commerce and l-commerce**: delivered by phone and location.
4. **Benefits to organisations.** Global reach, lower paper and inventory cost, customisation and 24-hour service.
5. **Benefits to consumers.** Ubiquity, choice, price comparison and personalisation.
6. **Benefits to society.** Telecommuting, access to services in remote areas, new jobs.
7. **Technological limits.** Standards, bandwidth, integration and special servers.
8. **Non-technological limits.** Cost justification, security, trust, legal uncertainty, delivery and digital divide.
9. **Conclusion.** Benefits are large but only realised when payment, logistics, law and trust are in place; for Nepal the decisive barriers are logistics, payment habits and trust.

**Q3 (10 marks). What is m-commerce? Explain its attributes, applications and the infrastructure of location-based commerce.**

*Introduction.* m-commerce is EC through a wireless device and network, and l-commerce adds the user's location (sections 1.9 and 1.10).

1. **Attributes.** Mobility, broad reach, ubiquity, convenience, instant connectivity, personalisation and localisation.
2. **Applications.** Mobile banking and wallets, mobile shopping, advertising, entertainment, enterprise and information services.
3. **Benefits.** Buying at the moment of need, reach to phone-only users, instant payment, financial inclusion.
4. **l-commerce infrastructure.** Mobile device, communication network, positioning (GPS, cell tower, Wi-Fi, beacon), application provider and content provider (Figure 1.2).
5. **Applications of LBS.** Navigation, ride hailing, geo-fenced offers, emergency location, asset tracking and tourism.
6. **Concerns.** Privacy, consent, battery, coverage and security. **Conclusion.** The same properties that make the phone valuable (personal, always on, aware of place) make privacy and security central.
