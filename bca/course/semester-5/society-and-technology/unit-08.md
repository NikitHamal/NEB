## 8.1 The technological society

A **technological society** is one in which *technology is the main driver of the economy, of social organisation and of everyday life*, so that people depend on machines, networks and technical systems for work, communication, health, learning and government. It is not just a society *that has* gadgets; it is one where technical rationality (efficiency, measurement, standardisation) has spread into every institution.

| Thinker | Idea | Key phrase |
|---|---|---|
| **Daniel Bell** (1973) | Societies move from pre-industrial (agriculture) to industrial (goods) to **post-industrial** (services and knowledge); theoretical knowledge becomes the central resource | *The Coming of Post-Industrial Society* |
| **Alvin Toffler** (1980) | Three waves: agricultural, industrial and **information**; each wave changes work, family and politics | *The Third Wave* |
| **Jacques Ellul** (1954) | Technique (efficient method) becomes autonomous and shapes society more than society shapes it; a warning | *The Technological Society* |
| **Manuel Castells** (1996) | Networks of information form the new social structure: the **network society**, where power belongs to those who control flows of information | *The Rise of the Network Society* |
| **Marshall McLuhan** (1964) | Each medium reshapes how people think and relate, apart from its content | *The medium is the message*; the *global village* |
| **Langdon Winner** (1980) | Artefacts have politics: a bridge, a database or an algorithm can embed choices about who benefits | *Do artifacts have politics?* |

### Characteristics of a technological society

1. **Knowledge and skills are the main capital.** Education, research and training decide a person's and a country's position.
2. **Rapid and continuous change.** Skills become outdated in a decade; lifelong learning is compulsory (the lesson of Moore's law, Unit 4).
3. **Networks and speed.** Information, money and ideas move across borders in seconds; time and distance shrink.
4. **Specialisation and dependence.** Few people understand the systems they use every day (banking, power grid, cloud), so society depends on *experts* and on trust.
5. **Rationalisation and surveillance.** Data are collected on everything for efficiency, creating new risks to privacy (Unit 7).
6. **New inequalities.** The gap between those who can use technology and those who cannot (the digital divide, Unit 6) adds to the old gaps of caste, class and gender (Unit 3).
7. **Cultural convergence and fragmentation.** Global platforms spread common styles while personalised feeds fragment audiences.

**Is Nepal a technological society?** Not fully. In the 2021 census a majority of households had a mobile phone and a growing share used the internet, while many still cooked with firewood and farmed without machines. Sociologists therefore describe Nepal as a **society in transition**: pre-industrial, industrial and information features coexist in the same village, even in the same house (a farmer who ploughs with oxen and pays by QR code).

## 8.2 Technological change in Third World societies

The term **Third World** originally meant countries not aligned with either bloc in the Cold War; today the neutral terms *developing countries*, *the Global South* or *low- and middle-income countries* are preferred, but the TU outline uses the older phrase, and you may use it in answers with a brief note. Nepal is a least-developed country (LDC) that is scheduled to graduate from the LDC category, a process that began with a 2021 UN recommendation, with graduation set for 2026.

### Why technological change is different in the South

| Feature | Situation | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Imported technology** | Most machines, software and phones come from abroad | Foreign exchange drain; dependence on suppliers; little local know-how |
| **Mismatch of conditions** | Tools designed for rich, urban and educated users may not suit low income, poor electricity or local languages | Failure or misuse; waste of scarce capital |
| **Dual economy** | A modern sector next to a large traditional and informal one | Benefits go first to cities and the educated |
| **Capital-intensive vs labour-intensive** | Imported machines save labour in countries where labour is the abundant resource | Jobs may fall while output rises |
| **Weak institutions** | Standards, regulators and maintenance services are limited | Poor quality, e-waste (Unit 11) and security gaps |
| **Brain drain and brain circulation** | Skilled workers migrate, then partly return with money and ideas | Loss of skills, partly offset by remittances and networks |

### Models and ideas

- **Modernisation theory.** Developing countries should follow the path of the West: capital, technology, education and urbanisation produce growth (Rostow, *stages of growth*).
- **Dependency theory.** Poor countries are kept dependent by rich ones through trade and technology; growth in the North is linked to underdevelopment of the South (Frank, Amin).
- **Technology transfer.** The movement of technology, along with knowledge and skills, from one organisation or country to another. Transfer succeeds only if the receiver can *absorb, adapt and maintain* the technology; *buying a machine is not the same as acquiring the technology*.
- **Appropriate technology.** E. F. Schumacher, in *Small is Beautiful* (1973), argued for "intermediate technology": tools that are cheap, small, locally maintainable, labour-friendly and environmentally sound. Nepali examples: **micro-hydro plants** and improved water mills (*ghatta*), **biogas plants** (Nepal has one of the oldest national biogas programmes in South Asia), **solar home systems** and the **improved cooking stove**.
- **Leapfrogging.** Skipping a stage because the newer technology is cheaper than the old one. Africa and South Asia skipped the landline for the mobile phone; Nepal moved from postal money orders to mobile wallets and QR payments without a long credit-card phase.

::: example Leapfrogging with mobile money
In Kenya, M-Pesa (launched 2007) let people send money by SMS where few had bank accounts. In Nepal, wallets such as eSewa and Khalti and interbank QR through Fonepay and NepalPay allowed a vegetable seller in a market to accept payment without ever owning a card terminal. Lesson: when a technology fits an existing need and works on the equipment people already own, it can leap past older infrastructure.
:::

### Criticism: the technology fix

Not every problem is technical. A **technological fix** is the belief that a gadget alone will solve a social problem. A tablet in a school does little without electricity, teacher training, content in the pupil's language and a maintenance plan. The lesson from many "one laptop per child" style projects, in South Asia as elsewhere, is that *technology amplifies the institutions around it*; it does not replace them.

## 8.3 Social and cultural bases of technological change

Technology does not enter an empty room. Whether a tool is adopted, how it is used and who benefits depend on the society's *economy, institutions, values and social structure*. Sociologists call these the **social and cultural bases** of technological change.

### Key ideas

- **Cultural lag (William Ogburn, 1922).** Material culture (tools, machines) changes faster than non-material culture (laws, customs, ethics). The gap is the *lag*: when drones, deepfakes or digital lending arrive, laws and manners take years to catch up.
- **Social construction of technology (SCOT).** Technologies are shaped by the groups that use them; early bicycles with a huge front wheel gave way to the safety bicycle because *users* (women, older riders) had different needs. Meanings, not only engineering, decide success.
- **Technological determinism versus social shaping.** The first view says technology decides society; the second says society decides technology. Most sociologists today accept a *two-way* influence (Unit 4, Section 4.2).
- **Values and worldview.** Max Weber linked the spirit of capitalism to a particular ethic of work and thrift; more generally, a culture that values enterprise, education, punctuality and trust in strangers adopts commerce and technology more readily.

### Social and cultural bases in the Nepali context

| Base | How it shapes technological change | Nepali example |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | Income, credit, remittance and cost of devices | Smartphones spread fast because remittances paid for them; a smart tractor cannot spread where plots are tiny |
| **Political and legal** | Policy, subsidy, regulation, stability | Telecom liberalisation (2003 onward) expanded mobile networks; import duty changes alter device prices |
| **Educational** | Literacy and technical skill | Adoption of e-banking is lower among less literate and older users |
| **Social structure** | Caste, gender and class decide who gets access, training and credit | Women share phones or need permission to use them; occupational castes lost trades (tailors, blacksmiths) when factory goods arrived |
| **Cultural values** | Beliefs about work, purity, risk and authority | Reluctance to use biogas toilets in some communities for reasons of purity; trust in the *vaidya* or the *dhami* before the clinic |
| **Language** | Content in the user's language | Voice assistants and forms in Nepali, Maithili and Tamang decide who can use them |
| **Family and community networks** | Social proof and word of mouth | A returnee from the Gulf shows the village how to use a wallet; the *aama samuha* adopts one savings app together |
| **Geography and infrastructure** | Roads, power, signal | Online classes fail in the high mountains without electricity or signal |

**Case: the improved cooking stove.** Engineers designed a stove with a chimney that cut indoor smoke. In some villages families kept using the open hearth for the same reasons: the hearth heated the room, dried grain and meat, kept insects away and was the centre of family life. Programmes succeeded when stoves were designed *with* women, repaired locally and priced for the user. The technology was sound; the *social fit* was the problem.

**Case: Community radio.** Nepal's community radio movement (from Radio Sagarmatha in 1997) spread because it matched a strong oral tradition, local languages and the need for local news, and because law and donors supported it. Today community radio coexists with Facebook live and TikTok.

## 8.4 Process and patterns of diffusion of innovation

**Diffusion** is the process by which an innovation spreads through a social system over time through communication channels (Everett Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations*, 1962). Unit 5 develops the mathematical models (logistic and Bass curves) and the adopter categories; this section links them to the social and cultural bases above.

### The process

1. **Knowledge.** People learn the innovation exists (mass media, friends).
2. **Persuasion.** They form an opinion after seeing its advantages and cost.
3. **Decision.** They adopt or reject after a trial.
4. **Implementation.** They use it and adapt it to local conditions.
5. **Confirmation.** They continue or discontinue based on results and social support.

### Patterns

| Pattern | Description | Nepali illustration |
|---|---|---|
| **S-shaped curve** | Slow start, rapid middle, saturation (Unit 5) | Mobile subscriptions reached a share above one SIM per person after 2010 |
| **Adopter categories** | Innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority, laggards | In a ward, a returned migrant and the teacher try a wallet first; the elderly adopt last |
| **Hierarchical diffusion** | Spreads from large towns to smaller towns and to villages | Digital banking: Kathmandu, provincial capitals, municipalities, wards |
| **Neighbourhood diffusion** | Spreads to adjacent places by contact | A QR code in one market is copied by neighbouring shops |
| **Opinion leaders** | Respected persons whose approval speeds adoption | Ward chair, teacher, women's group leader, health volunteer |
| **Reinvention** | Users change the innovation | Farmers use Viber groups to trade vegetables and weather alerts |

### What speeds or slows diffusion (Rogers' attributes)

*Relative advantage* (is it better?), *compatibility* (does it fit values and habits?), *complexity* (is it hard to learn?), *trialability* (can I test it?) and *observability* (can I see the results?). Innovations that are advantageous, compatible, simple, testable and visible spread fast.

## 8.5 Consequences of technological development on Nepalese society

Technology is a double-edged tool. Every consequence below has a positive and a negative side, and an exam answer scores best when it shows both.

| Area | Positive consequences | Negative consequences |
|---|---|---|
| **Economy** | Remittance-funded consumption and investment; digital payments; e-commerce and delivery apps; ride-hailing and freelance work; an IT-services and outsourcing industry; tourism marketing online | Dependence on imports and remittances; informal workers displaced by automation; online fraud; digital debt traps; unequal gains between cities and villages |
| **Employment and skills** | New jobs for developers, designers, data and support staff; remote work for foreign clients | Skill mismatch; low-paid platform work without security; youth emigration of the best-trained ("brain drain") |
| **Family and community** | Video calls keep migrant families connected; women run savings groups through apps | Absent parents, care for the old by phone only; screen time; reduced face-to-face community life |
| **Education** | Online classes during COVID-19, e-libraries, free tutorials, scholarship portals | Unequal access to devices and bandwidth; copying, cyber-cheating and attention problems |
| **Health** | Telemedicine; vaccination and disease tracking; female community health volunteers use phones | Misinformation, self-medication on the basis of online advice, privacy of health data |
| **Culture and language** | Digitisation of folk songs and manuscripts; global reach of Nepali music and film; mother-tongue content on YouTube | Homogenisation, English and Hindi dominance, loss of minority languages, imitation of foreign lifestyle |
| **Politics and governance** | e-governance (National ID, online tax filing, vehicle and passport services); campaigning and civic participation; faster accountability | Disinformation and hate speech; surveillance and censorship; polarisation; state control of the net |
| **Environment** | Weather alerts, GIS for landslide risk, smart irrigation, solar | E-waste, energy use of data centres, mining of materials (Unit 11) |
| **Security and crime** | Online complaints, CCTV | Cybercrime, identity theft, sextortion and gambling apps (Unit 9) |

### A recent illustration

In September 2025, a government order that blocked social media platforms that had not registered with the authorities led to large youth-led protests across the country, which led to the fall of the government. The events are used by analysts as a case of how deeply platforms are woven into Nepali civic life, how generational frustration and digital media reinforce each other, and how any regulation of the net becomes a question of rights as well as of order. Use it carefully in an exam: state the facts that you are sure of (the order, the protests, the resignation) and focus on the sociological lesson.

### Social problems linked to technology

- **Digital addiction and mental health**: gaming and short-video apps among teenagers.
- **Cyberbullying and harassment**, which disproportionately affect women and minority groups.
- **Misinformation**: false health and political posts spread faster than corrections.
- **Digital gambling and loan apps**, which exploit financial vulnerability.
- **Generation gap**: the young adopt quickly while elders depend on them (technology brokers).
- **Unemployment of the educated**: degrees without matching skills.

**Balanced conclusion.** Technology has widened choice and connection in Nepal, but it has also widened some gaps. The sociologist's view: outcomes depend on *who controls the technology, who has the skills and for whose purposes it is used*. Policy should therefore combine access (infrastructure), capability (education and local-language content) and safeguard (law, ethics, accountability).

## 8.6 Accountability of computer professionals towards society

A computing professional builds the systems on which banks, hospitals, schools and governments depend, and a mistake can harm thousands. **Accountability** means being *answerable for the effects of one's work*, not only to the employer but to users and society.

### Why computing professionals are accountable

1. **Impact and scale.** One piece of code reaches millions (a faulty update, a biased scoring rule).
2. **Special knowledge.** Users cannot check what the system does; they must trust the professional.
3. **Power to harm silently.** Data leaks and discrimination may not be visible for years.
4. **Public dependence.** Essential services run on software.

### Areas of accountability

| Area | Duty | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Safety and quality** | Test, document and refuse to release unsafe systems | Therac-25 radiation overdoses, caused by software defects, 1985-1987 |
| **Privacy and data protection** | Collect the minimum, protect it, use it only for the stated purpose (Individual Privacy Act 2075) | Encrypting citizen records; not browsing a customer's data out of curiosity |
| **Honesty and integrity** | No false claims, no hidden functions, honest reporting of bugs and risks | Declaring a security breach to those affected |
| **Fairness and inclusion** | Check systems for bias; design for people with disabilities and for local languages | Accessible forms; testing a loan model on every group |
| **Intellectual property** | Respect licences and copyright; credit authors | Avoiding pirated software and copied code |
| **Environmental responsibility** | Efficient software, long-life hardware, proper e-waste handling | Unit 11 |
| **Public good** | Use skills for society; volunteer; speak up when the public is at risk (whistle-blowing) | Civic-tech, open data and digital literacy classes |
| **Professional competence** | Keep learning; work within one's competence | Not building a medical device without medical-safety training |

**Codes of conduct.** The **ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct** (2018) starts with the principle that professionals should *contribute to society and to human well-being* and *avoid harm*; the **IEEE Code of Ethics** commits members to the safety, health and welfare of the public. Nepal has no single binding professional code for software engineers, but the Electronic Transactions Act 2063, the Individual Privacy Act 2075 and the Copyright Act 2059 define legal limits (see Unit 9).

::: example A decision under pressure
Your manager asks you to release an e-payment feature on Friday for a festival sale. Testing shows a rare bug that may charge some users twice. What do you do? Accountable response: (1) report the bug and its risk in writing; (2) propose a limited release, a rollback plan or a delay; (3) if the company still insists on releasing an unsafe system, escalate internally, and consider the whistle-blowing procedures. The professional's duty to avoid harm to users is higher than the wish to please a manager.
:::

## 8.7 Research in social science (introduction)

Some versions of the TU outline also ask for a brief understanding of *research in social science*. Research is a **systematic, objective and orderly search for knowledge**, so that your statements about society (for example, that "mobile banking is reducing the need for branches in villages") rest on evidence, not opinion.

### Steps of the research process

1. **Select the problem.** A clear, narrow and researchable question: "Why do women in ward 5 use mobile wallets less than men?"
2. **Review the literature.** What do earlier studies, reports and census tables say?
3. **State objectives and hypotheses.** A *hypothesis* is a testable statement: "Women with secondary education are more likely to use a wallet."
4. **Design the study.** Choose the unit of analysis, variables and method.
5. **Collect data.** Primary (from the field) or secondary (from existing sources).
6. **Analyse and interpret.** Tabulate, calculate percentages, test the hypothesis.
7. **Report.** Findings, limits and recommendations, with citations.

### Types and methods

| Distinction | Choices |
|---|---|
| **Approach** | *Quantitative* (numbers, surveys, statistics) and *qualitative* (words, meanings, interviews, observation); *mixed methods* use both |
| **Purpose** | Exploratory (new topic), descriptive (what is happening), explanatory (why), applied (solve a problem) |
| **Data source** | Primary (survey, interview, observation, focus-group discussion); secondary (census, annual reports, journals, administrative records) |

**Data collection tools.**

- **Questionnaire** and **schedule** (a questionnaire filled by the respondent; a schedule filled by the interviewer).
- **Interview** (structured, semi-structured, unstructured); **focus-group discussion** with six to ten people.
- **Observation** (participant and non-participant); **case study**; **content analysis** of texts, posts or media.

**Sampling.** A **population** is the entire group studied; a **sample** is a part chosen to represent it. *Probability* methods (simple random, systematic, stratified, cluster) let every unit have a known chance and allow generalisation. *Non-probability* methods (purposive, quota, snowball, convenience) are used when there is no frame, for example in qualitative studies.

**Validity and reliability.** *Validity*: does the instrument measure what it claims? *Reliability*: does it give consistent results? A questionnaire in English given to people who read only Nepali is neither.

**Research ethics.** Informed consent, voluntary participation, confidentiality and anonymity, no harm, honest reporting and acknowledgement of sources. Data about people are *sensitive*: store them securely and delete them when no longer needed, the same duty as in Section 8.6.

::: example A small survey design
Problem: *How do students of a BCA college use social media for learning?* Population: 400 students of semesters 1 to 4. Sample: 80 students chosen by *stratified random sampling* (20 from each semester). Tool: a ten-question questionnaire in English and Nepali. Hypothesis: students in later semesters use social media more for learning than for entertainment. Analysis: percentages by semester shown in a table and a bar chart. Limits: self-reported answers, one college only.
:::

## Chapter summary

- A technological society is one in which technology and technical knowledge drive the economy, organisation and everyday life (Bell: post-industrial; Toffler: third wave; Ellul: technique; Castells: network society). Nepal is a society in transition with old and new features side by side.
- In Third World societies, technology is mostly imported, so transfer, dependency, appropriate technology and leapfrogging are the key ideas. Technology works only if it fits local conditions and institutions.
- The social and cultural bases of technological change are economic, political, educational, social-structural, cultural, linguistic, community and geographic. Cultural lag appears where tools outrun norms and laws.
- Innovations diffuse through knowledge, persuasion, decision, implementation and confirmation; opinion leaders and the five Rogers attributes decide the speed.
- Consequences on Nepal are mixed: remittance and digital economy, connection and education on one side; inequality, misinformation, e-waste and crime on the other.
- Computing professionals are accountable for safety, privacy, honesty, fairness, intellectual property, environment and public good; codes of ethics and national laws guide them.
- Social research moves from problem and hypothesis through design, data collection and analysis to report, with ethics throughout.

## Model answers: medium (5 marks) and long (10 marks) questions

**Q1 (5 marks). What is a technological society? Explain its main characteristics.**

*Meaning.* A technological society is one in which technology and the technical way of thinking (efficiency and rational organisation) shape the economy, institutions and daily life.

*Characteristics.* Knowledge is the main resource (Bell's post-industrial society). Change is rapid, so continuous learning is required. Networks link people and organisations in real time (Castells). Specialisation produces dependence on experts and on trust in systems. Data collection and surveillance expand. New divides appear between those who can and cannot use technology.

*Nepal.* A society in transition, where traditional and digital practices overlap.

**Q2 (10 marks). Explain the social and cultural bases of technological change with Nepali examples.**

*Introduction.* Technology is adopted, adapted or rejected by a society according to its social and cultural conditions. Ogburn's cultural lag and the social construction of technology explain why.

*Economic base.* Income and remittance decide who can buy devices; smartphones spread because remittances financed them.

*Political and legal base.* Telecom liberalisation expanded networks; regulation and taxes change prices.

*Educational base.* Literacy and skills decide the adoption of online services; low literacy slows e-banking.

*Social structure.* Gender and caste decide who has phones, training and credit; machine goods displaced occupational castes.

*Cultural values.* Beliefs about purity, work and risk influenced the adoption of stoves and biogas toilets.

*Language and community.* Content in Nepali and mother tongues, and opinion leaders such as teachers and women's groups, speed adoption.

*Conclusion.* A technology succeeds when it fits these bases or when the bases are changed with it. Planners should therefore combine technical design with social design: consult users, train, and adapt to local needs.

**Q3 (10 marks). Discuss the consequences of technological development on Nepalese society.**

*Introduction.* Technology has altered almost every area of life in Nepal; the effects are both beneficial and harmful.

*Positive.* The economy gains from remittance-based consumption, digital payments, e-commerce and IT services. Education gains from online classes and e-libraries. Families stay in touch through video calls. Governance becomes faster with online services and the National ID. Culture gains reach through digitised music, film and mother-tongue content.

*Negative.* Inequality between rich and poor, urban and rural, men and women. Cybercrime, fraud and digital gambling. Misinformation and hate speech, which can harden identity lines. Loss of traditional occupations, brain drain and e-waste. Cultural homogenisation and weakening of face-to-face community life.

*Conclusion.* Technology is neither good nor bad in itself. Its impact in Nepal depends on access, skill, law and ethics; a balanced policy invests in infrastructure *and* in people and safeguards.

**Q4 (5 marks). What do you mean by accountability of computer professionals? Mention its areas.**

Accountability means being answerable for the consequences of one's technical work to the users and to society, not only to the employer. Areas: *safety and quality* (testing and honest release decisions), *privacy and data protection*, *honesty and integrity*, *fairness and inclusion*, *respect for intellectual property*, *environmental care* and *service to the public good*. The ACM and IEEE codes and Nepal's laws on electronic transactions, privacy and copyright guide the professional's conduct.
