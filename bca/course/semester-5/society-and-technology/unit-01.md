Every BCA graduate will write software that is used by people, inside families, offices, schools and government agencies. Why does a mobile wallet spread quickly in Kathmandu and slowly in a hill village? Why is a rule obeyed in one office and ignored in another? Why do two teams with the same tools produce different results? None of these questions has a purely technical answer. They are *social* questions, and **sociology** is the discipline that studies them in a disciplined, evidence-based way. This unit introduces the subject: what it is, how it came about, what it studies, and how it relates to the other social sciences (the first topic block of the TU syllabus for BCA 305).

::: key What this unit gives you
- Definitions of sociology and the idea of the *sociological imagination*.
- The nature of sociology as a science, and the three main perspectives: functionalism, conflict theory and symbolic interactionism.
- The scope of sociology: formal and synthetic schools, branches, micro and macro levels.
- How sociology relates to anthropology, psychology, economics, political science, history, geography and computer science.
- Why computer professionals need it.
:::

## 1.1 Definition of sociology

The word **sociology** was coined by the French philosopher **Auguste Comte** (1798 to 1857), who first used it in 1838 in his *Course of Positive Philosophy*. It combines the Latin *socius* (companion, associate) and the Greek *logos* (study or science): the *science of society*. Comte had earlier called it "social physics", because he wanted the study of society to be as systematic as physics.

Different thinkers have defined the subject from different angles. Learn two or three, not all, and be able to explain them in your own words.

| Thinker | Definition (in brief) | Emphasis |
|---|---|---|
| Auguste Comte | The science of social order and social progress (social statics and social dynamics). | Order and change |
| Emile Durkheim | The science of **social facts**: ways of acting, thinking and feeling that exist outside the individual and constrain him. | Society as a reality over individuals |
| Max Weber | A science that tries to understand **social action** by interpreting its meaning, and thereby to explain its causes. | Meaning and action |
| MacIver and Page | The study of **social relationships**, or the web of those relationships called society. | Relationships |
| Ogburn and Nimkoff | The scientific study of social life. | Method |
| Anthony Giddens | The study of human social life, groups and societies; its subject matter is our own behaviour as social beings. | Everyday life |

::: key A working definition for exams
Sociology is the systematic and scientific study of human society, social relationships, social institutions and social behaviour, and of how they change over time.
:::

### The sociological imagination

The American sociologist **C. Wright Mills** (1959) said that the task of sociology is to develop the **sociological imagination**: the ability to connect *personal troubles* to *public issues*. If one person is unemployed, that is a personal trouble; if a large share of young people in a country cannot find work and leave for foreign employment, it is a public issue with causes in the economy, education and policy. Nepal's foreign-employment migration is a good example. A family's decision to send a son abroad is personal; the fact that a large part of the economy's income arrives as remittances is a social structure that explains thousands of such decisions.

::: example Applying the imagination to a software problem
A mobile-banking app is uninstalled by most women in a district within a month. A technical view looks for bugs. A sociological view asks: who owns the phone (often shared, often the husband's)? Who controls money in the household? Is the interface in a language the user reads comfortably? Is there trust in digital money? The bug list may be empty, and the problem still real.
:::

### Where sociology came from

Sociology emerged in the nineteenth century as a response to three upheavals: the **Industrial Revolution** (factories, cities and new classes), the **French Revolution** and the rise of democratic ideas (questioning inherited authority), and the **Enlightenment faith in science**. People asked how social order is possible when old bonds of village and kin weaken. The founders gave different answers: Comte looked for a science of order, Marx for the laws of class conflict, Durkheim for the sources of solidarity, Weber for the meaning of rationalisation. Their ideas still shape the three perspectives in section 1.3.

## 1.2 Nature of sociology

Textbooks describe the *nature* of sociology by listing its characteristics. Each point below should be followed by a reason, not just stated.

1. **Sociology is a social science, not a natural science.** It studies human beings, who think, choose and attach meanings, so its results are probabilities and tendencies, not exact laws. A chemist can repeat an experiment; a sociologist cannot rerun a society.
2. **It is empirical.** Claims are tested against evidence from observation, surveys, interviews, census data and documents (Unit 8).
3. **It is theoretical.** Facts alone do not make a science; sociology builds concepts and theories (role, status, norm, class) to explain them.
4. **It is cumulative.** New theories are built on, corrected by, or replace older ones.
5. **It is value-neutral (objective) in aim.** Weber asked sociologists to separate *what is* from *what ought to be*. The researcher's personal values should not decide the findings, although values may well decide *which problem* is chosen.
6. **It is general rather than special.** Economics studies the economy and political science the state; sociology studies social life in general, including the relations between economy, state, family and religion.
7. **It is both pure and applied.** Pure sociology builds theory; applied sociology (policy, community development, user research, health and education programmes) uses it.

### Is sociology a science?

Yes in the sense of *method*: systematic observation, classification, hypotheses, verification, and openness to correction. Durkheim's study *Suicide* (1897) is the classic case. He did not ask why this man took his life; he compared **suicide rates** across countries, religions and family situations and showed that rates differ by degree of social integration (egoistic, altruistic, anomic and fatalistic suicide). Where sociology is *not* like physics is prediction and control: human beings can read the prediction and act differently.

::: caution Common exam mistake
Do not write that sociology is "the study of individuals". It studies **groups, relationships and structures**, and treats individual behaviour only as shaped by them.
:::

## 1.3 Three sociological perspectives

A **perspective** (or paradigm) is a lens: a set of assumptions about what society is and what matters in it. Three are standard.

**Functionalism** (Comte, Spencer, Durkheim, later Talcott Parsons and Robert Merton) treats society as a *system of interdependent parts*, like a body. Each institution (family, school, economy, state, religion) performs **functions** that keep the whole stable. Merton distinguished **manifest functions** (intended and recognised: a school teaches skills) from **latent functions** (unintended: a school also provides childcare and builds friendships) and **dysfunctions** (harmful effects). Strength: explains order and the need for shared values. Weakness: tends to justify the status quo and to underplay conflict.

**Conflict theory** (Karl Marx, later Ralf Dahrendorf, C. Wright Mills) sees society as an *arena of competition* over scarce resources: wealth, power and prestige. Marx saw history as the struggle between classes (owners of the means of production and workers). Modern conflict theory also covers caste, ethnicity and gender. Strength: explains inequality and change. Weakness: can overlook cooperation and shared norms.

**Symbolic interactionism** (George Herbert Mead, Charles Cooley, Herbert Blumer, Erving Goffman) is a *micro* view: society is built from everyday interaction through shared **symbols** (words, gestures, emojis, titles) to which people give meaning. Cooley's **looking-glass self** says we form our self-image from how we imagine others see us. Strength: explains meaning and identity. Weakness: says little about large structures like the economy.

| | Functionalism | Conflict theory | Symbolic interactionism |
|---|---|---|---|
| Level | Macro | Macro | Micro |
| Society is | A system of parts serving the whole | A struggle among groups | Created in everyday interaction |
| Key question | What function does this serve? | Who gains and who loses? | What does this mean to the people involved? |
| Key thinkers | Durkheim, Parsons, Merton | Marx, Dahrendorf, Mills | Mead, Cooley, Blumer, Goffman |
| Example: social media | Connects people, spreads information | Platforms profit, data owners gain power | Likes, profile pictures and posts as symbols of identity |

## 1.4 Scope of sociology

*Scope* means the area that sociology covers. Two classical schools disagreed about how wide it should be.

- **Formal (specialist) school:** Georg Simmel, Ferdinand Toennies, Max Weber, Leopold von Wiese. Sociology should be a *pure, specific* science that studies the **forms** of social relationships (cooperation, competition, conflict, domination, subordination) regardless of their content (economic, political, religious). Criticism: the forms cannot be separated from content, and the scope becomes too narrow.
- **Synthetic school:** Durkheim, L. T. Hobhouse, Pitirim Sorokin. Sociology should be a *general* science that **synthesises** findings of all social sciences into a view of society as a whole. Criticism: it risks becoming a vague collection of everything.

In practice, modern sociology covers both: the forms of relationships and the major institutions. Its main branches include:

| Branch | Typical questions |
|---|---|
| Sociology of the family | Joint and nuclear families, marriage, migration and remittance households |
| Sociology of religion | Festivals, rituals, secularisation, religious diversity |
| Sociology of education | Access, dropout, language of instruction, private versus public schools |
| Economic sociology | Markets, labour, informal work, foreign employment |
| Political sociology | Power, parties, movements, federalism and identity politics |
| Criminology and deviance | Cybercrime, social control, punishment |
| Rural and urban sociology | Urbanisation of the Kathmandu valley, out-migration from hills |
| Gender and development | Women's participation in IT, inheritance, safety |
| **Digital sociology** | Social media, online communities, algorithms, digital divide (Units 4 to 7) |

**Micro and macro.** *Microsociology* studies small-scale interaction (a conversation, a team meeting, a classroom). *Macrosociology* studies large structures and long processes (the class system, urbanisation, globalisation). Good work moves between the two.

## 1.5 Relationship of sociology with other social sciences

The social sciences study aspects of one human reality, so they overlap and borrow from each other. The difference is the **angle**.

| Science | Main focus | Link to sociology |
|---|---|---|
| **Anthropology** | Culture, evolution and small-scale societies; fieldwork (ethnography) | Sociology uses its concepts of culture and kinship; the two merge in *social anthropology*. Traditionally anthropology studied "simple" societies and sociology "complex" ones. |
| **Psychology** | Individual mind and behaviour | *Social psychology* is the bridge: how groups influence the individual. Sociology starts from the group, psychology from the individual. |
| **Economics** | Production, distribution, consumption; scarcity and choice | Economic behaviour takes place inside social norms and institutions (trust, caste networks, remittances). Economic sociology studies this. |
| **Political science** | State, government, power, policy | Political behaviour is shaped by class, ethnicity, religion and the media. Political sociology studies the social bases of power. |
| **History** | Past events in time order | Sociology explains patterns using historical data; history uses sociological concepts (class, institution). Historical sociology joins them. |
| **Geography** | Place, environment and population | Population size, distribution, migration and settlement are shared concerns (Unit 2). |
| **Social work** | Practical help to individuals, groups and communities | Applies sociological knowledge in community practice. |
| **Computer science** | Computation, data and systems | *Computational social science*, social network analysis and human-computer interaction use data and models to study society; sociology tells computing what the users' social world is. |

::: note In one line
Sociology is the *general science of social relationships*; the other social sciences are *special* sciences of one part of social life. Sociology draws from them and gives them a social framework.
:::

## 1.6 Why sociology matters to computer professionals

1. **Users are social beings.** Requirements come from roles, norms and institutions, not from individuals alone. A school management system must fit how a Nepali school really works (class teachers, parents' meetings, results day), not how a textbook says it works.
2. **Technology changes society.** Messaging, e-commerce, ride-sharing and social media change work, friendship, privacy and politics; professionals should foresee consequences (Unit 4).
3. **Responsibility.** Software decisions, such as who is shown a loan offer or a job advert, can reproduce social inequality. Sociology provides the vocabulary of class, gender, caste and ethnicity needed to notice this (Unit 7).
4. **Research skills.** Surveys, interviews and data analysis are used in user research, product analytics and the BCA project reports (Unit 8).
5. **Understanding Nepal.** The later units of this course (social structure, change, stratification and integration) describe the society in which you will work.

## Chapter summary

- Sociology, named by Comte in 1838, is the scientific study of society, social relationships, institutions and social behaviour.
- Durkheim (social facts), Weber (social action and meaning), MacIver and Page (social relationships) give the classic definitions; Mills' sociological imagination links personal troubles with public issues.
- Its nature: a social, empirical, theoretical, cumulative, general and (in aim) value-neutral science, both pure and applied.
- Functionalism (order and function), conflict theory (power and inequality) and symbolic interactionism (meaning and interaction) are the three main perspectives.
- The formal school limits sociology to forms of relationships; the synthetic school makes it a general science of society.
- Sociology is closest to anthropology and social psychology, and connects to economics, politics, history, geography and computing.

## Model answers: medium (5 marks) and long (10 marks) questions

**Q1 (5 marks). Define sociology and explain its nature.**

*Definition.* Sociology is the systematic and scientific study of human society, social relationships and institutions. The term was coined by Auguste Comte in 1838 (*socius* + *logos*). Durkheim defined it as the science of social facts and Weber as the interpretive understanding of social action.

*Nature.* (1) It is a *social science*; its findings are tendencies, not exact laws. (2) It is *empirical*, using surveys, interviews, observation and records. (3) It is *theoretical*, building concepts such as role and status. (4) It is *general*: it studies social life as a whole, unlike economics or political science. (5) It is *value-neutral* in aim, separating what is from what ought to be. (6) It is both *pure* and *applied*.

**Q2 (10 marks). Explain the main sociological perspectives. Which would you use to study social media? Justify.**

*Introduction.* A perspective is a theoretical lens. There are three main ones (section 1.3, Figure 1.1).

*Functionalism.* Society is a system of interdependent institutions; each has manifest and latent functions that maintain order (Durkheim, Parsons, Merton). Social media connects families across borders (manifest function), and also lets communities organise relief (latent function); fake news is a dysfunction.

*Conflict theory.* Society is a struggle over resources and power (Marx, Dahrendorf). Platforms own the data and advertising revenue; users and small producers have little bargaining power; access is unequal by income, language and gender.

*Symbolic interactionism.* Society is produced in interaction through shared symbols (Mead, Blumer, Goffman). Likes, profile pictures, emojis and hashtags carry meaning; users present a curated self (Goffman's presentation of self) and judge themselves by others' reactions (Cooley's looking-glass self).

*Choice and justification.* No single lens is complete. For *meaning and identity online* use interactionism; for *platform power and the digital divide* use conflict theory; for *how social media supports social cohesion and what its dysfunctions are* use functionalism. A good study combines them.

**Q3 (5 marks). Explain the relationship between sociology and anthropology, and between sociology and economics.**

*Anthropology.* Both study human society and culture. Anthropology began with small non-literate societies, using long fieldwork (ethnography); sociology studied industrial societies, using surveys and statistics. Today the methods are shared, and social anthropology merges the two.

*Economics.* Economics studies production, exchange and consumption under scarcity; sociology studies the social setting of economic life: norms, trust, class and kin networks. A remittance, for example, is an economic flow and also a family obligation, which economic sociology studies.
