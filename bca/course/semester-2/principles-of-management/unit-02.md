Management ideas did not appear from nowhere: each school of thought answered the problems of its time. Knowing them explains why organisations look and behave as they do. This unit traces the classical, human-relations, behavioural, systems, quantitative and contingency approaches, then studies the environment in which organisations operate, social responsibility and business ethics.

::: key What this unit gives you
- Classical theory: scientific management (Taylor), administrative theory (Fayol), bureaucracy (Weber).
- Human relations (Mayo, the Hawthorne studies) and behavioural science theories.
- Systems, decision, management-science and contingency theories.
- Emerging concepts: diversity, outsourcing, knowledge management, learning organisation.
- The business environment (internal/external; economic, socio-cultural, political, technological), social responsibility and business ethics, with the Nepali context.
:::

## 2.1 Classical theory

Context: the Industrial Revolution — big factories, unskilled workers, a need for efficiency. Classical thinkers looked for the **one best way** to organise work and the organisation.

### Scientific management — F. W. Taylor (1856–1915)

Focus: **efficiency of the individual worker on the shop floor.** Taylor's four principles:

1. Replace rule-of-thumb with a **scientific study** of each task (time and motion study) to find the best way.
2. **Scientifically select, train** and develop workers rather than letting them train themselves.
3. **Cooperate** with workers so that the work is done by the scientific method.
4. Divide work and responsibility almost equally: managers **plan**, workers **execute**.

Techniques: time study, standardised tools and methods, **differential piece-rate** pay (a higher rate for output above the standard), functional foremanship. Example: a worker with a standard of 100 units who produces 120 earns the higher rate of Rs 6 per unit = **Rs 720** instead of Rs 5 × 120 = Rs 600. *Others:* Frank and Lillian Gilbreth (motion study, "therbligs"), Henry Gantt (Gantt chart, task and bonus system).
*Limitations:* treated people as economic machines; ignored social needs; led to monotony, union opposition and exploitation.

### Administrative theory — Henri Fayol (1841–1925)

Focus: **management of the whole organisation from the top.** Fayol identified the five elements of management — planning, organising, commanding, coordinating, controlling — and fourteen principles (Unit 1), and six groups of activities in a business: technical, commercial, financial, security, accounting, managerial. *Limitations:* principles are too general and sometimes contradict (unity of command versus specialisation); ignored the informal organisation. *Also:* Mooney and Reiley, Gulick and Urwick (POSDCORB: Planning, Organising, Staffing, Directing, Coordinating, Reporting, Budgeting).

### Bureaucracy — Max Weber (1864–1920)

An **ideal bureaucracy** is a rational, rule-based organisation with: a clear **division of labour**; a **hierarchy of authority**; formal **rules and procedures**; **impersonality** (no favourites); **employment on merit** and technical qualification; **career orientation**; written records. Strengths: predictability, fairness, efficiency in stable, routine tasks (government offices, armies). Weaknesses ("red tape"): rigid, slow to change, impersonal, can displace goals (Unit 1). Weber also separated three kinds of legitimate authority: **traditional**, **charismatic** and **legal-rational**.

## 2.2 Human relations and behavioural science theories

### Human relations movement — Elton Mayo

**The Hawthorne studies** (Western Electric, Chicago, 1924–32): researchers varied lighting for workers and found that **output rose whether light increased or decreased**. Later phases (relay assembly test room, interviewing programme, bank wiring room) showed that:

- Workers respond to **attention and being studied** (the "Hawthorne effect").
- **Informal groups** with their own norms set output limits — more than pay did.
- **Social needs and supportive supervision** affect productivity.
Conclusion: people are **social beings**, not just economic ones; good human relations raise productivity. Criticism: weak research method, over-simple "a happy worker is a productive worker".

### Behavioural science theories
Later researchers applied psychology and sociology rigorously:

- **Abraham Maslow** — hierarchy of needs (Unit 5).
- **Douglas McGregor** — **Theory X** (people dislike work and need control) versus **Theory Y** (people can be self-directed and seek responsibility); managers' assumptions shape their style.
- **Frederick Herzberg** — motivation–hygiene theory (Unit 5).
- **Chris Argyris** — organisations should let immature individuals grow into mature, responsible ones.
- **Rensis Likert** — participative "System 4" management.
- **William Ouchi** — Theory Z (Japanese-style lifetime employment, consensus decisions).
Contribution: motivation, leadership, groups, communication and organisational behaviour as fields. Limitation: results are hard to generalise; individuals and cultures differ.

## 2.3 System, decision, management science and contingency theories

### System theory
An organisation is an **open system**: **inputs** (people, money, materials, information) → **transformation process** → **outputs** (goods, services, profit) with **feedback** from the **environment**. Key ideas: **interdependence** of subsystems (a delay in purchasing stops production), **synergy** (the whole is greater than the sum of its parts), **entropy** (decline if the system closes itself off), **equifinality** (many routes to the same goal). Contribution: a holistic view. Limitation: abstract, hard to apply directly.

### Decision theory
Management is basically **decision making** (Herbert Simon): managers choose among alternatives, usually with **bounded rationality** — limited information and time, so they **satisfice** (choose the first acceptable option) rather than optimise. Contribution: models of rational choice and its limits (Unit 3).

### Management science (quantitative) theory
Born in World War II **operations research**: mathematical models, statistics, simulation and computers for decisions on inventory, scheduling, transport and project control (linear programming, PERT/CPM, queuing, break-even, decision trees). Contribution: precision in complex problems; basis of today's analytics. Limitation: ignores human factors; models need good data.

### Contingency theory
There is **no one best way**: the right approach **depends** on the situation (size, technology, environment, people). *If–then*: **if** the environment is stable, **then** a mechanistic structure works; **if** it is turbulent, an organic one does. Contributors: Joan Woodward (technology and structure), Burns and Stalker (mechanistic/organic), Lawrence and Lorsch (differentiation and integration), Fiedler (leadership). Contribution: realistic and flexible. Limitation: no single guiding theory, hard to specify all the variables.

| School | Period | Focus | Key names | View of people |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Classical | 1900–30 | efficiency, structure | Taylor, Fayol, Weber | economic machines |
| Human relations | 1930–50 | social needs, groups | Mayo | social beings |
| Behavioural science | 1950–70 | motivation, leadership | Maslow, McGregor, Herzberg | individuals seeking growth |
| Management science | 1940s– | quantitative tools | operations research | rational decision-makers |
| Systems | 1960s– | interdependence | Katz & Kahn, von Bertalanffy | parts of a system |
| Contingency | 1970s– | fit with the situation | Woodward, Fiedler | it depends |

### Emerging management concepts

- **Workforce diversity**: managing differences of gender, age, ethnicity, language, ability, caste and region; benefits are creativity and wider markets, challenges are conflict and bias.
- **Outsourcing**: contracting out activities (IT support, payroll, call centres) to focus on core competencies and cut cost; risks are loss of control, quality and data security. Nepal's IT-outsourcing sector serves foreign clients.
- **Knowledge management**: capturing, sharing and using an organisation's knowledge (documentation, communities of practice, wikis) so it does not leave with employees.
- **Learning organisation** (Peter Senge): continuously learns and adapts, through *personal mastery, mental models, shared vision, team learning and systems thinking*.
- Also: total quality management, re-engineering, empowerment, agility, digital transformation.

## 2.4 The business environment

The **business environment** is the sum of all external and internal forces that affect an organisation's performance. Scanning it lets managers see opportunities and threats.

### Internal environment
**Owners and board**, **employees**, **organisational culture**, **structure**, **resources** (finance, technology, facilities), **management style**. Controllable, to a degree.

### External environment

| Level | Elements | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Task (specific) environment** | customers, suppliers, competitors, regulators, creditors, labour unions, pressure groups | a bank's depositors and the Nepal Rastra Bank |
| **General (macro) environment** | forces that affect all organisations — the **PEST/PESTLE** factors: |

| Component | Basic factors | Nepal examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Economic** | GDP growth, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, income, employment, remittances, trade policy | dependence on remittances; INR peg; high interest-rate swings |
| **Socio-cultural** | demographics, values, education, lifestyle, attitudes to work, religion, language | young, migrating population; festivals drive demand; Nepali–English bilingualism |
| **Political-legal** | stability, government policy, laws (company, labour, tax, consumer, IT), regulation | the Companies Act, Electronic Transactions Act; changes of government |
| **Technological** | innovation, automation, internet, mobile payments, R&D | eSewa/Khalti, mobile internet, AI tools |
| **Also:** international, natural (earthquakes, climate), legal | | 2015 earthquake; monsoon floods |

### Emerging business environment in Nepal
Federal structure with three levels of government; liberalised trade and investment; growth in **hydropower, tourism, ICT services, remittance-funded consumption and digital finance**; the push for e-governance (digital Nepal framework); concerns over **infrastructure, energy, bureaucracy, political instability, brain drain** and a large trade deficit. An environmental scan keeps managers' plans realistic.

## 2.5 Social responsibility and business ethics

**Social responsibility (CSR)** is an organisation's obligation to act in ways that benefit society as well as itself.

| View | Argument |
|---|---|
| **Classical (Milton Friedman)** | the only responsibility of business is to maximise profit within the law; spending on social causes uses shareholders' money |
| **Socio-economic** | business is a member of society; being responsible improves its long-term interest, image and survival |

**Carroll's pyramid** — economic (be profitable), legal (obey laws), ethical (do what is right and fair), philanthropic (be a good citizen — donations, community projects).

**Approaches (stances)**: **obstructionist** (resist and hide), **defensive** (do the legal minimum), **accommodative** (do it when asked), **proactive** (seek ways to contribute).

**Areas of social responsibility**: customers (safe products, honest advertising); employees (fair pay, safety, no discrimination); owners/investors (honest reporting); suppliers; competitors (fair play); community and the environment (waste, pollution, sustainability); government (taxes, laws). *Arguments for:* public image, long-term profit, avoiding regulation, attracting talent. *Against:* cost, dilution of focus, lack of accountability.

### Business ethics

**Ethics** are the moral principles that distinguish right from wrong. **Business ethics** applies them to business decisions. Significance: builds **trust** with customers and employees, avoids legal penalties and scandal, supports a healthy culture, and sustains long-term success. Ethical issues: bribery and corruption, conflicts of interest, false advertising, adulteration, tax evasion, insider trading, employee exploitation, data privacy, plagiarism.

Ethical decision tests: **Utilitarian** (the greatest good for the greatest number), **rights** (does it respect people's rights?), **justice** (is it fair?), and the practical "**newspaper test**" (would I be comfortable seeing this on the front page?). Organisations encourage ethics through a **code of ethics**, ethics training, whistle-blower protection, ethics committees and **leading by example**.

## Formula and fact sheet

- Scientific management (Taylor): one best way, time study, piece-rate; classical administration (Fayol): principles; bureaucracy (Weber): rules, hierarchy, merit, impersonality.
- Hawthorne: social factors and informal groups drive output. Theory X vs Theory Y.
- Systems: input → process → output with feedback; contingency: it depends.
- Environment: internal; task (customers, suppliers, competitors); general (economic, socio-cultural, political-legal, technological).
- Carroll's pyramid: economic, legal, ethical, philanthropic. CSR stances: obstructionist, defensive, accommodative, proactive.
- Taylor example: 120 units × Rs 6 = Rs 720 versus 120 × Rs 5 = Rs 600.

## Chapter summary

- Classical thinkers (Taylor, Fayol, Weber) sought efficiency through method, principles and rules; the Hawthorne studies and behavioural scientists discovered people's social and psychological needs.
- Systems, decision, management-science and contingency theories add holistic, rational, quantitative and situational views — no single best way.
- Diversity, outsourcing, knowledge management and learning organisations are current concepts.
- Organisations operate in internal, task and general environments; scanning economic, socio-cultural, political and technological factors guides decisions, including in Nepal's changing context.
- Social responsibility and business ethics are now part of managerial duty.
