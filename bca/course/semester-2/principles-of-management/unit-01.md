Every software team, college, hospital and shop is an **organisation**, and every organisation needs someone to coordinate effort towards its goals. That coordinating work is **management**. This unit defines organisations and their goals, then management itself: its characteristics, principles, functions, levels, skills and roles.

## 1.1 The nature of organisations

An **organisation** is a **deliberately structured social unit of two or more people who work together on a continuing basis to achieve common goals.** Four features: (1) **people**, (2) **common goals**, (3) **structure** (roles and relationships), (4) **a boundary** that separates members from outsiders. Examples: a company, a campus, a government office, an NGO, a football club.

| Type | Purpose | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Business (profit)** | earn profit by selling goods and services | Daraz, a software company, a bank |
| **Government / public** | public service and regulation | Tribhuvan University, Nepal Electricity Authority |
| **Non-profit / NGO** | social or charitable aims | a community library, a Red Cross unit |
| **Mutual benefit** | serve members | a cooperative, a trade union |

Organisations take **inputs** (people, money, materials, information), **transform** them, and send out **outputs** (products, services) to an environment from which they receive feedback — an **open system**.

### Organisational goals

A **goal** is a desired future state the organisation tries to reach. Goals give **direction**, a **standard** for judging performance, **motivation**, **legitimacy** (justifying existence) and a basis for **coordination**.

| Type of goal | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Official goals** | what the organisation says it exists for (mission statement) | "Provide quality computing education" |
| **Operative goals** | what it actually pursues, in daily decisions | graduating more students; raising fee income |
| **Operational goals** | specific, measurable targets | "Raise pass rate to 85 % by 2083" |
| **Strategic / tactical / operational** | by level of management and time | 5-year / 1-year / monthly |
| **Short-, medium-, long-term** | by time | |

**Features of effective goals** (the **SMART** test): **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**ealistic/relevant, **T**ime-bound; also **consistent** with each other, **understood and accepted** by those who carry them out, **challenging**, and **flexible**.

**Goal formulation** — the process: scan the environment, analyse the organisation's strengths, consult stakeholders (owners, managers, employees, customers, government), draft goals, check feasibility, approve and communicate them. **Approaches**: *top-down* (management sets them), *bottom-up* (units propose), and *participative* (negotiated — as in management by objectives, Unit 3).

**Goal succession and displacement.** *Succession*: when the original goal is achieved or impossible, a **new goal replaces it** so the organisation survives (the March of Dimes, formed to fight polio, turned to birth defects after the vaccine). *Displacement*: the **means become the ends** — following rules, procedures or budgets becomes more important than the purpose (a clerk insists on the stamp even when the service is failed; a sales team inflates numbers to hit the target).

**Problems of goal formulation**: conflicting goals of stakeholders; vague goals; too many goals; short-term pressure beating long-term aims; goals that cannot be measured; changing environment; goals imposed without commitment.

### Changing perspectives on organisation
Early views saw organisations as **machines** (efficiency, rules); then as **communities of people** (needs, relationships); then as **open systems** that adapt to the environment, **learning organisations** and **networks**. Today they are expected to serve **multiple stakeholders** and society, not shareholders alone.

## 1.2 What is management?

Several definitions, all with the same core:

- *Mary Parker Follett*: "the art of getting things done through people."
- *Stoner*: "the process of planning, organising, leading and controlling the efforts of organisation members and of using all other organisational resources to achieve stated organisational goals."
- *Koontz and O'Donnell*: "creating and maintaining an environment in which individuals work together in groups to accomplish selected aims."

In plain words: **management is working with and through other people to achieve organisational goals efficiently and effectively.**

- **Efficiency** — doing things *right*: getting the most output from the least input (resources, time, money).
- **Effectiveness** — doing the *right* things: achieving the goal.
A manager aims at both; efficiently producing a product nobody wants is efficient but ineffective.

### Characteristics of management

1. **Goal-oriented** — it exists to achieve objectives.
2. **Universal** — needed in every organisation and every level (a shop, a hospital, a project team).
3. **A social process** — about dealing with people.
4. **Multidimensional** — managing work, people and operations.
5. **Continuous** — an ongoing cycle, not a one-off act.
6. **Dynamic** — adapts to change.
7. **Group activity** — it coordinates the efforts of many.
8. **Both an art and a science** — a science because it has organised, tested knowledge; an art because applying it requires personal skill and judgement; and a **profession** to the extent that it has a body of knowledge, training and ethics, though no licence is required.
9. **Intangible** — seen in its results, not seen itself.

### Principles of management

A **principle** is a fundamental truth that guides decisions and behaviour — flexible guidelines, not rigid laws. **Henri Fayol's fourteen principles**:

| # | Principle | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Division of work** | specialisation raises efficiency |
| 2 | **Authority and responsibility** | the right to give orders goes with the duty to answer for results |
| 3 | **Discipline** | obedience and respect for agreements |
| 4 | **Unity of command** | one boss for each employee |
| 5 | **Unity of direction** | one plan and one head for each group of activities with the same objective |
| 6 | **Subordination of individual interest to general interest** | the organisation's interest comes first |
| 7 | **Remuneration** | fair pay for both employees and the organisation |
| 8 | **Centralisation** | the right balance between central and delegated authority |
| 9 | **Scalar chain** | the line of authority from top to bottom (with a "gang plank" for direct cross-links in emergencies) |
| 10 | **Order** | the right person and material in the right place at the right time |
| 11 | **Equity** | kindness and justice to staff |
| 12 | **Stability of tenure** | avoid unnecessary turnover |
| 13 | **Initiative** | allow employees to think and act |
| 14 | **Esprit de corps** | "union is strength": team spirit |

## 1.3 The management process and functions

Management is a **process**: a series of continuing, interrelated activities. The four classical **functions** (Fayol's five reduce to these four by merging command into leading):

| Function | Question it answers | Key activities |
|---|---|---|
| **Planning** | *Where do we want to go and how do we get there?* | set goals, forecast, choose strategies, make plans and schedules |
| **Organising** | *Who does what, and who reports to whom?* | design structure, divide work, assign authority, staff, coordinate |
| **Leading** (directing) | *How do we get people to perform?* | motivate, communicate, lead, resolve conflict |
| **Controlling** | *Are we getting there?* | set standards, measure, compare, take corrective action |

```
   Planning ──► Organising ──► Leading ──► Controlling
       ▲                                       │
       └─────────── feedback and revision ─────┘
```
The functions overlap and loop: control results feed new planning. Staffing (recruiting, training, appraising) is sometimes listed separately but is treated here as part of organising (Unit 4).

## 1.4 Managerial hierarchy and types of managers

### Levels of management

| Level | Titles | Time horizon | Main concern | Skills most needed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Top** (strategic) | board, CEO, managing director | long (3–5+ years) | mission, strategy, environment, overall policy | **conceptual** |
| **Middle** (tactical) | department heads, branch managers | medium (months to a year) | convert strategy into departmental plans, coordinate | conceptual + human |
| **First-line / supervisory** (operational) | supervisors, team leaders, foremen | short (day to week) | day-to-day operations, supervising non-managers | **technical** + human |

Below them are **operatives** (non-managerial employees). The shape is a pyramid: few at the top, many at the base.

### Types of managers

- **By scope**: **functional managers** (one area: marketing, finance, production, HR, IT) and **general managers** (responsible for a whole unit with all functions).
- **By role**: **line managers** (directly responsible for the core output) and **staff managers** (advisers and support: HR, legal, finance).
- **By level**: top, middle, first-line (above).
- **Project managers** for temporary projects; **team leaders** in flat organisations.

## 1.5 Managerial skills and roles

### Katz's three skills

| Skill | Meaning | Where it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Technical** | knowing the methods, tools and techniques of a specialised field (coding, accounting) | most at the lowest level |
| **Human (interpersonal)** | working with, understanding and motivating people; communication, teamwork | equally important at **all** levels |
| **Conceptual** | seeing the organisation as a whole, thinking abstractly, diagnosing problems, planning strategy | most at the top |

Some add **design** (solving problems creatively) and **political** skills.

### Mintzberg's ten managerial roles (1973)

| Category | Roles | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Interpersonal** | figurehead, leader, liaison | attending a graduation; motivating a team; networking with other departments |
| **Informational** | monitor, disseminator, spokesperson | reading reports; passing information to staff; speaking to the press |
| **Decisional** | entrepreneur, disturbance handler, resource allocator, negotiator | launching a new project; resolving a strike; allocating budget; negotiating a contract |

Mintzberg found that real managerial work is **fragmented and fast**: short bursts, many interruptions, verbal contacts — unlike the tidy picture of the four functions.

## 1.6 Emerging challenges for management

- **Globalisation** — competition and partners across borders; remote and outsourced teams.
- **Technology and digital disruption** — automation, AI, e-commerce, data; the need for continuous skill updating.
- **Workforce diversity** — gender, age, ethnicity, language, ability; inclusion.
- **Ethics and social responsibility** — honesty, sustainability, corporate governance, anti-corruption.
- **Quality and productivity** — customer expectations and cost pressure.
- **Knowledge workers and talent retention** — brain drain; in Nepal, the loss of skilled youth abroad.
- **Change and uncertainty** — pandemics, political and economic shocks, fast-moving markets.
- **Work–life balance, well-being and remote work.**

## Formula and fact sheet

- Management = getting things done through people to reach goals; efficiency = right way; effectiveness = right goal.
- Functions: planning, organising, leading, controlling. Fayol's principles: 14.
- Goals: SMART; official vs operative vs operational; succession (new goal) vs displacement (means become ends).
- Levels: top (conceptual), middle, first-line (technical); human skill needed at all levels.
- Mintzberg: 3 categories — interpersonal (3), informational (3), decisional (4) = 10 roles.

## Chapter summary

- An organisation is a structured group pursuing common goals; effective goals are SMART, accepted and consistent; goals can be displaced or succeeded.
- Management is the process of planning, organising, leading and controlling to achieve goals efficiently and effectively; it is universal, goal-directed and both an art and a science.
- Fayol's fourteen principles guide structure and behaviour as flexible rules.
- There are three levels of managers needing different mixes of technical, human and conceptual skills; Mintzberg describes ten roles.
- Globalisation, technology, diversity, ethics and change are the main challenges.
