## 6.1 Concept and process of control

**Controlling** is the function of **monitoring performance, comparing it with standards, and taking corrective action** so that actual results match plans. It closes the management loop: planning sets standards, control checks them. **Purposes**: keep plans on track; detect deviations early; cut waste; coordinate; maintain quality; protect assets; provide data for the next plan.

### The control process (four steps)

```
1. Set standards  →  2. Measure actual performance  →  3. Compare with standards (find deviation)  →  4. Take corrective action
        ▲                                                                                                │
        └──────────────────────────── revise standards if they were unrealistic ◄─────────────────────────┘
```

1. **Establish standards** — measurable targets derived from the plan: quantity, quality, cost, time (e.g. 98 % uptime, ≤ 3 % defects, project within Rs 5 lakh).
2. **Measure performance** — observation, reports, statistics, audits, sampling; measure at the right frequency and place.
3. **Compare** actual with standard; decide whether the **deviation** is acceptable (use a tolerance **range** — *management by exception* focuses attention only on significant deviations).
4. **Take corrective action** — fix the cause (retrain, repair, re-plan), or **revise the standard** if it was unrealistic; or do nothing if within tolerance.

::: example Worked example: variance analysis
Budget for a month: Rs 1,20,000; actual spending: Rs 1,35,000. **Variance = 1,35,000 − 1,20,000 = Rs 15,000 adverse**, i.e. 15,000/1,20,000 = **12.5 % over budget**. Investigate the cause (overtime? price rise? waste?), then act (control overtime, renegotiate with suppliers) or revise the budget if the cause is permanent.
:::

## 6.2 Types of control

| Type | When it acts | Aim | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Feedforward (preliminary, steering)** | **before** the activity | prevents problems by controlling inputs | screening job applicants, inspecting raw materials, pre-flight checks, design reviews |
| **Concurrent (real-time, yes/no)** | **during** the activity | corrects as the work proceeds | supervision, a software test run in CI, automatic temperature control, a bank's real-time fraud alert |
| **Feedback (post-action)** | **after** the activity | learns from results to improve next time | final inspection, financial statements, exam results, customer surveys |

Also: **strategic**, **tactical** and **operational** control by level; **financial**, **operational**, **behavioural** and **quality** controls; **bureaucratic** (rules, procedures) vs **clan** (culture and shared values) control; **preventive** vs **corrective**. The best systems use all three timings; feedforward is cheapest, feedback is the most common but acts too late.

## 6.3 Characteristics of an effective control system

**Suitable** (fits the activity and level), **timely** (information when action is still possible), **accurate**, **economical** (the benefit exceeds the cost), **flexible**, **understandable**, **objective**, **focused on critical points and exceptions**, **forward-looking**, **acceptable to people** (not seen as spying; participative), **action-oriented** (points to who must do what), **integrated** with planning.

Why people resist control: fear of blame, feeling of mistrust, loss of autonomy, goal displacement (gaming the measure), cost. **Overcome** by participation, clear and reasonable standards, rewarding results, and feedback that helps rather than punishes.

## 6.4 Control tools and techniques

| Tool | Use |
|---|---|
| **Budgets** | financial plans and controls: **operating** (sales, production, cost), **cash**, **capital**, **flexible** (adjust with activity level), **zero-based** (every item re-justified); compared with actuals (variance) |
| **Financial statements and ratios** | **liquidity** (current ratio), **profitability** (net profit margin, **ROI** = profit ÷ investment: 30,000 ÷ 2,00,000 = **15 %**), **leverage**, **activity** (inventory turnover) |
| **Break-even analysis** | the volume at which revenue covers cost (Unit 3) |
| **Audits** | **financial** (external, verifies statements), **internal** (efficiency and compliance), **management/operational** (effectiveness) |
| **Gantt charts and PERT/CPM** | schedule control; the critical path shows where delay hurts (Unit 3) |
| **Statistical quality control**: **control charts** | plot samples over time with a centre line and **upper/lower control limits** (mean ± 3 standard deviations: for mean 50, SD 2 the limits are **44 and 56**); a point outside, or a run in one direction, signals an out-of-control process |
| **Inventory control** | **EOQ**, **ABC analysis** (A: few items of high value, tightly controlled; C: many items of low value), **just-in-time (JIT)**, reorder levels |
| **MIS and dashboards** | key performance indicators (KPIs) in real time; **balanced scorecard** (financial, customer, internal process, learning and growth) |
| **Management by objectives, performance appraisal** | control of people's performance |
| **Observation, reports, MBWA** (management by walking around) | personal control |

## 6.5 Quality

**Quality** is the **degree to which a product or service meets or exceeds customer expectations** — "fitness for use" (Juran), "conformance to requirements" (Crosby). **Dimensions** (Garvin): performance, features, reliability, conformance, durability, serviceability, aesthetics, perceived quality. For software: functionality, reliability, usability, efficiency, maintainability, portability. **Importance**: customer satisfaction and loyalty, lower cost of defects and rework, competitiveness, reputation, regulatory compliance.

**Cost of quality** = *prevention* + *appraisal* + *internal failure* + *external failure* (the last is most expensive).

### Quality control and assurance
**Quality control (QC)** inspects products and fixes defects (reactive — *detect*); **quality assurance (QA)** designs processes so that defects do not arise (proactive — *prevent*); **TQM** makes quality everybody's responsibility. Inspection methods: 100 % inspection vs **acceptance sampling**; **statistical process control**.

## 6.6 Total Quality Management (TQM)

**TQM** is a **management philosophy of continuously improving the quality of all processes and products by involving everyone in the organisation, with a focus on the customer.**

### Principles and components

1. **Customer focus** — the customer defines quality (external and internal customers).
2. **Total employee involvement** and **empowerment** — teams, quality circles, training.
3. **Process-centred** — improve the process, not blame the person.
4. **Continuous improvement** (Kaizen).
5. **Leadership commitment** from the top.
6. **Fact-based decision making** with data.
7. **Supplier partnership.**
8. **Integrated system / prevention over detection**; benchmarking.

### TQM tools — the "seven basic quality tools" (Ishikawa)
**Check sheet** (collect data), **histogram** (distribution), **Pareto chart** (the vital few: if 2 of 10 causes explain 15 of 25 complaints = **60 %**, fix them first), **cause-and-effect (fishbone) diagram**, **scatter diagram** (relationship between two variables), **control chart**, **flowchart** (process map). Management tools include **benchmarking**, **quality function deployment (QFD)**, **FMEA**, **brainstorming**, **affinity diagrams**.

### Deming's contribution
W. Edwards Deming (who taught the Japanese after WWII; Japan's Deming Prize is named for him) argued that **85 % of problems are caused by the system (management), not by workers.** The **PDCA (Deming) cycle**:

**Plan** (identify the problem and design a change) → **Do** (try it on a small scale) → **Check** (measure the results) → **Act** (adopt, or adjust and repeat).

**Deming's 14 points** (summary): 1 create constancy of purpose; 2 adopt the new philosophy; 3 cease dependence on mass inspection; 4 end awarding business on price alone; 5 improve constantly and forever; 6 institute training on the job; 7 institute leadership; 8 drive out fear; 9 break down barriers between departments; 10 eliminate slogans and targets for the workforce; 11 eliminate numerical quotas; 12 remove barriers to pride of workmanship; 13 institute education and self-improvement; 14 put everyone to work on the transformation. Also the "seven deadly diseases" (lack of constancy of purpose, emphasis on short-term profits, performance appraisal, mobility of management, using visible figures only, excessive medical costs, excessive legal costs). *Other gurus*: Juran (quality trilogy: planning, control, improvement), Crosby ("zero defects", "quality is free"), Feigenbaum (total quality control), Ishikawa (quality circles).

## 6.7 Kaizen, Six Sigma and 5S

### Kaizen
Japanese for "change for the better": **continuous, incremental improvement by everyone, every day**, in small steps rather than big innovations. Features: involvement of all employees, standardisation then improvement, suggestion systems, "Gemba" (go to the place where work is done), elimination of **muda** (waste: overproduction, waiting, transport, over-processing, inventory, motion, defects). Used in Toyota's production system and lean management.

### Six Sigma
A **data-driven methodology (Motorola, 1980s; popularised by GE)** to cut defects and variation, aiming at **3.4 defects per million opportunities (DPMO)** — about 99.99966 % yield (with the conventional 1.5σ shift). Process: **DMAIC** — **D**efine, **M**easure, **A**nalyse, **I**mprove, **C**ontrol (for new designs, DMADV). Roles: **Champions**, **Master Black Belts**, **Black Belts**, **Green Belts**.

**DPMO = defects ÷ (units × opportunities per unit) × 1,000,000.**

::: example Worked example: DPMO
In a batch of 500 forms with 4 error opportunities each, 34 defects were found. DPMO = 34 ÷ (500 × 4) × 10⁶ = 34/2000 × 10⁶ = **17,000 DPMO** (a defect rate of 1.7 %, yield 98.3 %) — far from the six-sigma target of 3.4 DPMO, so a DMAIC project would study the error sources (Pareto), fix them and control the process.
:::

### The Japanese 5S practice
A workplace-organisation method with five Japanese words beginning with S:

| 5S | Japanese | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Sort** | Seiri | remove what is unnecessary | clear unused files and tools |
| **Set in order** | Seiton | a place for everything, everything in its place | labelled shelves, folder structure |
| **Shine** | Seiso | clean and inspect | clean workspace and equipment daily |
| **Standardise** | Seiketsu | make the first three a standard | checklists, colour coding |
| **Sustain** | Shitsuke | make it a habit; discipline and audits | regular 5S audits |

5S reduces waste and search time, improves safety and morale, and is the foundation of Kaizen and lean.

### Other modern control and quality ideas
**Just-in-time**, **lean**, **benchmarking**, **ISO 9001** quality management certification, **ISO/IEC 27001** (security), **CMMI** for software process maturity, **balanced scorecard**, and **operations and supply-chain management** — managing the flow of materials and information from suppliers to customers.

## 6.8 The global context of management

**Globalisation** — the growing interconnection of economies through trade, investment, technology and people. Drivers: trade liberalisation (WTO), falling transport and communication costs, the internet, multinational corporations, regional blocs (SAARC, BIMSTEC, the EU).

**Forms of international business**: **exporting/importing**, **licensing and franchising**, **joint ventures and strategic alliances**, **wholly owned subsidiaries**, **outsourcing and offshoring**. A **multinational corporation (MNC)** operates in several countries.

**Managing across cultures**: **Hofstede's dimensions** — power distance, individualism vs collectivism, masculinity vs femininity, uncertainty avoidance, long-term orientation (Nepal scores high on power distance and collectivism). Others: high- vs low-context communication (Hall). Managers need **cultural intelligence**: language, etiquette, holidays, negotiation style, religion, and legal differences.

**Opportunities**: larger markets, cheaper inputs, access to talent, economies of scale, knowledge transfer. **Challenges**: cultural and language differences, exchange-rate and political risk, different laws and ethics, competition, supply-chain disruption, dependence (Nepal's reliance on trade routes through India), brain drain.

**Management in a global age**: **think globally, act locally**; diverse and virtual teams; ethics and sustainability (ESG); digital collaboration; resilience.

## 6.9 Management trends and scenario in Nepal

- **Structure**: a mix of **public enterprises**, a large **private sector** dominated by **small and family-owned businesses**, a growing **cooperative and NGO** sector, and **foreign-investment** firms. Many family firms are shifting towards professional management and governance.
- **Management practice**: strong **hierarchy and personal relationships**; reliance on intuition and informal networks; limited use of formal planning and MIS in small firms but rising in banks, telecom, insurance and IT firms; formal training in management through BBS, BBA, MBA and BCA programmes.
- **Sectors driving change**: **banking and financial services** (Nepal Rastra Bank regulations, digital payments — eSewa, Khalti, IME Pay, mobile banking), **telecom**, **hydropower**, **tourism and hospitality**, **ICT/IT outsourcing**, **agriculture modernisation**, **education**, **remittance-linked businesses**.
- **Trends**: digitisation and e-governance; start-ups and entrepreneurship (incubators, venture funds); **corporate social responsibility** and sustainability (including post-earthquake reconstruction and disaster resilience); stress on quality (ISO, NS standards); **women and youth entrepreneurship**; **federalism** creating new local government administration and procurement needs; growing consumer awareness and consumer-protection law.
- **Challenges**: political instability and policy changes, weak infrastructure and energy, bureaucratic delay and corruption, **brain drain and shortage of skilled managers**, small domestic market and trade deficit, informal economy, weak enforcement of contracts, financial-sector risks, and vulnerability to disasters.
- **What it demands of managers**: ethical leadership, adaptability, professional training of staff, use of technology, community and environmental responsibility, and the ability to compete regionally and globally.
