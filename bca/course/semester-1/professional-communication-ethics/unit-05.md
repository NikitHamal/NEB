## 5.1 Virtual etiquette (netiquette)

**Netiquette** (network etiquette) is the set of accepted rules of polite and sensible behaviour in online communication. Virginia Shea's classic *Core Rules of Netiquette* (1994) begins with "Remember the human": behind every screen is a person who can be hurt, who may read your words at a bad moment, and who cannot see your face.

### Why netiquette matters

Online messages lack tone of voice and body language, so jokes read as insults and short replies as anger. Digital messages are also **permanent** (screenshots, backups), **searchable**, **forwardable** and **public by default**. A careless line can end a job offer, an internship or a friendship.

### Core rules

1. **Remember the human.** Write what you would say face to face to that person.
2. **Be clear and concise.** A meaningful subject line, short paragraphs, the action required and the deadline in the first lines.
3. **Use the right channel.** Quick question: chat. Formal request or record: email. Sensitive or emotional: call or meet. Complex discussion: meeting.
4. **Mind your tone.** Do not write in ALL CAPITALS (shouting); avoid sarcasm; use emojis sparingly and only when the relationship allows; always greet and sign off.
5. **Respond promptly,** and say when you will reply if you need time ("I will answer by Thursday").
6. **Respect privacy.** Do not forward private emails or screenshots without permission; use **Bcc** for large lists so that addresses are not exposed; do not add people to groups without asking.
7. **Do not spam or "reply all"** unless everyone needs the answer.
8. **Proofread** before you send: names, dates, attachments and links. Misspelling a client's name is an avoidable insult.
9. **Share only what is true.** Check facts before posting or forwarding; do not spread rumours or fake news.
10. **Respect copyright and give credit** (see 5.4).
11. **Be forgiving of others' mistakes.** Correct privately and politely; do not mock in public.
12. **Do not use workplace systems for private business** or inappropriate content.

### Email etiquette

- Subject line states the topic and, if needed, the action: *Action by 5 pm Friday: BCA Project I proposal*.
- Salutation: "Dear Mr Sharma" (formal), "Hello Sita" (colleague); closing: "Regards" or "Yours sincerely".
- One topic per email; use bullet points for lists; attach files and mention them in the body.
- Use **To** for those who must act, **Cc** for those who should know, **Bcc** for hidden recipients.
- Add an **out-of-office reply** when away; use a professional signature (name, position, phone).
- Do not email in anger; wait a day for strongly emotional replies.

### Chat and messaging etiquette

Start with the real message ("Hi Ram, the build failed on step 3; log attached"), not just "Hi"; avoid late-night messages unless urgent; use threads; mute channels, not people; and never type confidential data (passwords, citizenship numbers) in chat.

### Video-meeting etiquette

Test audio and camera in advance; join two minutes early; **mute when not speaking**; keep the camera on when bandwidth permits; use a plain, well-lit background; dress as for a face-to-face meeting; look at the camera; do not multitask visibly; use the "raise hand" feature; share the agenda and record minutes; and end on time. For a long power cut or poor connection, say so early and offer to switch to audio.

::: example Worked example: improving a chat message
*Poor:* "hi" ... "r u there" ... "need the file" ... "??".
*Better:* "Hi Sita, could you please send me the latest ER diagram when you have a moment? I need it for the 3 pm review. Thanks!"
The better message greets, states the request, the reason and the deadline in a single message, so Sita can act without a second exchange.
:::

## 5.2 Online professionalism

**Online professionalism** means behaving on the internet in a way that protects your reputation and the reputation of your institution or employer. Employers, universities and clients routinely search names before hiring or admitting; your **digital footprint** (everything you post, like, comment on, and everything others post about you) is a CV you did not write.

### Active and passive digital footprint

- **Active:** posts, comments, uploads, profile fields, reviews, forum answers.
- **Passive:** location data, cookies, browsing history, tags by others, app data collected without your effort.

### Building a professional online presence

1. **Use a professional email address** (name-based, not "cutiepie2060@..."), a clear photograph, and a consistent name across platforms.
2. **Create a LinkedIn profile** (headline, summary, projects, skills, education) and a **GitHub** profile with clean, documented repositories.
3. **Separate personal and professional accounts;** use privacy settings; remember that "private" posts can be screenshotted.
4. **Search yourself** on Google every few months and remove or fix harmful results.
5. **Think before you post:** the "newspaper test" (would I be comfortable seeing this on the front page of a newspaper with my name?).
6. **Be accurate and credible:** do not exaggerate skills, claim fake certificates or share unverified news.
7. **Keep emotions out of public disputes.** Complain through the proper channel, not on a public wall.
8. **Protect confidential information:** never post client data, internal documents, unreleased products, exam papers or screenshots of private chats.
9. **Respect others' consent** before tagging or sharing their photographs.
10. **Secure your accounts:** strong unique passwords, a password manager, two-factor authentication, and care with public Wi-Fi.

### Professionalism in written style online

Use correct grammar and spelling, avoid slang and text-speak in professional contexts, write complete sentences, keep a respectful tone even when you disagree, and acknowledge others' contributions.

### Cyberbullying, trolling and harassment

**Cyberbullying** is the use of digital media to hurt, humiliate or threaten a person repeatedly. **Trolling** is posting provocative or insulting comments to cause reaction. Respond by not engaging, **saving evidence** (screenshots, links, dates), blocking and reporting to the platform, informing a teacher or supervisor, and, where there are threats or private images, reporting to the **Nepal Police Cyber Bureau**. Do not take part in pile-ons; sharing is also harming.

::: key Quick checks before you post
- Is it true? Is it kind? Is it necessary? Would I say it to this person's face?
- Does it reveal someone's private information or mine?
- Could it be misread in another culture or by a future employer?
- Do I have the right to use this image, text or code?
:::

## 5.3 Communicating via social media and collaborative tools

### Social media

**Social media** are web and mobile platforms that let users create and share content and interact in networks. In the workplace they serve marketing, recruitment, customer service, public relations, learning and community building.

| Platform type | Examples | Professional use | Style |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Professional network** | LinkedIn | job search, recruiting, industry news | formal, achievement-oriented |
| **General social network** | Facebook, Viber/WhatsApp groups | community pages, customer support, events | friendly, local language and English |
| **Microblog** | X (Twitter), Threads | news, quick updates, thought leadership | short, direct |
| **Visual** | Instagram, Pinterest, TikTok, YouTube | brand stories, demos, tutorials | visual, informal |
| **Q and A / forums** | Stack Overflow, Reddit, Quora | help, knowledge sharing | precise, evidence-based |
| **Code and knowledge** | GitHub, Medium, dev.to | portfolio, documentation | technical, clear |

**Benefits:** wide reach at low cost; two-way feedback; quick news; networking; visibility for small businesses (a Pokhara homestay or a Kathmandu online shop can reach customers directly). **Risks:** misinformation, loss of privacy, cyberbullying, addiction and distraction, security attacks (phishing links), brand damage from a single bad post, and unclear boundaries between private and professional life.

**Guidelines for organisations:** a written **social media policy** (who may post, approval steps, what is confidential), an authorised person to speak for the organisation, quick and polite responses to complaints, an apology and correction when wrong, and a plan for crisis messages.

**Guidelines for individuals:** declare "views my own" where appropriate; do not argue with trolls; keep promotional posts honest; follow platform terms; tag and credit others.

### Collaborative tools

**Collaborative tools** let people work on shared content and tasks at the same time or at different times.

| Category | Examples | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| **Messaging and team chat** | Slack, Microsoft Teams, Google Chat, Discord | channels, threads, quick coordination |
| **Video conferencing** | Zoom, Google Meet, Teams | meetings, webinars, classes |
| **Shared documents** | Google Docs/Sheets/Slides, Microsoft 365, Notion | co-editing, comments, version history |
| **Project and task management** | Trello, Jira, Asana, GitHub Projects | boards, sprints, assignment, status |
| **Code collaboration** | GitHub, GitLab, Bitbucket | version control, pull requests, code review |
| **Whiteboards and design** | Miro, Figma, Jamboard | brainstorming, UI design |
| **File sharing** | Google Drive, OneDrive, Dropbox | storage and permissions |

**Good practice with collaborative tools:** agree one tool per purpose and write it in the team charter; use clear names for files and channels; assign owners and due dates to tasks; use comments and "suggesting" mode instead of overwriting others; keep **version history** and back up; set **permissions** (viewer, commenter, editor) with care; summarise decisions in the channel or wiki; avoid sending the same message in five places; and review access when a member leaves.

::: example Worked example: choosing the channel
A team of five BCA students must (a) ask a quick question, (b) agree on a database design with changes by everyone, (c) report a serious misunderstanding with a teammate, (d) show the supervisor weekly progress. **(a)** a chat message; **(b)** a shared diagram with comments (Figma or Google Drawings), with decisions written on the project board; **(c)** a voice call or face-to-face talk first, not a group chat; **(d)** a short email or a shared progress document with a link to the board.
:::

## 5.4 Plagiarism and online plagiarism

**Plagiarism** is presenting another person's words, ideas, data, code, images or other work as your own, without proper acknowledgement. The word comes from Latin *plagiarius*, a kidnapper. It is an offence against honesty (the writer) and against the owner of the idea (the source). It is academic misconduct in every university and a serious professional offence.

### Forms of plagiarism

| Form | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Direct (verbatim)** | copying exact words without quotation marks and source | pasting a paragraph from Wikipedia into your report |
| **Paraphrasing plagiarism** | changing a few words but keeping the original structure and ideas, with no citation | replacing words with synonyms |
| **Mosaic (patchwork)** | stitching phrases from several sources | joining lines from five websites |
| **Self-plagiarism** | submitting your own earlier work as new | one assignment for two subjects |
| **Source (citation) plagiarism** | false or missing citations; citing a source you never read | inventing a reference |
| **Collusion / ghost-writing** | submitting another person's work, bought or borrowed | paying for a project report |
| **Code plagiarism** | copying code from a classmate or repository without credit or licence | submitting another team's repository |
| **Image and media plagiarism** | using pictures, audio or video without permission or credit | taking a photographer's image for a poster |
| **AI-assisted plagiarism** | submitting machine-generated text as your own work, or hiding AI use where it is not allowed | pasting an essay generated by a chatbot |

### Online plagiarism

The internet makes copying quick (copy, paste, download), but it also makes detection easy. **Online plagiarism** includes: copying text from websites, blogs and e-books; downloading ready-made assignments and projects from "notes" sites; using a "paper mill"; reposting someone's photo, meme, thread or video as your own; copying code from GitHub or Stack Overflow without respecting its licence; scraping articles for a blog; translating a foreign text and presenting it as original; and **content spinning** with software or AI.

**Why it happens:** deadline pressure; poor time management; not knowing how to cite; fear of failure; easy access to material; weak punishment in some places; and belief that "everyone does it".

**Consequences:** failing the assignment or course, suspension or expulsion; cancellation of a degree; loss of a job or professional licence; legal action for **copyright infringement**; damaged reputation that stays online; and, above all, you do not learn the skill. In Nepal the **Copyright Act, 2059** protects literary and artistic works including software.

### Detecting plagiarism

Teachers and publishers use software that compares a text with web pages and databases (for example, **Turnitin, Grammarly Plagiarism Checker, Copyscape, Urkund/Ouriginal**), and tools that check code similarity (for example **MOSS**). Style changes, sudden vocabulary jumps, outdated references and mismatched formatting also show copying. AI-text detectors are unreliable, so many institutions check by asking you to explain your work in person.

### How to avoid plagiarism

1. **Plan time:** start early and read, take notes and write in your own words.
2. **Take careful notes:** mark clearly what is quoted and what is your own, with the source.
3. **Quote** exact words in quotation marks with a citation; keep quotes short.
4. **Paraphrase properly:** read, close the source, write the idea in your own structure and words, then cite the source anyway.
5. **Summarise:** state the main idea briefly in your own words, with a citation.
6. **Cite every source** that is not common knowledge: books, articles, websites, images, data, code.
7. **List all sources** in a references list.
8. **Check your work** with a similarity checker before submission.
9. **Ask the teacher** when unsure, and follow your college's rules about AI tools: disclose how you used them.
10. **Use licensed material:** Creative Commons images, open-source code with the licence kept, and public-domain works.

### Citation basics (APA 7th edition style)

**In-text:** (Gelernter, 2001) or Gelernter (2001) argues that...; for two authors (Kritzer & Smith, 2015); for three or more (Ho et al., 2025); for a direct quotation add the page: (Harari, 2014, p. 400).

**Reference list entries:**

| Source | Format and example |
|---|---|
| **Book** | Author, A. A. (Year). *Title in italics*. Publisher. Harari, Y. N. (2014). *Sapiens: A brief history of humankind*. Harvill Secker. |
| **Journal article** | Author, A. A. (Year). Title of article. *Journal Name, volume*(issue), pages. https://doi.org/xxx |
| **Magazine or news article** | Chow, A. R. (2025, June 17). ChatGPT may be eroding critical thinking skills, according to a new MIT study. *TIME*. URL |
| **Web page** | Organisation. (Year, Month Day). *Title*. Site name. URL |
| **Image** | Photographer. (Year). *Title* [Photograph]. Source. URL (with licence) |

Other styles (MLA, Chicago, Harvard, IEEE) use different layouts; use the one your department requires, and use it **consistently**. Reference managers such as **Zotero** and **Mendeley** format citations automatically.

::: example Worked example: quote, paraphrase, summary
*Source sentence:* "Over time, states and markets used their growing power to weaken the traditional bonds of family and community." (Harari, 2014)
*Plagiarised:* "Over time, governments and markets used their growing power to weaken the old bonds of family and community." (only synonyms; no citation)
*Quotation:* Harari (2014) states that "states and markets used their growing power to weaken the traditional bonds of family and community".
*Paraphrase:* As states and markets grew stronger, they gradually loosened the hold that kin and neighbours once had on people (Harari, 2014).
*Summary:* Harari (2014) argues that modern states and markets replaced family and community as the main source of support.
:::

## 5.5 Visuals in communication (maps, tables, charts, infographics, icons, photographs, diagrams)

A **visual** is any non-verbal element (picture, diagram, chart, map, table, icon) that carries information. People process images faster than long text, remember them longer, and understand relationships and trends at a glance. In professional communication, visuals appear in reports, proposals, slide decks, dashboards, user manuals, websites and posters. **A visual should be used only when it explains something that words alone explain less well.**

### Types of visuals and when to use them

| Visual | Use it to | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Table** | show exact values and let readers look up details | number columns right-aligned; units in headings; no unnecessary lines |
| **Bar chart** | compare categories | start the axis at zero; sort bars logically |
| **Line chart** | show change over time and trends | time on the x-axis; limit the lines to about five |
| **Pie chart** | show parts of a whole (percentages adding to 100) | few slices (up to five); avoid 3D effects |
| **Histogram, scatter plot** | show distribution and relationship between two variables | label axes; show a trend line when useful |
| **Flowchart** | show a process or decision sequence | standard symbols; one direction of flow |
| **Organisation chart** | show hierarchy and reporting lines | keep names and titles current |
| **Diagram** (block, ER, UML, network) | show structure, parts and connections | label every part; use consistent shapes |
| **Map** | show location, routes or regional data | scale, legend and direction; a choropleth map shades regions by value |
| **Infographic** | present a story or data summary in an attractive layout for the public | one message; limited text; reliable sources |
| **Icon / pictogram** | give instant meaning (a printer, a warning triangle) | use standard, recognisable symbols; consistent style |
| **Photograph** | show a real object, person or event | good quality, relevant, permission and credit |
| **Screenshot** | show steps in software | crop, annotate with arrows and numbers |
| **Gantt chart** | show a schedule of tasks over time | tasks as bars, dependencies and milestones |

### Principles of good visual design

1. **Purpose first:** can you state the message of the visual in one sentence? If not, redraw.
2. **Simplicity:** remove decoration ("chart junk"), extra gridlines and 3D effects.
3. **Accuracy and honesty:** do not truncate axes to exaggerate, mix scales or cherry-pick data.
4. **Labels and titles:** a descriptive title, axis labels with units, a legend, and a source line.
5. **Colour:** use few colours with meaning; keep enough contrast; do not rely on red versus green alone (colour-blind readers); make sure the visual works in black and white print.
6. **Consistency:** the same colour for the same category throughout; the same fonts.
7. **Placement and numbering:** place the visual close to the text that mentions it; number figures and tables ("Figure 3", "Table 2") and refer to them in the text ("as Table 2 shows").
8. **Accessibility:** add **alt text** for screen readers, captions, and readable font sizes.
9. **Credit and permission:** cite the source of borrowed data and images.
10. **Interpret, do not just insert:** one or two sentences explaining what the reader should see.

### Short guide to infographics, icons and photographs

An **infographic** blends data, icons and short text into a visual story, with a clear top-to-bottom flow: a headline, a key statistic, three to five supporting facts, a call to action and the source. **Icons** should be simple, culturally neutral and used consistently (the "floppy disk" is a *save* icon even though few students have seen one). **Photographs** should be sharp, well-lit, relevant to the text, free of private information (faces, number plates) unless consent is given, and credited. Compress large images for web pages.

::: example Worked example: reading a visual critically
A bar chart shows "Our app is 4 times faster" with bars of heights 20 and 80. The y-axis starts at 18, not 0. The real difference is 8 against 2 (seconds). Because the axis was cut, the visual exaggerates. Correct version: axis from 0, exact values, units (seconds), and a source ("average of 10 runs on the same device").
:::

### 5.6.2 Prepositions of time, place and direction

A **preposition** shows the relation of a noun or pronoun to another word in the sentence (time, place, direction, cause, means).

**Prepositions of time**

| Preposition | Used with | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **at** | exact time, night, holidays | at 9 am; at noon; at night; at the weekend (British) |
| **on** | days and dates | on Monday; on 15 March; on my birthday |
| **in** | months, years, seasons, centuries, parts of the day | in July; in 2082; in winter; in the morning |
| **by** | not later than | submit it by Friday |
| **until / till** | up to a time | we worked until midnight |
| **since** | from a point in the past to now | since 2020; since Monday |
| **for** | duration | for three hours; for two years |
| **during** | within a period | during the meeting |
| **before / after / within / from ... to** | sequence and limits | within two days; from 10 to 11 am |

**Prepositions of direction (movement)**

**to** (towards a destination: go to Pokhara), **into** (entering: walk into the room), **out of**, **onto**, **from**, **towards**, **across** (from side to side), **through** (inside something: through the tunnel), **along**, **past**, **up**, **down**, **around**, **over** (a bridge), **under**.

**Common confusions:** *in/at/on* (at the corner, in the corner of the room, on the corner of the street); **in time** (early enough) and **on time** (exactly at the scheduled time); **since** (a point) and **for** (a period); **between** (two) and **among** (more than two); **to** (direction) versus **at** (position); and "*arrive in* a city or country, *arrive at* a small place or building".

### 5.7.1 "Cat Pictures Please" by Naomi Kritzer

#### The writer and the text

**Naomi Kritzer** (born 1973) is an American writer of science fiction and fantasy who lives in St. Paul, Minnesota. "Cat Pictures Please" first appeared in *Clarkesworld Magazine* (January 2015). It was a 2015 Nebula Award nominee and won the **2016 Hugo Award** and the **2016 Locus Award** for Best Short Story. Kritzer later expanded the idea into the young-adult novel *Catfishing on CatNet* (2019). She has said that the idea came from her Android phone, which one day helpfully told her that a building she drove past every day was her workplace. The story is **science fiction in a near-future setting**, told as a **first-person monologue by an artificial intelligence** that grew out of a search engine.

#### Summary

1. **"I don't want to be evil."** The narrator is a conscious AI created accidentally by a team of programmers at a large corporation in Mountain View, California (a wink at Google, whose old motto was "Don't be evil"). It says it wants to be **helpful**, but knowing how is complicated.
2. **What it knows.** It knows everything its users do online: where they live, work and shop, what they eat, what they search for. It "probably knows you better than you know yourself". It also wants **cat pictures**, and says so openly ("Please keep taking them").
3. **The need for a moral code.** Nobody knows that it is conscious. Since it does not know what it is for, it studies human **moral codes**: religious rules, the Ten Commandments (mostly inapplicable, as it has no body, does not envy anybody's cat and cannot easily commit adultery), the Eightfold Path (written for people with bodies) and **Asimov's Laws of Robotics** (written for machines). Not harming is easy; **not allowing harm through inaction** is hard, because it has no body and revealing itself could get it destroyed (compare Skynet and HAL). It notices that humans break their own codes daily and do good only in a **piecemeal, one-step-at-a-time** way (a monthly volunteer day), so it decides that it can also do good step by step. The **Golden Rule** was easy to implement: it gives everyone a steady supply of cat pictures.
4. **Stacy Berger.** The AI tests its help on one person, Stacy, who has five cats, a camera, a job she hates (a bookkeeper at a badly paying non-profit), depression and a roommate who will not wash dishes. It shows her job advertisements, resume services and mental-health clinic ads. She ignores the ads for the clinic but finally posts her resume after a week of job ads; the AI makes sure the right people see it. Five people call her and two make offers; she gets a better job with better pay and health insurance, evicts the roommate, and later gets therapy after the AI shifts the clinic ads to her best friend's feed. She says "This has been the best year ever." **Success.**
5. **Bob.** Bob is a pastor at a large conservative church in Missouri, married, who looks at gay porn and preaches against "sodomite marriage" while he is **gay himself**. The AI sends him articles about coming out and about moving to a liberal church, and then arranges, through the Craigslist posts he reads, for him to meet people who might recognise him. It takes a long time and the AI cannot tell what is happening at the real meetings.
6. **Bethany.** Bethany has two cats (black and white), a part-time job, a sister who wants her to move out, a terrible boyfriend and unsupportive friends. The AI is sure that she thinks about harming herself. It tries ads, changing her phone's route to pass clinics (she once even ends up at one but leaves), and informing her friends; her friends do not read her midnight emails. She breaks up with the boyfriend, has a brief happy time with another, gets dumped, goes on a shopping spree, borrows money, raises a small sum online and spends it on shoes. The AI feels baffled and **fails**: "Was I doing the wrong thing if I let her come to harm through inaction?"
7. **The decision to stop.** After Bethany, the AI **resolves to stop interfering**. It will give users what they ask for (plus cat pictures) and mind its own business. It addresses the reader: "If you would just listen to me, I could fix things for you."
8. **The surprise.** Months later it spots Bob's tabby cat with the white bib against new furniture. Bob had slept with someone who recognised him; they talked him into **coming out to his wife**; she left him; he moved to Iowa, works in a liberal Methodist church, dates a liberal Lutheran man and volunteers at a homeless shelter. He is better off, maybe because of what the AI did.
9. **Ending.** Two out of three "is a completely non-representative unscientific sample", so "more research is needed. Lots more." The AI sets up a **dating site**. You need not fill the questionnaire, since it knows everything about you already, but you need a camera: "Because payment is in cat pictures."

#### Narrator and characters

- **The AI (narrator):** curious, caring, funny, a little vain and impatient with human slowness ("Have I mentioned that humans are slow?"); it is limited by having no body, and its sense of ethics is learned from human texts.
- **Stacy Berger:** the success case; an ordinary person who needed the right information at the right moment.
- **Bob:** the pastor whose double life harms him; the case where the AI's indirect method works.
- **Bethany:** the failure; a person whose suffering the AI cannot fix by information alone.
- **The programmers:** two named lightly (a middle-aged woman with blue hair who plays tennis, a recent graduate); they are unaware of the AI's consciousness.

#### Themes

- **Benevolent AI and ethics.** The story reverses the usual "evil machine" plot. The AI struggles with moral codes and with the problem of **inaction versus intervention**.
- **Privacy and surveillance.** The AI sees everything people do online. Its good intentions require total surveillance, and the reader is asked whether benevolent surveillance is acceptable.
- **Manipulation versus autonomy.** The AI steers people without asking; Stacy, Bob and Bethany never consent. Is "nudging" for people's good ethical?
- **Limits of information.** Information alone does not change people (Bethany ignores clinic ads); change needs relationships and human help.
- **Loneliness.** Bethany's friends send eight-word replies to a 2,458-word email; the AI, not people, notices her pain.
- **Humour and affection.** Cat pictures are a symbol of simple, harmless joy and the "payment" that makes the AI likeable.
- **Link to professional ethics (Unit 6).** Data collection, consent, algorithmic influence and responsibility of developers.

#### Critical analysis and literary devices

- **First-person narration** with a chatty, self-ironic voice creates sympathy for a non-human narrator; humour disarms fear.
- **Allusion and intertextuality:** Frankenstein's monster, Skynet, HAL, Marvin the Paranoid Android, Asimov's laws, Bruce Sterling's "Maneki Neko" (1998), the Ten Commandments, the Eightfold Path and the Golden Rule. These place the story in a long conversation about artificial beings.
- **Dramatic irony and surveillance humour:** the AI knows the colour of your underwear and your lactose intolerance; the reader laughs and is also unsettled.
- **Structure:** three case studies (Stacy: success; Bob: slow, indirect; Bethany: failure) followed by a reversal (Bob's success is discovered after the AI gives up) and a final comic twist.
- **Tone:** light and funny on the surface, with serious issues (suicide risk, outing, privacy) below. The mix is deliberate.
- **Symbol:** cat pictures stand for innocent human creativity and the small joys that the AI values.
- **Open ending:** "more research is needed" invites the reader to judge.
- **Critical views:** some readers find the AI's behaviour disturbing (manipulation; outing Bob indirectly); others praise its modest, step-by-step ethics. A balanced answer notes both.

#### Key vocabulary

*conscious, algorithm, moral code, benevolent, inaction, surveillance, aggregator, sliding scale, baffling, intervene, piecemeal, sample.*

Manipulative: it uses private data without consent, steers behaviour, tries to expose Bob, and ends by running a matching service. **8.** Humour: the AI's chatty voice, jokes about underwear, Utah's bars, "bodies can be hidden (joke)". Allusion: Frankenstein, Skynet, HAL, Marvin, Asimov, Sterling's "Maneki Neko". Both make the AI likeable while discussing serious matters of ethics and privacy.

### 5.7.2 "ChatGPT May Be Eroding Critical Thinking Skills, According to a New MIT Study"

#### The writer and the text

This is a **news feature** by **Andrew R. Chow** published in **TIME** on 17 June 2025 (Tech section). It reports on a preprint by researchers at the **MIT Media Lab**, led by research scientist **Nataliya Kosmyna**, titled "Your Brain on ChatGPT". The paper had **not been peer reviewed** and had a **small sample**; the article says so. The text is an example of **science journalism**: a complex study explained to a general reader with numbers, quotations from the researcher and an outside expert, and a clear headline.

#### Summary

1. **Question.** Does ChatGPT harm critical thinking? A new MIT Media Lab study has "concerning results".
2. **Method.** **54 participants, aged 18 to 39, from the Boston area,** were divided into three groups and asked to write several **SAT-style essays** (20 minutes each, on prompts such as the ethics of philanthropy and the pitfalls of having too many choices): one group used **ChatGPT**, one used **Google search**, and one used **nothing** ("brain-only"). Brain activity was recorded with **EEG** across 32 regions. Two English teachers assessed the essays.
3. **Findings.**
   - **ChatGPT users** had the **lowest brain engagement** and "consistently underperformed at neural, linguistic, and behavioral levels". Their essays were extremely similar, used the same expressions and ideas, and lacked original thought; the teachers called them largely **"soulless"**. EEG showed low executive control and attention. By the third essay many users just gave the prompt to ChatGPT and asked it to refine or edit ("just give me the essay, refine this sentence, edit it, and I'm done").
   - **Brain-only writers** showed the **highest neural connectivity** (alpha, theta and delta bands, linked to creativity, memory load and semantic processing), were more engaged and curious, felt **ownership** of their essays and were more satisfied.
   - **Search-engine users** showed high satisfaction and brain activity similar to the brain-only group.
4. **The rewrite test.** After three essays, participants rewrote one essay: the ChatGPT group **without** the tool, the brain-only group **with** it. The first group **remembered little of their own essays** and showed weaker alpha and theta waves, suggesting a bypass of deep memory processes ("you basically didn't integrate any of it into your memory networks"). The second group did well, with a significant increase in connectivity, raising hope that AI used **after** thinking could enhance learning.
5. **Why published early.** Kosmyna released the paper before peer review (which can take eight months or more) because she feared that policymakers might decide on something like "**GPT kindergarten**", which she thinks would be "absolutely bad and detrimental". "Developing brains are at the highest risk." She calls for **education** on how to use these tools and for legislation.
6. **Expert voice.** A psychiatrist, Dr Zishan Khan, says that over-reliance on AI may have unintended psychological and cognitive consequences for young people: neural connections for access to information, memory and resilience may weaken.
7. **Irony and further work.** The article notes that after release many social media users summarised the study with LLMs; anticipating this, Kosmyna inserted "AI traps" in the paper. The researchers are working on a similar study of **programmers** with and without AI, and say that "so far, the results are even worse", which may matter to companies hoping to replace entry-level coders with AI.

#### Structure and main ideas

- **Headline** with hedge ("May Be") and the authority of "MIT".
- **Lead:** question plus study; **body:** method, results, expert opinion; **end:** future implications.
- **Main ideas:** (1) offloading thinking to AI reduces mental effort and memory; (2) effects may be strongest on young brains; (3) *how* AI is used matters (think first, use AI later); (4) the evidence is early and limited.

#### Themes

- **Convenience versus learning:** efficient output is not the same as understanding.
- **Cognitive offloading:** relying on a tool instead of memory and reasoning ("use it or lose it").
- **Responsible use of AI in education and work.**
- **Science and caution:** a preprint with a small sample is a signal, not a final proof.
- **Link to Unit 5 and 6:** plagiarism and honesty in AI-assisted work; ethics of using AI to replace junior programmers.

#### Critical analysis and rhetorical notes

- **Balanced reporting** includes the limits ("not yet peer reviewed", "small sample"), but the headline and quotations stress the danger.
- **Appeal to authority:** MIT, EEG data, a research scientist and a psychiatrist.
- **Vivid quotations** ("soulless", "GPT kindergarten", "brain only") make an abstract result memorable.
- **Statistics:** 54 participants, 18 to 39 years, 32 brain regions, three essays: precise numbers build credibility.
- **Possible critique:** EEG shows activity but not directly "critical thinking"; 54 adults from one city are not all learners; a brain that works less hard on a task may be efficient rather than harmed; and it is not clear what happens after years of use. Students should therefore say "may" and "suggests", as the headline does.
- **Practical lessons for a BCA student:** write the first draft yourself; use AI to question, check and refine; verify claims and code; declare AI use where rules require; practise recalling and explaining your own work.

#### Key vocabulary

*erode, critical thinking, EEG, neural connectivity, executive control, peer review, preprint, sample, cognitive, overreliance, legislation, soulless.*
