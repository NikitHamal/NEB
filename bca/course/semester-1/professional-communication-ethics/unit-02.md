Most professional decisions are still made by voice: a phone call to a client, a stand-up meeting, a five-minute demo, an interview. Speech is fast and personal, but it leaves no record and cannot be edited after it is said, so it needs more preparation than most people give it. This unit covers the oral skills the syllabus names: telephone conversation, public speaking and presentations, meetings with agendas and minutes, elevator pitches, and interviews; then professional idioms and reported speech; and two prescribed readings, "Death by Powerpoint" and "Our world on fire needs you".

## 2.1 Telephone conversation

A telephone (or voice-call) conversation has **no body language**. The listener judges you by three things only: *what* you say, *how* you say it (tone, pace, volume) and *how you listen*. A smile can be heard; so can boredom. Because the call is usually unplanned from the other side, the first twenty seconds decide the impression.

### Structure of a professional call

| Stage | What to do | Model phrase |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Greeting and identification | greet, give name and organisation | "Good morning, Himalaya Software, Sita speaking. How may I help you?" |
| 2. Purpose | state why you call in one sentence | "I am calling about the invoice sent on 12 March." |
| 3. Exchange | listen, take notes, ask short questions, confirm facts | "May I confirm: the delivery is on Friday at 10 am?" |
| 4. Action and summary | repeat decisions, names, numbers, deadlines | "So I will send the revised quotation by tomorrow noon." |
| 5. Closing | thank, close politely, let the caller end first if senior | "Thank you for your time. Have a good day." |

### Do's and don'ts

- **Answer by the third ring**, with a pen and notepad ready. Speak slowly and clearly; slightly lower pitch sounds calmer and more confident.
- **Spell and confirm** names, numbers, email addresses (use the phonetic alphabet if the line is poor: *A for Alpha, B for Bravo*).
- **Ask permission before putting a caller on hold** and return within thirty seconds; offer a call-back instead of a long hold.
- **Take messages in full:** caller's name, organisation, number, message, date, time, your name.
- **Do not** eat, type loudly, shout across the room or complain about the caller to others while the line is open. Do not give confidential information to an unverified caller.
- **Mobile calls:** go to a quiet place, say if you are driving, avoid speakerphone in shared rooms, and switch the phone to silent in meetings.
- **Difficult callers:** stay calm, let them finish, acknowledge the feeling ("I understand this is frustrating"), then move to what you *can* do, and escalate with the caller's permission.

::: example Model call: a customer reports a fault
**Agent:** Good afternoon, Everest Broadband support, Ramesh speaking. How may I help you?
**Caller:** My internet has been down since morning and nobody has called me back.
**Agent:** I am sorry about the inconvenience. May I have your customer ID and the registered phone number?
**Caller:** It is 40512, number 9841000000.
**Agent:** Thank you, Mr Gurung. I can see your complaint was logged at 9 am. A technician is assigned and will reach you by 4 pm today. I will also text you the ticket number. Is there anything else I can do?
**Caller:** No, that is all.
**Agent:** Thank you for your patience. Have a good afternoon.
:::

**Voice mail and call-back messages** should be short: name, organisation, reason, number (repeated slowly) and the best time to call back. **Phone vs email:** use the phone when the matter is urgent, emotional, or needs quick back-and-forth; confirm any decision by email afterwards so that there is a record.

## 2.2 Public speaking and presentation skills

**Public speaking** is delivering a prepared spoken message to a live audience. A **presentation** is a structured talk, usually supported by slides, demonstrations or handouts, designed to inform, persuade, train or sell. Fear of speaking (glossophobia) is normal; preparation and practice turn nervous energy into energy for the talk.

### Types of presentation

| Type | Purpose | IT workplace example |
|---|---|---|
| Informative | explain facts and processes | a lecture on how SQL joins work |
| Persuasive | change a belief or win a decision | convincing the manager to move to cloud hosting |
| Instructional (training) | teach a skill with practice | training staff to use a new HR system |
| Demonstrative | show a product working | a live demo of a mobile app to a client |
| Progress / report | update on status, risks and next steps | a weekly sprint review |
| Sales / pitch | sell a product or an idea | a start-up pitch to investors |
| Motivational / ceremonial | inspire, welcome, honour | a commencement address, a farewell speech |

By **delivery method**: *manuscript* (read word for word; accurate but stiff), *memorised* (risky; a blank stops you), *impromptu* (no preparation; keep to a simple pattern such as past-present-future) and *extemporaneous* (prepared outline, spoken naturally; the best for professional talks).

### Guidelines for presentation

1. **Analyse the audience and purpose.** Who are they, what do they know, what do they need to decide? Finish the sentence "After this talk the audience will ___".
2. **Structure it:** *opening* (hook, purpose, preview: 10%), *body* (two to four main points with evidence: 80%), *closing* (summary and call to action: 10%). "Tell them what you will tell them; tell them; tell them what you told them."
3. **Design slides to support, not replace, you.** One idea per slide; about six lines and six words a line at most; large fonts (24 pt or more for body text); high contrast; one chart or picture instead of a paragraph; no reading from the slide.
4. **Rehearse aloud** with a timer, and in front of someone. Prepare for the room, projector, laptop adaptor and a backup copy (PDF on a pen drive).
5. **Deliver with presence:** stand steadily, make eye contact in sweeps of two to three seconds, use open gestures, vary pace and volume, pause after key points, avoid filler words ("um", "you know").
6. **Handle questions:** listen to the whole question, repeat or rephrase it, answer briefly and honestly ("I do not know, but I will find out and email you"), and keep control of time. Treat hostile questions as a request for evidence.
7. **Manage nervousness:** breathe slowly, know your first minute by heart, arrive early, drink water, and focus on the audience's benefit rather than your performance.
8. **Close strongly:** restate the one message and the action you want; thank the audience.

## 2.3 Meetings, agendas and minutes

### Meetings

A **meeting** is a planned gathering of people to share information, discuss, solve a problem or take a decision. Meetings are expensive: ten people for an hour is ten person-hours. A meeting is justified only when a decision or discussion needs people together.

| Type | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Information / briefing | one-way update | monthly staff briefing |
| Decision-making | choose between options | selecting a vendor |
| Problem-solving / brainstorming | generate ideas | finding the cause of server crashes |
| Stand-up (daily scrum) | 15-minute team sync | "yesterday, today, blockers" |
| Review / retrospective | learn from a sprint or project | end-of-sprint review |
| Virtual / hybrid | participants online or mixed | a video call with a client abroad |

**Roles:** the *chairperson* leads the discussion and keeps to time; the *secretary* (minute-taker) records; *participants* prepare, speak briefly and follow up. **Good practice:** send the agenda 24 to 48 hours before, start on time, state the purpose, give everyone a chance, stay on the agenda, summarise decisions, and end with action items.

**Formal meeting terms:** *quorum* (the minimum number of members whose presence makes decisions valid), *motion* (a proposal), *resolution* (a motion that has been passed), *second* (support for a motion), *vote*, *casting vote* (the chair's vote to break a tie), *adjournment* (closing or postponing).

### Agendas

An **agenda** is the list of items to be discussed, in order, with time and the person responsible. It tells participants *why* and *what to prepare*.

::: example Model agenda
**Himalaya Software Pvt. Ltd. — Project Review Meeting**
Date: Friday, 14 March 2025 · Time: 10:00 to 11:00 am · Venue: Conference Room 2 (and Google Meet link) · Chair: Mr A. Sharma · Secretary: Ms R. Thapa

| No. | Item | Lead | Time |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Welcome and apologies for absence | Chair | 5 min |
| 2 | Minutes of the previous meeting (confirm) | Secretary | 5 min |
| 3 | Matters arising: server migration status | Mr Karki | 10 min |
| 4 | Sprint 6 progress and risks | Ms Rai | 15 min |
| 5 | Client feedback on the mobile app | Ms Gurung | 15 min |
| 6 | Any other business | All | 5 min |
| 7 | Date of next meeting | Chair | 5 min |
:::

### Minutes

**Minutes** are the official written record of what was discussed and decided. They are *not* a transcript. They are written in the **past tense** and **reported speech** (Section 2.6.2), in the third person, in a neutral tone, with each decision and its owner clear.

**Contents of minutes:** name of the organisation and meeting; date, time, venue; list of those present, absent and apologies; confirmation of the previous minutes; each agenda item with discussion summary, decision and action (who, by when); any other business; date of next meeting; signature of chair and secretary. Types: **action minutes** (decisions and tasks only, the commonest in industry), **discussion minutes** (include summary of views) and **verbatim minutes** (word for word, only for legal bodies).

::: example Model minutes (extract)
**Minutes of the Project Review Meeting held on Friday, 14 March 2025 at 10:00 am in Conference Room 2.**
**Present:** Mr A. Sharma (Chair), Ms R. Thapa (Secretary), Mr Karki, Ms Rai, Ms Gurung. **Apologies:** Mr Joshi.
**1. Minutes of the previous meeting.** The minutes of 7 March were read and confirmed.
**2. Server migration.** Mr Karki reported that 70 per cent of the databases had been migrated. It was agreed to complete the remainder by 21 March. *Action: Mr Karki, 21 March.*
**3. Sprint 6.** Ms Rai said that the payment module was two days behind schedule because of an API change. The Chair approved one extra developer for the module. *Action: Ms Rai, from Monday.*
**4. Client feedback.** Ms Gurung reported that the client wanted a dark mode. It was decided to include it in Sprint 7.
The meeting ended at 10:55 am. The next meeting will be on 21 March 2025.
Signed: ____________ (Chair) ____________ (Secretary)
:::

## 2.4 Elevator pitches

An **elevator pitch** is a 30 to 60 second spoken summary (about 80 to 150 words) of who you are or what your idea is, short enough to deliver in a lift ride. It is used at job fairs, networking events, start-up competitions and in everyday introductions. Its purpose is not to tell everything but to make the listener say, "Tell me more."

**Structure (five parts):** (1) **Hook**, a question, surprising fact or one-line problem; (2) **Who you are / what it is**, in plain words; (3) **Problem and solution**, what pain you remove; (4) **Proof**, one number, skill or achievement; (5) **Ask**, a specific next step (a meeting, an email, a trial).

::: example Two model pitches
**Personal (job fair):** "Good morning. I am Anita Shrestha, a final-semester BCA student. Companies tell me that fresh graduates can write code but not explain it. I built and documented a college library system in Java and MySQL that is used by 600 students, and I presented it at our college expo. I am looking for a junior developer internship where I can learn from a team. May I send you my CV and project link today?"
**Product (start-up):** "Farmers in Nepal lose a fifth of their crop to late disease detection. Our app, KrishiAI, lets a farmer photograph a leaf and gets a diagnosis in Nepali within ten seconds. In a pilot with 120 farmers in Chitwan, treatment started three days earlier on average. We are looking for a partner cooperative for a three-month trial. Can we meet next week?"
:::

**Tips:** write it out, cut it to 120 words, remove jargon, speak at a natural pace, practise until it sounds conversational, prepare three versions (10, 30, 60 seconds) and always carry a card or QR code.

## 2.5 Interviewing and professional dialogue

An **interview** is a structured conversation with a purpose: selecting (job), informing (press), evaluating (appraisal), or gathering requirements (client). **Professional dialogue** is any two-way workplace talk (stand-up, review, feedback, negotiation) in which both sides listen, respond and agree on what happens next.

### The job interview

| Stage | Candidate's task |
|---|---|
| Before | research the organisation and the job; match your skills to the advertisement; prepare stories; print the CV; plan the route; dress neatly |
| Opening | arrive 10 minutes early, greet with a smile and a handshake, sit when invited, switch the phone off |
| During | listen to the whole question; answer in 1 to 2 minutes; give evidence; be honest; keep posture open; ask for a repeat if unclear |
| Closing | ask two intelligent questions (training, team, next steps); thank the panel; confirm when to expect a reply |
| After | send a thank-you email within 24 hours; reflect on weak answers |

**Answering behavioural questions with STAR:** *Situation* (the context), *Task* (your responsibility), *Action* (what you did), *Result* (the measurable outcome). Example: "Tell me about a time you resolved a conflict." S: our project group quarrelled over the database design. T: I was the team coordinator. A: I called a 20-minute meeting, listed each design's pros and cons and proposed a test with sample data. R: the team chose the normalised design and finished a week early.

**Common questions and approach:** *Tell me about yourself* (present, past, future in 60 seconds, job-relevant); *Why this company?* (research, values, role); *Your strengths and weakness?* (real strengths with proof; a weakness you are improving, with the step you took); *Where do you see yourself in five years?* (growth in the role, skills, responsibility); *Why should we hire you?* (match skill to need, one achievement); *Any questions for us?* (always yes).

**Technical interviews** test fundamentals and thinking: say your reasoning aloud, ask clarifying questions, start with a simple solution, then improve it, and admit what you do not know.

**For the interviewer:** prepare a question list, ask the same core questions of all candidates, avoid illegal or discriminatory questions (caste, religion, marital status), listen more than speak, and explain the next steps.

**Professional dialogue skills:** open with a clear purpose, ask open questions ("How did the deployment go?") and closed questions ("Did it pass the tests?"), use *paraphrasing* ("So you need the report before Monday?"), *clarifying* and *summarising*, disagree politely ("I see it differently; may I explain why?"), and close by stating who will do what by when.

::: note Exam hint
Frequently asked: "What are the qualities of a good presentation?", "Write the agenda and minutes for a meeting", "Prepare a 60-second elevator pitch", and "How would you prepare for a job interview?". Always give a model (agenda, minutes, pitch) in the proper format; marks are given for format.
:::
