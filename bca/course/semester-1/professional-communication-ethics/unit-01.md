An IT professional spends more time communicating than coding: reading requirements, explaining a bug, writing to a client, presenting a design. Projects rarely fail because the compiler was wrong; they fail because two people meant different things by the same sentence. This unit builds the foundation: what language is, how meaning is made, how professional communication developed, the principles that make it work, the communication process, the silent channel of body language and the barriers that break it. It closes with commonly confused words, the uses of tense, and two prescribed readings: Amy Tan's "Mother Tongue" and Dhumaketu's "The Letter".

## 1.1 Fundamentals of language

### 1.1.1 Language and meaning

**Language** is a system of symbols (sounds, signs or written letters) combined by shared rules (grammar) so that people can exchange meaning. The Swiss linguist Ferdinand de Saussure described every word as a **sign** with two faces: the **signifier** (the sound or letters, such as *k-u-k-u-r*) and the **signified** (the concept in the mind, a four-legged barking animal). The link is **arbitrary**: nothing in the animal forces the sounds "dog", "kukur" or "Hund"; speakers simply agree. That agreement is why a word means different things in different communities.

The **Ogden and Richards triangle** (1923) adds the real thing: a *symbol* (the word) does not point directly to a *referent* (the object); it points to a *thought* in a person's mind, and the thought points to the object. Because thoughts differ from person to person, meaning is never fully shared: **meanings are in people, not in words**.

| Idea | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Denotation | the dictionary meaning | *server*: a computer that provides a service |
| Connotation | the feeling or association around a word | *cheap* and *affordable* denote low price; *cheap* sounds poor in quality |
| Polysemy | one word, several senses | *bank* (river edge, money), *bug* (insect, program fault), *port* (harbour, network socket) |
| Jargon | specialist vocabulary | *latency*, *refactor*, *deprecated* |

Professional messages should use the plainest word that is accurate: "use" rather than "utilise", "start" rather than "commence". Jargon is acceptable only when the reader shares it.

### 1.1.2 Language, society and culture

Language and society shape each other. A community's language records what it values (Nepali has separate pronouns *ta*, *timi*, *tapai* and *hajur* for degrees of respect, which English does not mark), and the way people talk marks who they are.

- **Dialect and register.** A *dialect* is a regional or social variety; a *register* is the level of formality chosen for a situation (a text to a friend versus a letter to a bank manager).
- **Code-switching.** Moving between languages or registers in a single conversation ("Meeting ma join garnu hai, link Viber ma pathaidinchu") is natural in multilingual Nepal and signals group identity; in a formal document the language should be a single, consistent variety.
- **Language and power.** People are often judged by accent, grammar or fluency rather than by the quality of their ideas. This is the central problem of Amy Tan's essay in 1.8.1.
- **High-context and low-context cultures** (Edward T. Hall). In high-context cultures much of the meaning sits in the setting and in what is *not* said (a polite "we will see" can mean no); low-context cultures expect the words to carry the message directly. Nepal, India and Japan lean high-context; Germany and the USA lean low-context. A global team needs both habits understood.
- **Linguistic diversity.** Nepal is home to well over a hundred languages; a professional here regularly works across Nepali, English and a mother tongue, and must choose the language that the reader can actually read.

### 1.1.3 Functions of language

Roman Jakobson (1960) listed six functions, each tied to one element of the communication act.

| Function | Focus | Workplace example |
|---|---|---|
| Referential | the context, facts | "The server went down at 14:05." |
| Emotive (expressive) | the speaker's feelings | "I am delighted to join the team." |
| Conative (directive) | the receiver, to make them act | "Please submit the report by Friday." |
| Phatic | keeping the channel open, social contact | "Good morning! How was the journey?" (small talk) |
| Metalingual | the language itself | "By *latency* I mean the delay between request and reply." |
| Poetic (aesthetic) | the form of the message | a slogan, a memorable tagline |

Most real messages mix functions. A good email opens phatically (a greeting), states facts (referential), makes a request (conative) and closes with courtesy (emotive).

### 1.1.4 The nature of communication

The word comes from Latin *communis*, "to make common". Communication is the process of creating shared meaning. Its main characteristics:

1. **A process, not an event.** It has no fixed beginning or end; each message is shaped by earlier ones.
2. **Two-way.** Without feedback the sender cannot know whether the meaning arrived.
3. **Symbolic.** Words, gestures and images stand for ideas; they are not the ideas.
4. **Contextual.** Time, place, relationship and culture change the meaning of identical words.
5. **Inevitable.** Paul Watzlawick: "one cannot *not* communicate"; silence, absence and a late reply also send a message.
6. **Irreversible.** A sent message cannot be unsent; an email can be forwarded or screen-shotted. Think before you send.
7. **Purposeful.** Professional messages aim to inform, instruct, persuade, request or build goodwill.

Models help us think. **Shannon and Weaver (1948)** described a *linear* path: source, transmitter, channel, receiver, destination, with noise on the channel. **Schramm** added shared fields of experience and feedback (an *interactive* model). **Barnlund's transactional model** says both people send and receive at the same time and meaning is built together. The model used in this book (1.4) is the interactive one.

### 1.1.5 Understanding meaning-making

Meaning is **constructed**, not delivered. Between the sender's intention and the receiver's understanding there are three layers.

- **Literal meaning:** what the words say. "Can you open the window?" asks about ability.
- **Implied meaning:** what the speaker intends. The same sentence is normally a polite request.
- **Social meaning:** what the message says about the relationship: respect, distance, authority.

The philosopher H. P. Grice explained how listeners find implied meaning through the **cooperative principle** and four maxims: **Quantity** (say as much as needed, no more), **Quality** (say only what you believe true), **Relation** (be relevant) and **Manner** (be clear and orderly). When a speaker appears to break a maxim, the listener infers a hidden meaning. A manager who answers "How good is the new developer?" with "He is always on time" has broken Quantity and Relation on purpose; the implied message is that there is nothing else good to say. Professionals should recognise implicature in others and avoid creating accidental implicature in their own writing.

::: example Worked example: one sentence, three meanings
A client writes: "We might look at this next quarter." *Literal:* a possibility. *Implied:* possibly a polite refusal. *Social:* the client wishes to avoid a direct "no". A good reply asks a clarifying question: "To plan our resources, could you confirm in writing whether the work is approved for next quarter?"
:::

### 1.1.6 Effective communication

Communication is **effective** when (1) the receiver understands the meaning the sender intended, (2) the receiver responds in the way intended, and (3) the relationship survives or improves. Before sending, ask: *Who is the reader? What do they already know? What do I want them to do? Which channel and which words make that easiest?* Effectiveness is lost through unclear purpose, wrong channel, poor timing, an unprepared sender, an unwilling receiver and noise (1.6).

## 1.2 A brief history of professional communication

| Period | Development | Effect on work |
|---|---|---|
| Before writing | speech, drums, fire signals, memory | communication limited to the reach of the voice |
| c. 3400 to 3200 BCE | Sumerian cuneiform on clay tablets | first written records of trade and tax |
| c. 3000 BCE onward | Egyptian hieroglyphs on papyrus | administration by scribes and letters |
| c. 1000 BCE | Phoenician alphabet, ancestor of Greek, Latin and Brahmi-derived scripts | writing becomes learnable by many |
| 5th century CE | Lichhavi stone inscriptions in the Kathmandu Valley (Changu Narayan pillar, 464 CE) | public, durable official notices |
| 105 CE (traditional date) | paper associated with Cai Lun in China | cheap writing surface |
| c. 1450 | Gutenberg's movable-type press in Europe | mass-produced documents |
| 1844 | Morse's telegraph | instant, brief messages across distance; the first "telegraphese", the ancestor of concise business style |
| 1876 | Bell's telephone | spoken business at a distance |
| 1881 | first postage stamps of Nepal | organised national postal communication |
| 1913 | first telephone line in Kathmandu (reported, in the Rana period) | official use of voice communication |
| 1870s to 1980s | typewriter, carbon copy, memo, photocopier, fax | the standardised business letter and memo |
| 1936 | Dale Carnegie, *How to Win Friends and Influence People* | the human relations approach to business talk |
| 1948 | Shannon's mathematical theory of communication | communication treated as a measurable system |
| 1971 | Ray Tomlinson sends the first networked email | the start of email culture |
| 1990s | World Wide Web; internet reaches Nepal (1995) | websites, email for every office |
| 2000s to 2010s | social media, instant messaging, video calls | always-on, informal, global communication |
| 2020 to 2021 | pandemic: remote work and video meetings become normal | netiquette and virtual teams (Unit 5) |
| 2022 onward | generative AI writing assistants | drafting is cheap; judgment, accuracy and honesty matter more |

Professional communication as a field grew out of the needs of trade, government, railways and large offices. Technical and scientific writing expanded in the Second World War, when manuals had to be understood by newly trained operators. Each new technology made messages faster and shorter; none removed the need for a clear purpose and a reader-centred message.

## 1.3 Principles of professional communication

**Professional communication** is purposeful communication in a workplace to inform, instruct, persuade, record or build relationships, in a style that respects the reader's time and the organisation's standards. Seven principles (the **7 Cs**) are used as a checklist.

| C | Rule | Weak | Better |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clear | one main idea, plain words | "Kindly effectuate the closure of the ticket." | "Please close the ticket." |
| Concise | no wasted words | "due to the fact that" | "because" |
| Concrete | facts, numbers, dates | "soon", "a lot" | "by Friday 5 pm", "320 users" |
| Correct | accurate facts, grammar, names, tone | wrong date, misspelt name | proofread; verify figures |
| Coherent | logical order, linked ideas | ideas in random order | purpose, details, action |
| Complete | all the information the reader needs | no time, venue or contact | who, what, when, where, how |
| Courteous | respect, positive tone, "you" view | "You failed to attach the file." | "The file seems missing; could you resend it?" |

Two further principles are used in practice: **audience-centred** (write from the reader's need, not your own convenience) and **purpose-first** (the reader should know within the first two lines why you are writing).

### 1.3.1 Importance of professional communication

1. **Fewer errors.** Clear requirements prevent rework; unclear ones cause defects and cost.
2. **Faster decisions.** A manager who gets a short summary with a recommendation decides sooner.
3. **Teamwork.** Teams share information, resolve conflict and divide work through communication.
4. **Client trust.** Prompt, honest messages build confidence; silence breeds suspicion.
5. **Career growth.** Employers rank communication among the top skills; promotion depends on explaining ideas to non-experts.
6. **Legal and written record.** Emails, minutes and contracts are evidence of what was agreed.
7. **Organisational image.** Every letter, post and phone call represents the organisation.
8. **Ethical conduct.** Honest reporting of risks and mistakes depends on the courage to communicate them (Unit 6).

## 1.4 The communication process

Every act of communication has the same elements. Figure 1.1 shows them for an announcement that the college is closed on Monday.

| Element | Meaning | Example (a lecturer announces a deadline) |
|---|---|---|
| Sender (source) | the person with an idea to share | the lecturer |
| Encoding | turning the idea into words, symbols or images | writing "Submit by Friday 5 pm" |
| Message | the encoded content | the deadline |
| Channel (medium) | the route the message travels | class notice, email, Viber group |
| Receiver | the person for whom it is meant | students |
| Decoding | interpreting the message | "Friday of this week or next?" |
| Noise | anything that distorts the message | a typo, a loud classroom, an unclear phrase |
| Feedback | the receiver's response | "Which Friday, sir?" |
| Context | the setting, relationship and culture around the exchange | exam week, a strict lecturer |

**Formal channels** follow the organisational chart (memos, official emails, minutes); the **informal channel**, or *grapevine*, is social, fast and unreliable. **Direction of flow:** *downward* (instructions from manager to team), *upward* (reports and suggestions), *horizontal* (between peers) and *diagonal* (across departments and levels). **Levels:** intrapersonal (self-talk), interpersonal, group, organisational, public and mass communication.

## 1.5 Non-verbal communication in the workplace

**Non-verbal communication** is every message sent without words: body, face, voice, space, time and appearance. It is usually unconscious, so listeners trust it more when it conflicts with the words. (The popular "93 % of communication is non-verbal" is a misreading of Albert Mehrabian's small experiments on *feelings and attitudes* when words and tone disagree; it is not a rule for all communication.)

| Code | Study of | Workplace example |
|---|---|---|
| Kinesics | gesture, posture, facial expression | open posture and a nod show attention; crossed arms and a frown can look closed |
| Oculesics | eye contact | steady, natural eye contact in an interview signals honesty (norms differ by culture) |
| Haptics | touch | a firm handshake; in many cultures other touch is inappropriate at work |
| Proxemics | personal space | Edward Hall's zones: intimate (under about 0.5 m), personal (to about 1.2 m), social (to about 3.5 m), public (beyond). Business talk happens in the social zone |
| Chronemics | use of time | arriving 10 minutes early shows respect; a late reply to an urgent email sends a message |
| Paralanguage | tone, pitch, volume, pace, pauses | the same "fine" can mean agreement or irritation |
| Appearance and artefacts | dress, grooming, desk, ID card | dress to the culture of the workplace and the occasion |
| Environment | seating, lighting, room layout | a round table invites discussion; a raised podium signals authority |

**Functions of non-verbal signals** (Knapp): they *repeat* (pointing while saying "that file"), *contradict* ("I'm fine" with a shaking voice), *substitute* (a thumbs-up), *complement* (a smile with thanks), *regulate* conversation (a raised hand to speak) and *accent* a point (a pause before the key figure).

**Applying it.** *Interview:* arrive early, greet with a smile, sit upright, keep hands visible. *Presentation:* face the audience, vary the voice, avoid pacing. *Video call:* camera at eye level, look at the lens when speaking, keep the background neutral and the microphone muted when not talking. *Phone call:* only voice carries the message, so smile, slow down and confirm points aloud. *Culture:* the Nepali *namaste* with joined palms, the Japanese bow and the Western handshake are all correct in their setting; pointing with a finger or the foot, or touching the head, can offend. When in doubt, observe, then follow the local custom.

## 1.6 Barriers to effective communication

A **barrier** is anything that prevents the message from being understood as intended.

| Barrier | Cause | Example | Remedy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical (environmental) | noise, distance, poor lighting, weak network | a loud workshop, a dropping video call | quiet room, test the channel, repeat key points |
| Semantic (language) | jargon, ambiguity, poor translation, difficult accent | "ship it" read literally | plain words, define terms, ask for paraphrase |
| Psychological (emotional) | anger, fear, stress, mistrust | an angry reply sent at midnight | pause, calm down, communicate later |
| Perceptual | stereotypes, halo effect, prejudice | assuming a quiet team member has no ideas | ask questions, judge the message, not the person |
| Cultural | different values, gestures, time sense | a vague "yes" meaning "I heard you" | learn norms, confirm in writing |
| Organisational | hierarchy, filtering, unclear roles | juniors afraid to report a problem | open-door policy, clear reporting lines |
| Technological | wrong tool, format or file type | a file nobody can open | agree tools, send standard formats |
| Information overload | too many messages | 200 emails a day | summaries, priority labels, fewer recipients |
| Attitudinal and poor listening | lack of interest, planning one's reply | interrupting, checking the phone | active listening (Unit 2) |

::: key How to overcome barriers
Know your audience; choose the right channel; use the 7 Cs; ask for and give feedback; listen actively; respect cultural differences; check understanding by asking the receiver to say it back.
:::

## 1.7 Vocabulary and grammar

### 1.7.1 Commonly confusing words

Words that sound or look alike are misused even by fluent writers. Spell-checkers do not catch them because each word is correct on its own.

| Pair | Meaning and example |
|---|---|
| accept / except | *accept* = receive: "Please accept the offer." *except* = excluding: "Everyone except Ram came." |
| advice / advise | *advice* (noun): "Take my advice." *advise* (verb): "I advise you to back up." |
| affect / effect | *affect* (verb) = influence: "Delay affects cost." *effect* (noun) = result: "The effect was positive." |
| assure / ensure / insure | *assure* a person; *ensure* = make certain; *insure* = buy insurance |
| complement / compliment | *complement* = complete: "The font complements the logo." *compliment* = praise |
| council / counsel | *council* = a committee; *counsel* = advice or a lawyer |
| principal / principle | *principal* = chief or head; *principle* = a rule: "an ethical principle" |
| stationary / stationery | *stationary* = not moving; *stationery* = paper and pens |
| its / it's | *its* = belonging to it; *it's* = it is: "It's in its folder." |
| their / there / they're | possessive; place; they are |
| lose / loose | *lose* = misplace; *loose* = not tight |
| than / then | comparison; time: "Better than before; then we tested it." |
| whose / who's | possessive; who is: "Who's the author, and whose is this file?" |
| farther / further | *farther* = physical distance; *further* = more, additional |
| fewer / less | *fewer* with countable nouns (fewer errors); *less* with uncountable (less time) |
| eminent / imminent | famous; about to happen: "The deadline is imminent." |
| discreet / discrete | careful and private; separate: "discrete units" |
| disinterested / uninterested | *disinterested* = impartial; *uninterested* = not interested |
| elicit / illicit | draw out; illegal |
| precede / proceed | come before; go ahead |
| site / sight / cite | place; vision; quote a source |
| device / devise | noun, a gadget; verb, to plan |
| emigrate / immigrate | leave a country; enter a country |
| formally / formerly | in a formal way; previously |
| practice / practise | noun; verb in British English (practice for both in American) |
| born / borne | given birth; carried |
| sensual / sensuous | physical pleasure; appealing to the senses |
| canvas / canvass | cloth; to solicit votes or opinions |
| allude / elude | refer indirectly; escape |
| ordinance / ordnance | a local law; military supplies |
| personal / personnel | private; staff |

::: example Worked example: proofread
"The affect of the new policy on it's personal is imminent." Corrected: "The *effect* of the new policy on *its personnel* is *imminent*." (*imminent* is correct; *affect*, *it's* and *personal* are wrong.)
:::

### 1.7.2 Uses of tense

A **tense** shows the time of an action and its aspect (simple, continuous, perfect, perfect continuous).

| Tense | Form | Main use (workplace example) |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present | V1 / V1+s | habits and facts: "The server backs up daily." |
| Present continuous | am/is/are + V-ing | action now or planned: "We are testing the app." |
| Present perfect | have/has + V3 | past action with present result: "I have sent the file." |
| Present perfect continuous | have/has been + V-ing | action continuing up to now: "We have been working since 9." |
| Simple past | V2 | completed past action: "The team released version 2 last week." |
| Past continuous | was/were + V-ing | action in progress in the past: "I was coding when the power failed." |
| Past perfect | had + V3 | earlier of two past actions: "The bug had gone before we looked." |
| Past perfect continuous | had been + V-ing | duration before a past event: "We had been waiting for an hour." |
| Simple future | will + V1 | decision, promise, prediction: "I will call you at five." |
| Future continuous | will be + V-ing | action in progress at a future time: "At noon I will be presenting." |
| Future perfect | will have + V3 | completed by a future time: "We will have finished by Friday." |
| Future perfect continuous | will have been + V-ing | duration up to a future time: "By June I will have been working here for a year." |

**Rules to remember.** (1) Use present tense for general truths and for timetables ("The meeting starts at 10"). (2) Use present perfect when *time is not stated* ("I have completed it") and simple past when it is ("I completed it yesterday"). (3) Do not use *will* after *if*, *when*, *as soon as* in a time or condition clause: "If it *rains*, we will cancel." (4) State-of-mind verbs (*know*, *like*, *want*, *believe*) are not normally used in continuous forms. (5) Keep tense consistent within a paragraph unless time really changes. (6) In minutes use past tense; in reports of facts use present tense; in plans use future.

**Common errors.** *I am working here since 2022* should be *I have been working here since 2022*. *He said he will come* should be *he would come* (reported speech, Unit 2). *I am knowing the answer* should be *I know the answer*.

### 1.8.1 "Mother Tongue" by Amy Tan

#### The writer and the text

**Amy Tan** (born 1952, Oakland, California) is a Chinese-American novelist, the daughter of immigrants from China. Her first novel, *The Joy Luck Club* (1989), made her famous. "Mother Tongue" is a personal essay first published in 1990 (*The Threepenny Review*); it comes from a talk in which she reflected on the language she had grown up with. **Genre:** personal essay (memoir with argument). **Point of view:** first person. **Tone:** reflective, humorous and quietly defiant. **Setting:** a lecture hall, her childhood home, a bank, a hospital, a school test room and her desk.

#### Summary

1. **A writer who loves language.** Tan opens by saying she is not a scholar of English; she is a writer who loves language and has always been fascinated by its power to evoke images, convey emotion and create ideas. She writes about the English she learned.
2. **The "different Englishes".** While giving a talk about her book, she realises that she is speaking a polished, academic English that her mother has never heard her use. That evening her mother is in the audience and Tan becomes aware of the contrast. She realises that she uses **several Englishes**: the formal English of talks and writing, and the "family talk" she uses with her mother: intimate, full of shortcuts, grammatically unorthodox, yet perfectly clear to both. Her mother's English is often called "broken" or "limited" by outsiders; Tan avoids those labels and calls it simply a different English, one that expressed her mother's thoughts vividly.
3. **How her mother was treated.** Because of her accent and grammar, others did not take her mother seriously. Tan gives examples: in shops and restaurants staff gave worse service or no service; a **stockbroker** failed to treat her mother's request properly until Tan herself phoned; and at a **hospital** the staff had lost the films of her mother's scan and showed no concern or apology until Tan telephoned and spoke in fluent English, after which they apologised and acted. Tan, as a child and teenager, became her mother's translator and spokesperson and felt both ashamed and responsible.
4. **Her mother's real ability.** Tan stresses that her mother understood English very well: she read widely (including financial publications), followed the stock market and managed her own investments. The problem lay not in her mother's mind but in other people's prejudice about the way she spoke.
5. **How language limited Tan.** She says that her mother's English influenced her own: as a child she thought it was normal; as she grew up she began to see it as "wrong", and she saw herself as less capable in English. In school she did well in math and science, but in English tests, especially **analogy questions** with a single "correct" answer, she scored worse because her mind was busy with alternatives and she lacked the cultural background for such tests. Teachers and counsellors steered her towards math and science, as is often assumed for Asian-American children.
6. **Choosing to write.** Tan rebelled against that advice. She studied English, became a writer, first of business writing and later of fiction. She decided to write stories using **all the Englishes she grew up with**, including the language of her family. She imagined her **mother as her reader** and tried to capture her intent, her passion, her imagery and her rhythms of speech.
7. **The final test.** When her mother read *The Joy Luck Club*, she said it was easy to read. Tan treats that remark as the real measure of success: her mother understood and recognised the language, so the book worked.

#### Themes

- **Language and identity.** The way a person speaks is part of who they are; there is no single "correct" English.
- **Prejudice and power.** Society equates fluency with intelligence and denies respect to people with accents (class, race, immigration).
- **Intimacy of the mother tongue.** The "family English" carries love, history and thought that polished English cannot.
- **The immigrant child as translator.** A child carries adult responsibilities and feels shame, then pride.
- **Stereotype and expectation.** The steering of Asian-American students towards math is a prejudice that the writer resists.
- **The writer's craft.** Writing for a specific, real reader makes language alive.

#### Critical analysis and rhetorical notes

- **Structure:** it moves from the general (language) to the personal (mother) to the public (hospital, school) and back to the personal (the book and its first reader). The ending returns to the mother, giving the essay circularity.
- **Rhetoric:** *ethos* (credibility) comes from admitting she is a writer, not a scholar; *pathos* from the scenes of her mother being ignored; *logos* from the analogy-test example showing how tests reward one kind of thinking.
- **Style:** simple, conversational sentences with occasional humour; she includes samples of her mother's speech to *show* rather than *tell* that it is expressive.
- **Key word choice:** the phrases "broken", "fractured" and "limited" are discussed and rejected; this *reframing* is her central argument.
- **Critical view:** the essay speaks for one family; critics note that it risks suggesting that immigrant English is a problem solved by an educated child. Its strength is that it does not blame the mother but society's reaction.
- **Link to professional communication:** accent and grammar influence credibility at work; a professional should judge ideas, not accents, and should adapt register to the audience: the principle of audience-centred communication (1.3).

#### Key vocabulary

*evoke* (bring to mind), *fractured* (broken, unconventional), *imagery*, *wince* (flinch at), *benign* (harmless), *insular* (isolated), *intimacy*, *nominalisation* is not used; *translator*, *prejudice*, *endorsement* (approval).

### Exercise: "Mother Tongue"

1. Why does Tan say she uses "all the Englishes" she grew up with?
2. How did people treat Tan's mother, and why?
3. Describe the incident at the hospital. What did it show about language and power?
4. What is Tan's difficulty with analogy questions and with English tests in general?
5. What is the "real test" of Tan's writing, and why does it matter to her?
6. Long answer: "Language is more than correctness; it is identity." Discuss with reference to the essay.
7. Write a paragraph on how accent and grammar can affect a person's career in an IT company, and what a manager should do.

::: note Answers and worked solutions
**1.** Because each English belongs to a relationship and a purpose: the polished English of public talks and the intimate family English with her mother. Writing in only one would leave out her real life.
**2.** Staff in shops, restaurants, a bank and a hospital did not take her seriously because of her accent and "limited" grammar. They assumed that poor spoken English meant poor understanding.
**3.** The hospital had lost her mother's scan and offered no real help; when Tan telephoned in fluent English, the staff apologised and acted. The same problem got a different response depending on the speaker's English, showing that language confers power.
**4.** Analogy questions have one "correct" answer but, for Tan, many answers seemed possible; and her English at home did not match the test's cultural expectation. This led teachers to push her towards math and science.
**5.** Her mother's comment that the book was easy to read. Tan wrote for her mother as the imagined reader, so understanding by her mother proves that the book kept the language of home while being literature.
**6.** Introduction: Tan rejects the label "broken English". Body: (i) her mother's English is expressive and clear; (ii) society judges by correctness, as in the stockbroker and hospital incidents; (iii) language carries intimacy and culture; (iv) Tan's literary career is built from this "family English". Conclusion: correctness matters in some settings, but identity and communicative success matter more.
**7.** Model: Accent should not be a basis for judging an engineer. A manager should focus on content, ask clarifying questions politely, share written summaries after meetings, and train all staff in listening across accents.
:::

### 1.8.2 "The Letter" by Dhumaketu

#### The writer and the text

**Dhumaketu** is the pen name of **Gaurishankar Govardhanram Joshi** (1892 to 1965), a major Gujarati novelist and short-story writer. "The Letter" is an English translation of his Gujarati story, whose original title is often given as "The Post Office". **Genre:** short story. **Point of view:** third person, mostly following the postmaster. **Setting:** a small town's post office, five years of waiting. **Tone:** tender, ironic, tragic.

#### Summary

1. **The old man at the post office.** Every morning, **Coachman Ali**, an old man, comes to the post office and asks if there is a letter from his daughter **Miriam**. Years earlier he had been a skilled hunter (*shikari*); when his daughter married a soldier and left to live far away, he was left alone, felt for the first time the pain of separation, and gave up hunting (he realised what the animals he had killed had suffered when parted from their young). He has been coming at dawn for about five years.
2. **Ridicule.** The post-office staff laugh at him, call him mad, and tell him to go away. The **postmaster** treats him as a nuisance and speaks harshly. Ali never replies angrily; he returns the next day, believing the letter will come.
3. **The change in Ali.** After some days of absence, Ali returns weak and nearly broken. When he asks again and the postmaster scolds him, Ali loses some of his patience, but not his faith. He then gives the clerk **Lakshmi Das** five gold coins and requests that if Miriam's letter arrives after his death, it be placed on his grave. After that, he does not return.
4. **The postmaster's own fear.** The postmaster's own daughter falls seriously ill in another town. He waits desperately for news, searching the mail every day, restless and anxious. For the first time he understands what it is to wait for a letter about someone you love.
5. **The letter arrives.** One day he finds, among the letters, one addressed to Coachman Ali. He recognises his earlier cruelty, feels guilty and decides to deliver it personally.
6. **The ghost at the door.** Early next morning, when he opens the post-office door, he sees Ali standing there with tears in his eyes, leaning on his stick. Soon after, he learns that Ali has died and left coins and a request. Shocked, he places the letter on Ali's grave. (The story leaves it open whether the postmaster has seen a ghost, a vision or his own conscience.)

#### Characters

- **Ali:** faithful, patient, dignified in suffering; a devoted father; he represents unconditional love and hope.
- **The postmaster:** educated, authoritative, cold; he lacks empathy until his own loss. He changes (a *round*, developing character).
- **The clerks:** unthinking, mocking; they represent the crowd that laughs at what it does not understand.
- **Miriam:** never appears; her silence is the engine of the plot.

#### Themes

- **Parental love and waiting** that outlasts disappointment and death.
- **Empathy:** we understand suffering only when we feel it ourselves.
- **Authority without compassion** versus common humanity; people are not "pests" but have stories.
- **Communication and silence:** a single letter carries more than information; its absence is a message. A professional lesson: replying matters.
- **Guilt and redemption:** the postmaster's visit to the grave is an act of atonement.

#### Critical analysis and literary devices

- **Irony:** the postmaster ignores Ali's need for a letter and later suffers the same longing; the letter finally comes after Ali's death (*dramatic irony*).
- **Symbolism:** the post office is a place of connection; the letter stands for love and recognition; the gold coins show how seriously Ali trusts the institution to keep its promise.
- **Foreshadowing:** Ali's weakness and the coins prepare the reader for his death.
- **Structure:** a circle of waiting; the story moves from mockery to remorse, with a supernatural touch at the end.
- **Narration:** the story gives few details of Ali's thoughts; the reader's sympathy is built through repeated action.
- **Link to the workplace:** frontline staff deal with anxious people every day. Courtesy, patience and a clear reply, even "no letter today", are part of professional communication.

#### Key vocabulary

*coachman* (driver of a horse carriage), *shikari* (hunter), *pest* (nuisance), *remorse* (deep regret), *atone* (make amends), *stoop* (bend), *anxious*, *tender*, *grave*.

### Exercise: "The Letter"

1. Who is Ali and what does he do every day?
2. How do the post-office staff treat him?
3. What does Ali ask Lakshmi Das to do, and why?
4. What changes the postmaster's attitude?
5. Explain the irony in the story.
6. Long answer: Discuss the theme of empathy in "The Letter".
7. Write a short note on the character of the postmaster.

::: note Answers and worked solutions
**1.** Ali is an old coachman and former hunter who goes to the post office every day to ask for a letter from his married daughter Miriam.
**2.** They laugh at him, call him mad and send him away; the postmaster speaks harshly.
**3.** He gives five gold coins and asks that if Miriam's letter arrives after his death, it be placed on his grave. He fears he will not live to read it but wants her words to reach him.
**4.** His own daughter is seriously ill, he waits anxiously for news and then he finds a letter for Ali. He feels the same longing and understands Ali's pain.
**5.** The postmaster, who mocked Ali's waiting, waits in the same way for his daughter; and Miriam's letter arrives when Ali is already dead. A message that mattered so much comes too late.
**6.** Introduction; the postmaster begins without empathy; his own fear teaches him what Ali feels; he places the letter on the grave in atonement; conclusion: the story argues that empathy usually comes from shared experience and that a professional must try to imagine it in advance.
**7.** Educated and authoritative but unfeeling at first; harsh to Ali; his daughter's illness awakens guilt; at the end, humble and remorseful. He is a developing character.
:::
