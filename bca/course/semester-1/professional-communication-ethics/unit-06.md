## 6.1 Professional ethics in computing

### Ethics, morals and law

**Ethics** is the systematic study of right and wrong conduct and the principles used to decide. **Morals** are a person's or society's beliefs about right and wrong. **Law** is the set of rules made and enforced by the state. They overlap but are not identical: many legal acts are unethical (misleading a client with technically true statements), and some illegal acts may be ethical (breaking an unjust law in civil disobedience). A professional asks two questions: *Is it legal?* and *Is it right?*

| | Ethics | Law |
|---|---|---|
| Source | reason, conscience, culture, profession | legislature and courts |
| Enforcement | conscience, peer pressure, professional bodies | police, courts, penalties |
| Scope | wider (honesty, fairness, care) | narrower (what the state decides to regulate) |
| Change | slow, debated | by amendment |

### What is a profession?

A **profession** is an occupation that needs advanced education and skill, serves society, has a body of knowledge, is organised by a professional association with a code of conduct, and is trusted with autonomy because it acts in the public interest. Medicine, law, engineering and accountancy are classic examples. Computing is a **young profession**: in many countries software work is not licensed, and anyone can publish code. This makes **personal professional ethics** more important, since there is often no outside guard.

**Professionalism** means competence, integrity, accountability, respect for others and continuing learning.

### Why computing needs ethics

1. **Scale and speed:** a single bug or policy affects millions in seconds (a faulty update can stop hospitals).
2. **Invisibility:** users cannot see code, so they must trust developers.
3. **Power over information:** IT professionals access private data, communications and money.
4. **Dual use:** the same technology can protect or harm (encryption, face recognition, drones, AI).
5. **Safety-critical systems:** aircraft, medical devices, cars and power plants run on software.
6. **Social effects:** algorithms shape jobs, credit, news and elections.
7. **Few external checks,** so self-regulation by individuals and companies is essential.

### Main ethical theories

| Theory | Core idea | Test question | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Utilitarianism** (Bentham, Mill) | the right act gives the greatest good for the greatest number | Who benefits and who is harmed overall? | considers consequences | can sacrifice minorities; hard to measure |
| **Deontology / duty ethics** (Kant) | act on duties and rules that you could make universal; treat people as ends, never merely as means | Would I want everyone to do this? Am I respecting persons? | protects rights and honesty | rigid when duties conflict |
| **Virtue ethics** (Aristotle) | be a person of good character: honest, courageous, fair, temperate | What would a person of integrity do? | focuses on character | gives little concrete guidance |
| **Rights-based ethics** (Locke) | people have basic rights (life, liberty, privacy) that must be respected | Does this violate anyone's rights? | strong protection | rights can conflict |
| **Justice and fairness** (Rawls) | benefits and burdens should be distributed fairly; imagine rules chosen behind a "veil of ignorance" | Would I accept this if I did not know my place in the system? | fairness to the weakest | disagreement over what is fair |
| **Ethics of care** (Gilligan, Noddings) | relationships and responsibility for those who depend on us | Who is vulnerable and what do they need? | human and contextual | can favour close relations |
| **Social contract** | rules that rational people would agree to for mutual benefit | Is this a rule everyone could accept? | basis of laws and codes | depends on who is in the "contract" |
| **Ethical relativism** (to compare) | right and wrong depend on culture | What does this culture accept? | respects diversity | cannot condemn harmful customs |

*Example.* A company wants to track employees' keystrokes. **Utilitarian:** productivity gain versus stress and loss of trust. **Deontologist:** secret monitoring treats people as means; open and consented monitoring may be acceptable. **Virtue:** a trustworthy employer would be honest about it. **Rights:** employees have a right to privacy. **Justice:** would managers accept being monitored in the same way?

### Ethical problems specific to computing

Privacy and surveillance; security and safety; intellectual property; honesty and "dark patterns" (deceptive interfaces); fairness and algorithmic bias; access and the digital divide; accountability for autonomous systems; the environmental cost of computing (energy, e-waste); and the effect on jobs.

## 6.2 Professional code of ethics

A **code of ethics** (or code of conduct) is a written statement of the values and rules of conduct that a profession or organisation expects. It guides decisions, tells the public what to expect, protects members who refuse to do wrong, and supports discipline.

### The ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018)

The **Association for Computing Machinery** (ACM) adopted its current code in 2018. It has four parts: 1. General ethical principles, 2. Professional responsibilities, 3. Professional leadership principles, 4. Compliance with the code.

| # | Principle (general) | In practice |
|---|---|---|
| 1.1 | **Contribute to society and to human well-being,** acknowledging that all people are stakeholders in computing | think about the effect of a system on all those affected |
| 1.2 | **Avoid harm** | test safety-critical software thoroughly; report risks |
| 1.3 | **Be honest and trustworthy** | do not exaggerate skills; admit errors; no hidden functions |
| 1.4 | **Be fair and take action not to discriminate** | check systems for bias; design for accessibility |
| 1.5 | **Respect the work required to produce new ideas, inventions, creative works and computing artefacts** | credit sources; honour licences |
| 1.6 | **Respect privacy** | collect only necessary data and protect it |
| 1.7 | **Honour confidentiality** | do not disclose employer or client secrets |

**Professional responsibilities (2.x):** strive for high quality in both process and products; maintain high standards of professional competence, conduct and ethical practice; know and respect existing rules about professional work; accept and provide appropriate professional review; give comprehensive and thorough evaluations of computer systems and their impacts, including risks; perform work only in areas of competence; foster public awareness and understanding of computing; access computing and communication resources only when authorised; and design and implement systems that are robustly and usably secure.
**Leadership principles (3.x):** ensure that the public good is the central concern; articulate, encourage acceptance of and evaluate fulfilment of social responsibilities; manage personnel and resources to enhance the quality of working life; create opportunities for members to grow as professionals.

### Other codes

- **ACM/IEEE-CS Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional Practice** (1999): eight principles: **Public** (act consistently with the public interest), **Client and Employer**, **Product**, **Judgment**, **Management**, **Profession**, **Colleagues**, **Self** (lifelong learning). The public principle comes first.
- **IEEE Code of Ethics:** commits members to safety, health and welfare of the public, avoiding conflicts of interest, honesty in claims, rejecting bribery, improving understanding of technology, maintaining competence, seeking and giving honest criticism, and non-discrimination.
- **BCS Code of Conduct** (British Computer Society): public interest, professional competence and integrity, duty to relevant authority, duty to the profession.
- **Nepal:** engineers are regulated by the **Nepal Engineering Council** (with its own code), and organisations such as the **Computer Association of Nepal (CAN)** and the **Nepal Telecommunications Authority** set sector norms; many companies have internal codes of conduct. Check the current documents.

### Contents of a typical code

Honesty and integrity; competence; confidentiality; respect for law; avoidance of conflicts of interest; respect for intellectual property; protection of privacy; responsibility for public safety; non-discrimination and respect for people; and procedure for reporting violations.

### Strengths and limits of codes

*Strengths:* they set a standard, support individuals against pressure from managers, and show the public that the profession takes ethics seriously. *Limits:* they are general and cannot cover every case; they conflict sometimes (loyalty to the employer versus the public); they have little force without enforcement; and "ethics washing" (a code on the wall but not in practice) is common. A code is **a guide for judgement, not a substitute for it**.

## 6.3 Responsibilities of IT professionals

An IT professional is responsible to many parties. When duties conflict, the **public interest** comes first.

| Toward | Responsibilities |
|---|---|
| **The public and society** | safety, privacy, accessibility; honest information about risks; refusal to build harmful systems; care for the environment |
| **Employer and clients** | competent, timely work; honest estimates; confidentiality; avoiding conflicts of interest; no misuse of company resources; delivering what was promised |
| **Users** | usable, secure, reliable products; clear terms; informed consent; support and updates |
| **Colleagues and team** | respect, fair credit for work, honest review, mentoring, no harassment or discrimination |
| **The profession** | uphold standards; share knowledge; report misconduct; resist unethical pressure |
| **Oneself** | continuous learning; honesty about own limits; professional well-being; refusal to claim fake qualifications |

### Eight core responsibilities in practice

1. **Competence and quality:** accept only tasks you can do; test, review and document. *Doing poor work is an ethical failure, not only a technical one.*
2. **Safety and reliability:** think about failure modes; use safeguards (human override, fail-safe design) in critical systems.
3. **Security:** treat security as a duty: hash and salt passwords, patch vulnerabilities, apply least privilege, never leave default credentials, report breaches to those affected.
4. **Privacy and data protection:** collect only necessary data (data minimisation), say why (purpose limitation), get consent, store securely, delete when no longer needed.
5. **Honesty:** truthful in estimates, claims, advertising and reports; no hidden features (back doors, trackers); admit mistakes.
6. **Respect for intellectual property:** follow licences, credit sources, do not pirate software, and do not copy code you have no right to copy.
7. **Fairness and accessibility:** avoid discrimination in algorithms and hiring, design for screen readers, low-end phones, slow connections and Nepali (Unicode) users.
8. **Accountability and learning:** accept responsibility for the results of your work; keep skills current; report problems early, in writing.

::: example Worked example: a deadline against a safety problem
You are the tester of a hospital app. Two days before release you find that the drug-dose calculator rounds wrongly for children. The manager says, "Release now, fix later; the contract date is fixed."
**Duties in conflict:** loyalty to employer and client (deadline) versus safety of patients (public). **Response:** (1) document the defect with examples; (2) tell the manager in writing and propose options: a quick fix, a limited release with the calculator disabled, or a delay; (3) if the manager still refuses, escalate to a senior person or the client; (4) do not hide the problem. The ACM principle "avoid harm" and the SE code's "Public" principle place patient safety above the schedule.
:::

## 6.4 Ethical decision-making in IT

An **ethical dilemma** is a situation in which every available choice has an ethical cost: two duties conflict, or any action harms someone. Pressure, time limits and loyalty make clear thinking harder, so professionals use a method.

### A seven-step model

1. **Recognise the issue.** Is something unfair, harmful, dishonest or illegal? Name it in one sentence.
2. **Get the facts.** What happened, who is involved, what is known and not known? Do not decide on rumour.
3. **Identify the stakeholders.** Users, customers, employees, the employer, the public, yourself, future users.
4. **List the options.** Include a "third way" (a compromise that avoids both harms), not only yes or no.
5. **Evaluate each option** with several lenses: *law and policy* (is it allowed?), *code of ethics* (what do ACM/IEEE say?), *consequences* (who benefits and who is hurt?), *duties and rights* (does it respect persons and promises?), *character* (what would a person I admire do?), *fairness* (would I accept it if I were on the other side?).
6. **Decide and act.** Choose the best option, discuss with a trusted senior or the ethics office, and **document** your reasoning.
7. **Review.** What happened? What would you do differently, and what rule or process should change?

**Quick tests:** the **publicity test** (would I be comfortable if it were reported in the news?), the **mirror test** (could I explain it to my family and to myself?), the **universal test** (what if everyone did this?), and the **reversibility test** (would I accept it if I were the affected person?).

::: example Worked example: a client asks you to hide a flaw
A client's online shop stores customer card numbers in plain text. The client says, "Do not tell customers. It will damage our brand; just add a better firewall."
**Facts:** the data is unencrypted; no breach has yet been proved. **Stakeholders:** customers (financial loss), client (reputation and legal liability), you (honesty and licence), other users of the payment system. **Options:** obey and stay silent; fix the storage (encryption, tokenisation) and advise the client to notify if a breach is suspected; refuse the work; report to a regulator. **Evaluation:** silence fails honesty, harm avoidance and probably the law; fixing and advising respects confidentiality, avoids harm and helps the client; refusing protects you but leaves customers at risk; reporting is for the case of continuing serious harm after internal steps. **Decision:** fix and advise, documented in writing, with the regulator as a later option.
:::

### IT professional malpractice

**Malpractice** is professional misconduct or negligence: failure to use the skill and care that a reasonably competent professional would use, which harms someone. Related terms: **negligence** (careless failure to take proper care), **misconduct** (deliberate wrongdoing), **breach of duty** (failure to meet an expected standard), and **liability** (legal responsibility for the loss).

**Forms of IT malpractice:**

| Form | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Negligent design and testing** | releasing safety-critical software without proper tests; ignoring known bugs |
| **Poor security practice** | storing passwords in plain text; leaving databases open; not patching |
| **Breach of confidentiality** | selling or leaking customer data; looking at private records out of curiosity |
| **Misrepresentation** | claiming skills, certificates or experience one does not have; false progress reports; inflated cost or benefit claims |
| **Failure to disclose** | hiding a known defect, breach or conflict of interest |
| **Intellectual property violation** | using pirated software; copying code against its licence |
| **Abuse of access** | an administrator reading colleagues' email; insider trading on information |
| **Fraud and sabotage** | logic bombs; data manipulation; embezzlement; "kill switches" |
| **Deceptive practices** | rigging tests (the Volkswagen emissions software), dark patterns |
| **Abandonment** | leaving a client's system unmaintained without notice |

**Real cases (check the sources before quoting details in an exam):**

| Case | What happened | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **Therac-25** (1985 to 1987) | a radiation therapy machine with software race conditions and no hardware interlocks gave massive overdoses to at least six patients, some fatal | test safety-critical software; keep hardware safeguards; investigate complaints |
| **Boeing 737 MAX** (2018 and 2019) | the MCAS flight-control software, relying on one sensor, was a factor in two crashes (Lion Air, 189 dead; Ethiopian Airlines, 157 dead) | redundancy, honest disclosure to regulators and pilots, no schedule pressure over safety |
| **Volkswagen emissions** (2015) | engineers wrote software that detected tests and cut pollution only during tests, on about eleven million diesel cars | engineers, not only managers, are responsible when they write deceptive code |
| **Equifax breach** (2017) | an unpatched web-server vulnerability exposed personal data of about 147 million people | patch management is an ethical duty |
| **Knight Capital** (2012) | an incomplete software deployment sent erroneous trades and lost about 440 million US dollars in 45 minutes | deployment discipline and rollback plans |

**Consequences of malpractice:** harm to people; loss of jobs and licences; legal liability (fines, compensation, imprisonment for fraud); company collapse; loss of trust in the whole profession.

**Preventing malpractice:** codes and training; code review and independent testing; change management; clear contracts and documentation; whistle-blowing channels; audits; insurance (professional indemnity); a culture in which raising concerns is welcome.

### Common ethical issues for IT users

Not only professionals but every user of computers and the internet faces ethical choices.

| Issue | What it means | Right practice |
|---|---|---|
| **Piracy and software licences** | using cracked software, copying paid courses, sharing paid books | buy, use free/open-source or student licences; respect licence terms |
| **Plagiarism and academic honesty** | passing off others' work or AI output as your own | cite, disclose AI use (see 5.4) |
| **Privacy** | collecting, sharing or searching for information about others without consent | ask consent; protect others' data and images; do not share private chats or photographs |
| **Passwords and account sharing** | sharing passwords, using others' accounts, weak passwords | unique strong passwords; two-factor authentication |
| **Cyberbullying and harassment** | humiliating or threatening people online | do not take part; report; support the victim |
| **Misinformation and rumours** | forwarding unverified or doctored content | verify before sharing; correct mistakes |
| **Hacking and unauthorised access** | entering systems without permission, even "just to test" | only with written permission (ethical hacking) |
| **Phishing, fraud and scams** | tricking people to reveal money or data | verify links and senders; never share OTPs |
| **Digital divide and accessibility** | unequal access to devices, internet, skills | design for low bandwidth; help others learn |
| **Screen time and addiction** | compulsive use of games and social media | set limits; take breaks |
| **E-waste and energy** | discarded devices and large data centres | repair, reuse, recycle; efficient code |
| **AI ethics** | bias, deepfakes, copyright of training data, over-reliance | verify outputs; declare use; never create deceptive fakes |

### Intellectual property and software licences (summary)

| IP type | Protects | Software example |
|---|---|---|
| **Copyright** | expression (source code, text, images); arises automatically; not the idea | a program's source and its documentation |
| **Patent** | new, useful, non-obvious inventions | a novel algorithm in a device (software patents differ by country) |
| **Trademark** | names, logos, slogans | the name "Daraz" and its logo |
| **Trade secret** | confidential commercial information | a private ranking algorithm |

| Licence | Rights | Obligation |
|---|---|---|
| Proprietary (EULA) | use only | no copying, modification or resale |
| **MIT / BSD** | use, modify, redistribute, even commercially | keep the copyright notice |
| **Apache 2.0** | like MIT plus patent grant | notices; state changes |
| **GPL** (copyleft) | use, modify, redistribute | derivative works must be GPL with source |
| **Creative Commons** (for content) | share and adapt as the licence allows | attribution; share-alike if SA |

**Fair use / fair dealing** allows limited use for criticism, review, teaching or research with credit. In Nepal the **Copyright Act, 2059 (2002)** protects works including computer programs, and the **Patent, Design and Trademark Act, 2022 (1965)** covers inventions, designs and marks. Check the current texts and amendments.

### Nepal's cyber-related laws (overview)

| Law | Relevance |

| **Individual Privacy Act, 2075 (2018)** | privacy of person, home, documents, correspondence, data; rules for collecting and using personal information |

## 6.5 Whistle-blowing and professional integrity

**Whistle-blowing** is the disclosure by a member of an organisation of illegal, unethical or dangerous practices to people or bodies who can act on them. **Internal** whistle-blowing reports to higher management or an ethics office; **external** reports to a regulator, the police, the media or the public.

### When is whistle-blowing justified?

Common criteria (Richard De George): (1) the harm to the public is **serious and considerable**; (2) the employee has **reported it to their superior** and used the **internal channels**; (3) the employee has **good documented evidence** that a reasonable person would find convincing; and (4) there is good reason to believe that going public will **prevent** the harm. Many add: the whistle-blower acts from **public interest, not revenge or reward**.

### Steps for a responsible whistle-blower

1. Be sure of the facts; write down dates, documents, names and what you saw.
2. Read the company's policy and your contract (confidentiality clauses do not usually cover crimes, but check).
3. Raise the matter internally with a supervisor, then with ethics, compliance, audit or a senior officer, **in writing**.
4. If nothing changes and public safety or serious crime is involved, go to the regulator or the police.
5. Seek advice from a lawyer, a union or a professional body before going public.
6. Do not steal or access data you are not authorised to see; limit disclosure to what the public needs.
7. Prepare for retaliation (dismissal, harassment), and check the legal protection available in your country.

### Examples

- **Edward Snowden** (2013) disclosed documents about US government mass surveillance programmes. Supporters call him a whistle-blower who exposed mass surveillance; the US government treats the disclosure as a crime. The case shows that **law and ethics may conflict** and that methods matter.
- **Frances Haugen** (2021) gave documents to regulators and journalists about the internal research of the social network company Facebook (Meta) on the harms of its products, and testified before the US Senate.
- **Engineers and flight-safety staff** in aviation and automotive cases have also raised concerns that were ignored, showing the value of internal channels that work.

**Protections:** many countries have whistle-blower laws; in Nepal, protection exists only in parts of the law (for example, in anti-corruption and good-governance rules) and is weaker than in many countries, so personal risk is high. Check the current law.

### Professional integrity

**Integrity** means that your values, words and actions agree, even when no one is watching. In IT it means: telling the truth about progress and quality; refusing bribes and favours; declaring conflicts of interest; not accepting credit for others' work; keeping promises; being open about mistakes; respecting confidentiality; and standing by the code of ethics under pressure.

| Test of integrity | Ethical response |
|---|---|
| A vendor offers you a laptop for choosing their software | decline and report; declare a conflict of interest |
| You are told to fake a test report to pass an audit | refuse; offer to fix the problem; report if pressed |
| You made a mistake that deleted data | report immediately and help to recover |
| A friend asks you to look up a person's private records | refuse; explain that access is for official use only |

## 6.6 Workplace ethical challenges in IT

| Challenge | Typical situations | Ethical response |
|---|---|---|
| **Conflict of interest** | a relative's company bids for a contract you evaluate; side jobs for a competitor | declare; step aside from the decision |
| **Bribery, gifts and kickbacks** | an offer of cash or expensive gifts for a contract | refuse; follow policy; report |
| **Pressure to cut corners** | skip testing, ignore security to meet a deadline | state the risk in writing; propose options; escalate |
| **Confidentiality and data misuse** | using customer data for private benefit; copying code or client lists when leaving a job | respect the contract; take nothing that belongs to the employer |
| **Discrimination and harassment** | biased hiring, harassment in chat | respect, report through channels |
| **Overwork and "crunch"** | unpaid overtime, burnout | negotiate scope; protect health; managers plan realistically |
| **Misuse of company resources** | mining cryptocurrency on office servers; private use of licences | use only as authorised |
| **Monitoring and surveillance** | employer tracks keystrokes, screens, location | transparency, proportionality, consent, policy |
| **Whistle-blowing dilemmas** | discovering fraud or unsafe code | see 6.5 |
| **AI and automation** | biased hiring tools, replacing staff, using AI without disclosure | test for bias, human oversight, be transparent |
| **Outsourcing and offshoring** | poor pay or conditions at contractors; unrealistic fixed-price bids | fair contracts; honest estimates |
| **Honesty in marketing and CVs** | exaggerating features; false experience | claim only what is true |
| **Open source and licensing** | using GPL code in a closed product | check licences; get legal advice |

::: example Worked example: moving to a competitor
Sunita is leaving her job to join a rival company. On her last day she is tempted to copy her project code and client contact list "to be useful at the new job".
**Analysis:** the code and client list belong to her employer (copyright, trade secret, confidentiality). Taking them is a breach of contract, may be a crime, and shows poor integrity. **Right action:** hand over the work properly, document it, return all access, and bring only her general skills and knowledge to the new employer.
:::

::: note Answers and worked solutions
**1.** Ethics is the study of right and wrong and the principles for deciding; morals are beliefs about right and wrong held by a person or society; law is the rule set enforced by the state. Ethics is wider than law, enforced by conscience and professional bodies rather than courts, and changes slowly; law is narrower and changes by amendment. An act can be legal and unethical.
**2.** Because software works at great scale and speed, users cannot see inside it, IT staff can access private data, the same technology can be used for good or harm, safety-critical systems depend on it, and there are few external checks.
**3.** Utilitarianism judges an action by its overall consequences (the greatest good for the greatest number); a company decides whether to monitor employees by weighing productivity against stress. Deontology judges by duties and rules, treating people as ends not means; secret monitoring violates the duty of honesty and respect even if it increases profit.
**4.** A written statement of values and rules that a profession expects of its members. ACM general principles: contribute to society and human well-being; avoid harm; be honest and trustworthy; be fair and non-discriminatory; respect the work needed to produce new ideas; respect privacy; honour confidentiality.
**5.** Public: safety, privacy, honest information about risks. Employer: competent work, honest estimates, confidentiality, no conflicts of interest. Profession: maintain standards, share knowledge, report misconduct.
**6.** Recognise the issue; get the facts; identify stakeholders; list options; evaluate with law, code, consequences, rights and fairness; decide, act and document; review. Check with the publicity and mirror tests.
**7.** Failure to give the care and skill expected of a competent professional, causing harm. Examples: releasing untested safety-critical software; plain-text passwords; leaking customer data; false claims of skill. Real case: Therac-25 radiation overdoses (1985 to 1987) from untested software with no hardware safeguards.
**8.** Piracy (use legal or open-source software); plagiarism (cite sources and disclose AI use); privacy violations (get consent); cyberbullying (do not participate, report); misinformation (verify before sharing); weak or shared passwords (unique passwords and two-factor authentication).
**9.** Disclosure of illegal, unethical or dangerous practices to those who can stop them. It is justified when the harm is serious, internal channels have been tried, evidence is good, and disclosure is likely to prevent harm. Steps: verify facts, read the policy, report in writing internally, then to a regulator if necessary, take legal advice, disclose only what is needed, prepare for retaliation.
**10.** A situation in which personal interests could bias professional judgement. Example: evaluating a vendor owned by your brother. Handle by declaring the conflict and withdrawing from the decision.
**11.** Dilemma: duty to the employer (schedule) versus duty to users (security, avoiding harm). The tester documents the flaw and its risk in writing, proposes options (a fix, a delayed launch, a limited launch with the vulnerable feature disabled), escalates to senior management or the security officer if refused, and does not hide the issue; if serious harm is likely and internal routes fail, the regulator or an external route may be considered. The ACM principles avoid harm, honesty and the public-first principle support this.
:::

### 6.7.2 Concord (subject-verb agreement)

**Concord** (agreement) means that the verb must agree with its subject in **number** (singular or plural) and **person**. It also applies to pronouns and their antecedents. The main rule: a singular subject takes a singular verb (*The server **is** down*); a plural subject takes a plural verb (*The servers **are** down*). In the present tense, third-person singular verbs take **-s/-es** (*she writes, it works*), but **I** and **you** take the base form (*I write, you write*).

**Rules and examples**

1. **Subjects joined by *and* take a plural verb:** *The manager and the developer **are** present.* **Exception:** when the two nouns name **one person or idea**, or are felt as one: *Bread and butter **is** my breakfast. The founder and CEO **has** resigned.* (one person)
2. **Each, every, either, neither, one, anyone, someone, everybody, nobody** take a **singular** verb: *Each of the students **has** a laptop. Neither of the answers **is** correct. Everyone **was** late.*
3. **Either ... or, neither ... nor, not only ... but also:** the verb agrees with the **nearer subject:** *Neither the manager nor the **employees** were told. Either the students or the **teacher** is responsible.*
4. **Phrases such as *with, along with, together with, as well as, in addition to, besides, except*** do not change the number; the verb agrees with the **first** subject: *The manager, along with his team, **is** attending. The teacher, as well as the students, **was** present.*
5. **Collective nouns** (team, committee, class, staff, audience, family, government): singular when the group acts as a **unit**, plural when the **members** act separately: *The committee **has** approved the plan. The committee **are** divided in their opinions.* (more common in British English)
6. **Nouns plural in form but singular in meaning:** *news, mathematics, physics, economics, ethics, politics, athletics, measles, the United States:* *Ethics **is** a branch of philosophy. The news **was** shocking.*
7. **Nouns always plural:** *scissors, trousers, jeans, glasses (spectacles), clothes, police, people, cattle, belongings:* *The police **are** investigating. My glasses **are** broken.* (but *a pair of scissors **is** on the table*).
8. **Quantity, money, distance, time and weight as a unit:** singular: *Ten thousand rupees **is** a large sum. Five kilometres **is** not far. Two hours **is** enough.*
9. **A number of** takes a plural verb, **the number of** takes a singular verb: *A number of students **are** absent. The number of absentees **is** high.*
10. **Fractions, percentages and "some, all, most, half, none" + *of***: the verb agrees with the noun after *of*: *Half of the cake **is** left. Half of the students **are** absent. Sixty per cent of the data **is/are** correct. All of the work **is** done. None of the books **were/was** damaged* (both accepted; plural is common).
11. **There is / there are:** the verb agrees with the noun that follows: *There **is** a bug. There **are** three bugs.*
12. **Relative pronouns (who, which, that)** take the number of the antecedent: *He is one of the engineers who **have** signed the petition* (who = the engineers). *He is the only one of the engineers who **has** signed.*
13. **"Many a", "more than one"** with a singular noun take a singular verb: *Many a student **has** failed. More than one candidate **was** disqualified.*
14. **Gerunds, infinitives and clauses as subjects:** singular: *Writing clean code **is** a skill. What he said **was** true.* But a clause with a plural noun complement: *What we need **are** better tools* (informal: is).
15. **Titles and names** of books, companies and organisations: singular: *"Gulliver's Travels" **is** a satire. Tata Steel **has** announced...*
16. **The + adjective** (the poor, the rich, the young) takes a plural verb: *The poor **need** help.*
17. **The word *data*:** formally plural (*The data **are** stored*); in everyday and technical English *data* is also used as singular (*The data **is** stored*). Follow your teacher's or publisher's style, and be consistent. *Media, criteria, phenomena* are plural (singular: medium, criterion, phenomenon).
18. **Subject placed after the verb (inversion):** *Here **comes** the bus. In the box **were** two keys.*
19. **Don't be misled by words between subject and verb:** *The quality of the programs **is** high.* (quality, not programs)
20. **Pronoun agreement:** *Every student must bring **his or her** (or **their**) ID card.* Singular *they* is accepted for a person of unknown gender.

::: key Mini-checklist for concord
Find the real subject; ignore phrases in between; decide singular or plural; match the verb (and pronoun); check special cases (collective nouns, *either/neither*, *a number of*, plural-form nouns).
:::

### 6.8.1 "The Necklace" by Guy de Maupassant

#### The writer and the text

**Guy de Maupassant** (1850 to 1893) was a French writer, a pupil of Gustave Flaubert and one of the masters of the modern **short story**. His work belongs to **realism and naturalism**: it describes ordinary, often poor or middle-class people with a cool, observant eye. He wrote about 300 stories and six novels; "Boule de Suif" and "The Necklace" ("La Parure") are among the best known. "The Necklace" was first published in the Paris newspaper *Le Gaulois* in **February 1884**. It is famous for its **twist ending** and its comment on vanity, class and honesty.

#### Summary

1. **The unhappy wife.** **Mathilde Loisel** is a pretty, charming young woman born, "as if by a mistake of destiny", into a family of clerks. She has no dowry, no hope of marrying a rich man, so she marries a **minor clerk in the Ministry of Education**. She dresses plainly but feels unhappy, as if she had fallen from a higher class. She suffers from the poverty of her flat and its shabby furniture and dreams of servants, silk, silver and delicious dinners.
2. **The rich friend.** She has a wealthy friend, **Madame Forestier**, from her convent school, whom she no longer likes to visit because she returns home in tears.
3. **The invitation.** One evening her husband comes home triumphant with an invitation to a **ball at the Ministry** (given by the minister and his wife). Instead of joy she is angry: she has nothing to wear. Her husband, upset by her tears, offers the **400 francs** he had saved to buy a gun for hunting trips. She buys a dress, but is still unhappy because she has **no jewels**. Her husband suggests she borrows from Madame Forestier.
4. **The borrowing.** Madame Forestier lets her choose from a box; Mathilde chooses a **diamond necklace** (a "superb" one in a black satin case).
5. **The triumph.** At the ball Mathilde is the prettiest woman there: every man looks at her, the minister notices her, and she dances in a cloud of happiness. They leave at four in the morning; she refuses her husband's humble coat and rushes into the street so that the other guests will not see her poverty.
6. **The loss.** At home she looks in the mirror and finds that the **necklace is gone**. They search the cab route, report to the police and the cab company, and put an advertisement in the newspapers; nothing is found. Her husband writes to Madame Forestier that the clasp of the necklace is broken and that it is being repaired, to **gain time**.
7. **The replacement.** In a shop of the Palais-Royal they find a diamond necklace like it for **40,000 francs** (the jeweller offers it for **36,000**). Loisel has **18,000 francs** left to him by his father; he **borrows the rest** (a thousand here, five hundred there, signing notes and dealing with moneylenders at ruinous interest), and the necklace is bought and returned to Madame Forestier, who remarks a little coldly that it should have been returned sooner, but does not open the case.
8. **Ten years of hardship.** The couple pay off the debt. They **dismiss the maid**, move to a garret, and Mathilde does the heavy housework, washes the dishes, carries water and haggles with grocers. Her husband works evenings doing accounts and at night copies manuscripts. After **ten years** everything is paid, including the interest. Mathilde has become old, coarse and strong, like the women of poor households, with untidy hair and red hands.
9. **The encounter and the twist.** One Sunday she meets Madame Forestier, still young and beautiful, walking in the Champs-Élysées. Mathilde, now emboldened because the debt is paid, speaks to her and Forestier does not recognise her. Mathilde tells the whole story of the lost necklace and the sacrifices. Madame Forestier, deeply moved, takes her hands and says: **"Oh, my poor Mathilde! But mine was paste. It was worth at the very most five hundred francs!"**

#### Characters

- **Mathilde Loisel (protagonist):** beautiful, vain, discontented, ambitious for luxury; at first selfish and proud, later brave, hardworking and stoic in poverty. A **round, dynamic** character.
- **M. Loisel (husband):** kind, simple, patient, devoted; he sacrifices his savings, inheritance and health, never blaming his wife.
- **Madame Forestier:** a rich friend, generous with her jewels; she is the unwitting cause of the tragedy and is shocked at the end. She is a **foil** to Mathilde.
- **The minister and guests:** the glittering world Mathilde longs for.

#### Themes

- **Vanity and appearance versus reality.** Mathilde values show over substance; the **false** necklace is a symbol of false values.
- **Class and aspiration.** Her unhappiness comes from comparing herself with the rich; ambition destroys her contentment.
- **Honesty and communication.** The whole disaster could have been avoided if Mathilde had **told her friend the truth** about the loss. The failure of communication costs ten years: a direct link to this subject.
- **Pride and its price.** Mathilde's pride prevents her from admitting that she lost the necklace; Madame Forestier's lack of honesty about it (mere paste) is also discovered late.
- **Fate, chance and irony.** A small accident (a lost ornament) changes two lives.
- **Hard work and dignity.** The ten years of labour give Mathilde a quiet strength, so that even in poverty she "did her duty".
- **Sacrifice and loyalty:** M. Loisel's support.

#### Critical analysis and literary devices

- **Irony:** the central device. *Situational irony:* the borrowed jewel was worthless, so the sacrifice was for nothing; the one night of glory costs ten years. *Dramatic irony:* the reader sees Mathilde's vanity before she does. *Verbal irony* in the narrator's gentle comments on her "fall".
- **Twist (surprise) ending:** the last line reverses the meaning of the whole story; it is a classic example of **O. Henry-like** endings, though Maupassant's is bitter rather than comic.
- **Symbolism:** the necklace (illusion, false value, the cost of vanity); the ball (the dream of another class); the garret and the red hands (reality).
- **Structure:** the plot has clear stages: exposition (discontent), complication (invitation, loss), rising action (debt, ten years), climax (meeting), resolution (twist). It is economical, with the main turning points in a few lines.
- **Narrative point of view:** third-person, omniscient, detached and ironic; the narrator never preaches but lets facts judge.
- **Realism and detail:** concrete amounts (400, 18,000, 36,000, 40,000 francs, 500 francs), places (Palais-Royal, Champs-Élysées, rue des Martyrs) give the story a documentary truth.
- **Characterisation by action and contrast** (Mathilde before and after, Mathilde and Forestier).
- **Interpretations:** a moral tale against vanity; a social critique of a society that makes women dependent on appearance and marriage; a tragedy of fate. An unexpected view: Mathilde grows through suffering, and the "waste" is also the making of her character.
- **Link to professional ethics and communication:** honesty (the first duty of a professional), the cost of hiding a mistake, the importance of verifying facts (no one checked that the jewel was real), and integrity in paying one's debts.

#### Key vocabulary

*dowry, clerk, pretty, vanity, envy, humiliation, paste (imitation jewel), clasp, usurer (moneylender), garret, drudgery, haggle, ruinous, stoic.*

### 6.8.2 "The Digital Citizen" by Luigi Ceccarini

#### The writer and the text

**Luigi Ceccarini** is an Italian political scientist, Professor of Politics and head of the School of Political and Social Studies at the **University of Urbino Carlo Bo**, and co-editor of the journal *Comunicazione Politica*. The reading comes from his book ***The Digital Citizen(ship): Politics and Democracy in the Networked Society*** (Edward Elgar Publishing, 2021, an open-access title). The textbook prints a short extract; the text is **academic expository writing**, more abstract than the other readings, and it asks what it means to be a **citizen** when much of public life moves online. *Check your textbook copy: the paragraphs below follow the book's central argument and the ideas of its introduction; use your own copy for exact wording.*

#### Summary of the main argument

1. **Citizenship is changing.** Traditional citizenship means membership of a political community with rights (to vote, to protection) and duties (to obey laws, pay taxes, take part). The internet and social media change **how** people take part: they follow politics through feeds, discuss in groups, sign online petitions, share information, join campaigns and watch their governments. The spread of **e-participation** shows a change in the **way citizens belong** to a community.
2. **Beyond representative politics.** Representative democracy (electing parties and leaders) is under strain: trust in parties and institutions has fallen and people increasingly act outside them. Ceccarini describes this as **post-representative** politics, in which new, flexible forms of participation exist beside voting.
3. **The "good citizen" and the "monitoring citizen".** The ideal of the **good citizen** (informed, dutiful, loyal, a regular voter) is giving way to the **monitoring citizen** who keeps watch on those in power, checks facts, criticises and reacts to events through short, issue-based actions (a hashtag, a petition, a boycott). The internet makes such **monitoring democracy** cheap and easy: information about the political community can be shared at lower cost.
4. **Individualised participation.** Online participation is often **individualised**: people act on causes they choose for personal reasons, rather than as members of a party or union with a fixed ideology. The commitment to the common good is thus pursued through an **individual** perspective. It is "post-bureaucratic" (not organised by big institutions) and "post-ideological".
5. **Young people and activism.** Online groups and political conversations, even in forums not about politics, can encourage political engagement and lead to **offline** action. Young people are often **more politically involved** than some earlier research suggested, but they take part in new ways.
6. **Hybrid forms.** Online action is mixed ("hybridised") with traditional modes: the press, civic associations and street protest. Citizens move in an environment of interpersonal and media communication, **old and new**, where norms and meanings are produced.
7. **Communities beyond borders.** Digital citizens create or join communities that **cross state boundaries**, start or join debates, advertise support for causes and promote national and global campaigns. The digital citizen is therefore both **local** and **global**.
8. **Opportunities and risks.** Opportunities: wider access to information, voice for people who were unheard, faster mobilisation, more transparency. Risks (discussed in the book and in wider debate): misinformation, polarisation in "echo chambers", surveillance by governments and companies, shallow "clicktivism", the digital divide, and the gap between online noise and real decisions.

#### Key concepts

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Citizenship** | membership of a political community with rights and duties |
| **E-participation / digital citizenship** | taking part in civic and political life through digital tools; also responsible, informed, safe use of technology |
| **Post-representative politics** | politics in which participation goes beyond elected representatives |
| **Monitoring (monitorial) citizen** | a citizen who scans events and acts when something matters |
| **Individualised participation** | taking part as an individual, by choice and cause, not as a loyal member of an organisation |
| **Disintermediation** | direct communication without journalists, parties or other middlemen |
| **Hybridisation** | mixing of online and offline, new and old forms |

#### Themes

- **Participation and democracy in the digital age.**
- **Individual freedom and collective responsibility.**
- **The generational shift:** the young have different repertoires of action.
- **Information, power and accountability:** transparency helps citizens watch those in power.
- **Links to this unit:** digital rights and duties, privacy, misinformation, and the ethical obligation to be a responsible user and a responsible builder of digital systems.

#### Critical analysis and rhetorical notes

- **Register:** formal academic prose with long sentences, abstract nouns (participation, mobilisation, mediation) and references in brackets (for example Bimber 2003; Isin and Ruppert 2015; Beyer 2014); the aim is to place the argument within a research discussion.
- **Structure:** claim, explanation of concepts, evidence from research, and a view of the consequences; the text signals relationships with connectives (moreover, thus, in particular).
- **Strengths:** precise concepts (good citizen, monitoring citizen), connection of theory with the concrete activity of internet users, balanced between enthusiasm and warning.
- **Critique:** an academic argument can be abstract for students; Italian and European examples are not always the same as Nepal's. Nepal's own experience shows both sides: the 2006 and later movements, the use of social media for relief after the 2015 earthquake, online petitions, and the September 2025 youth protests that began over a social-media ban and changed the government (check news sources for details) show digital participation; the digital divide, rumour and online abuse show its risks.
- **Link to professional ethics:** developers build the spaces where citizenship now happens, so they have duties of honesty, privacy, accessibility and fairness.

#### Key vocabulary

*citizenship, participation, democracy, representative, mobilisation, mediation, disintermediation, activism, networked, hybrid, civic, accountability.*
