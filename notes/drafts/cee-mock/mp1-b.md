**81.** (b) — 4.4 g ÷ 44 g mol⁻¹ = 0.1 mol; 0.1 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 6.02 × 10²² molecules.
**82.** (b) — H₂ = 2 mol, O₂ = 0.5 mol; O₂ is limiting, so 0.5 mol O₂ gives 1 mol H₂O = 18 g (1.5 mol H₂ is left over).
**83.** (b) — n = 3, l = 2 is the 3d subshell: five orbitals (m = −2 … +2) × 2 electrons = 10.
**84.** (d) — Balmer lines end on n = 2 (visible region); jumps to n = 1 are Lyman and to n = 3 are Paschen.
**85.** (c) — N has a stable half-filled 2p³ set, so its IE (1402 kJ mol⁻¹) exceeds that of O (1314), where the paired 2p electron is easier to remove.
**86.** (a) — Six bond pairs and no lone pair on S need six hybrid orbitals (one s, three p, two d): sp³d², octahedral.
**87.** (d) — In o-nitrophenol the –OH and –NO₂ are adjacent and H-bond within one molecule (chelation), so it is more volatile than the para isomer.
**88.** (c) — K₂Cr₂O₇: 2 + 2x − 14 = 0, x = +6. Na₂S₄O₆: 2 + 4y − 12 = 0, y = +2.5.
**89.** (b) — Graham's law: r(H₂)/r(O₂) = √(M(O₂)/M(H₂)) = √(32/2) = 4.
**90.** (a) — Anhydrous CaCl₂ (like solid NaOH) deliquesces. Washing soda effloresces; conc. H₂SO₄ is hygroscopic but stays a liquid.
**91.** (d) — x = Δn(gas) = moles of gaseous products − reactants = 2 − 4 = −2.
**92.** (d) — [OH⁻] = 2 × 0.001 = 2 × 10⁻³ M; pOH = 3 − 0.30 = 2.7; pH = 14 − 2.7 = 11.3.
**93.** (c) — 0.1 M H₂SO₄ (dibasic) = 0.2 N; N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ gives 0.2 × 25 = 0.2 × V, so V = 25 mL.
**94.** (c) — 12.5 % left = 1/8 = (1/2)³, i.e. three half-lives: 3 × 30 = 90 min.
**95.** (c) — Q = 2 × 965 = 1930 C = 0.02 F; Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag, so 0.02 mol × 108 g mol⁻¹ = 2.16 g.
**96.** (a) — ΔG = ΔH − TΔS becomes negative only when TΔS exceeds ΔH, i.e. above T = ΔH/ΔS.
**97.** (d) — α: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th; two β⁻ raise Z by 2: ²³⁴₉₂U, same Z as the parent, so an isotope.
**98.** (a) — D₂O slows fast neutrons by collisions while absorbing very few of them, so it is a good moderator.
**99.** (b) — O₃ is a resonance hybrid of two structures (one O=O, one O–O), so bond order 1.5; a lone pair on the central O bends it (about 117°).
**100.** (a) — P₄ + 3NaOH + 3H₂O → PH₃ + 3NaH₂PO₂ (sodium hypophosphite); CO₂ or N₂ keeps air out.
**101.** (d) — SO₂ + 2H₂O → H₂SO₄ + 2[H]; the reduced dye is re-oxidized by air. Cl₂ bleaches by oxidation, permanently.
**102.** (c) — Sulphide particles are wetted by the oil (pine oil) and rise with the froth; gangue is wetted by water and sinks.
**103.** (b) — Calomel is mercury(I) chloride, Hg₂Cl₂; HgCl₂ is corrosive sublimate.
**104.** (a) — Fe²⁺ is d⁶; the large octahedral splitting by CN⁻ pairs all six in t₂g (t₂g⁶ e_g⁰), so it is diamagnetic.
**105.** (b) — Zn (E° = −0.76 V) is oxidized before Fe (E° = −0.44 V), giving sacrificial (cathodic) protection.
**106.** (c) — Vitamin B₁₂ (cyanocobalamin) has Co at its centre; haemoglobin contains Fe and chlorophyll contains Mg.
**107.** (d) — Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase exports 3 Na⁺ and imports 2 K⁺ per ATP, keeping the cell interior negative.
**108.** (a) — Number the four-carbon chain from the C bearing –OH: OH on C-1, methyl on C-3.
**109.** (c) — Stability rises 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl because more alkyl groups donate electrons (+I effect and hyperconjugation).
**110.** (c) — Butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol, 2-methylpropan-1-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol; counting the three ethers as well gives 7.
**111.** (b) — Iso-octane (2,2,4-trimethylpentane) = 100 and n-heptane = 0; n-hexadecane (cetane) is the reference for cetane number.
**112.** (a) — Only a terminal alkyne has an acidic ≡C–H (sp carbon); it forms silver but-1-ynide. But-2-yne has no such H.
**113.** (d) — Peroxide (Kharasch) effect: a free-radical addition, Br· adds to the terminal carbon, giving the anti-Markovnikov product.
**114.** (a) — In SN2 the nucleophile attacks from the side opposite the leaving group in one step (Walden inversion); SN1 gives racemization.
**115.** (d) — 2CHCl₃ + O₂ → 2COCl₂ + 2HCl; phosgene (COCl₂) is highly poisonous. A little ethanol is added to destroy it.
**116.** (b) — Boron adds to the less substituted carbon and is replaced by –OH: overall anti-Markovnikov addition of water.
**117.** (d) — Williamson is SN2, so the halide must be primary/methyl; a tert-butyl halide with CH₃O⁻ mainly eliminates to give isobutylene.
**118.** (c) — Cannizzaro needs an aldehyde with no α-hydrogen; C₆H₅CHO gives benzyl alcohol and sodium benzoate.
**119.** (b) — Reactivity follows leaving-group ability, Cl⁻ > RCOO⁻ > RO⁻ > NH₂⁻; amide N donates most by resonance.
**120.** (a) — The test needs CH₃CO– or CH₃CH(OH)–. Propan-1-ol, CH₃CH₂CH₂OH, has neither.
**121.** (c) — RCOCl + H₂ → RCHO + HCl over Pd/BaSO₄; the poisoned catalyst stops reduction at the aldehyde.
**122.** (d) — In water, +I effect and solvation of the ammonium ion together make the order (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > NH₃.
**123.** (a) — 1° amine gives a solid oxamide, 2° a liquid oxamic ester, and 3° does not react.
**124.** (b) — CH₃⁻ adds to the C=O carbon of (CH₃)₂CO; hydrolysis gives the tertiary alcohol (CH₃)₃COH.
**125.** (d) — 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ over V₂O₅ at about 450 °C; Fe is the Haber catalyst and Pt–Rh the Ostwald catalyst.
**126.** (a) — NH₄Cl is treated with lime to recover NH₃ for recycling; the CaCl₂ formed is discarded as by-product.
**127.** (c) — Six-carbon diamine + six-carbon diacid, linked by amide bonds with loss of water. The distractors give nylon-6, terylene and bakelite.
**128.** (b) — NaCN from fusion forms Na₄[Fe(CN)₆], which with Fe³⁺ gives Prussian blue, Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃. Blood red indicates N and S together.
**129.** (d) — The salt CH₃COONa hydrolyses, so the equivalence point is near pH 8–9, within phenolphthalein's range (8.3–10).
**130.** (b) — On heating, NH₄Cl sublimes (dissociates and recombines on a cool surface) while NaCl stays behind.
