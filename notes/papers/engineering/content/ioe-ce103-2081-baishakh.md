@s 1
**Factors governing the selection of a construction material**

| Factor | What is checked |
|---|---|
| Mechanical properties | Strength, stiffness, ductility, toughness, hardness, durability under the expected loads |
| Availability and transport | Local sources (stone, sand, timber, clay) cut haulage cost and delay |
| Cost | First cost, life-cycle cost, maintenance and repair cost |
| Durability and weather resistance | Resistance to rain, frost, heat, chemicals, insects and fire |
| Workability | Ease of cutting, shaping, placing, jointing and finishing |
| Appearance | Colour, texture and finish demanded by the architecture |
| Physical / thermal / acoustic behaviour | Density (self weight), thermal and sound insulation, water absorption |
| Safety, health and environment | Fire rating, toxicity, energy used in production, recyclability, standards and codes |

**Two mechanical properties**

- **Strength** is the maximum stress a material can carry before it yields or fractures (compressive, tensile, shear, flexural). Concrete and stone are chosen for compression (columns, footings), mild steel for tension (reinforcement). Strength decides member sizes.
- **Ductility** is the ability to undergo large plastic (permanent) deformation before rupture, measured by percentage elongation. A ductile material such as mild steel gives visible warning (large deflection, cracks) before failure and redistributes loads, which is why steel is used for earthquake-resistant frames. (Other properties are elasticity, toughness, hardness and creep.)

@s 2
**Why surface dressing is used**

Dressing of stone means working the surface of a quarried block to a required shape, size and finish. It is used:

- to give the stone the required shape and size, with regular beds and joints so blocks sit true in masonry,
- to obtain a smooth, plane bearing surface so that loads are transferred uniformly and the mortar joint thickness is even,
- to improve the appearance of the face (ashlar, chisel-drafted, polished),
- to remove weathered, loose and soft skin and sharp or uneven projections,
- to reduce the surface area exposed to moisture (a smooth surface sheds water and gathers less dirt and growth), and to make the stone easier to handle.

**Deterioration of stone** is caused by the following actions.

| Cause | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Rain and moisture | Water enters pores, dissolves soluble minerals, and in cold climates freezes and expands (frost action), splitting the stone |
| Temperature change | Different minerals expand differently; repeated heating and cooling breaks the stone along grains |
| Chemical action | CO$_2$, SO$_2$ and other gases dissolve in rain to form weak acids that attack limestone and sandstone (acid rain, mortar decay) |
| Salt crystallisation | Soluble salts in pore water crystallise and generate pressure; efflorescence and flaking |
| Wind and abrasion | Erosion by wind-blown sand, running water and traffic |
| Vegetation and organisms | Roots enter cracks; lichens, moss and algae retain moisture and produce acids |
| Poor selection, dressing or laying | Wrong bed orientation (laminae vertical), porous stone, rough surface holding water |
| Fire | Quartz-rich stones crack, limestone calcines to lime |

Preventive measures: select dense, non-porous stone, dress the surface smooth, lay the stone on its natural bed, and apply a coat of water-proof or silicone coating.

@s 3
**First class and second class bricks**

| Property | 1st class brick | 2nd class brick |
|---|---|---|
| Moulding | Table-moulded, burnt in a kiln | Ground-moulded, burnt in a kiln |
| Shape and surface | Uniform, standard size, true rectangular, sharp edges, smooth plane surface | Slightly distorted, rougher surface, edges less sharp |
| Colour and sound | Uniform deep red or copper colour; clear metallic ringing sound when struck | Slightly less uniform; dull sound |
| Compressive strength | Not less than about 10.5 N/mm² | About 7–10 N/mm² |
| Water absorption (24 h) | Not more than 20 % | Not more than 22 % |
| Efflorescence | Nil to slight | Moderate allowed |
| Use | Superstructure of buildings, face work, load-bearing walls, flooring | Walls to be plastered, internal walls, foundation of ordinary buildings |

**Causes of efflorescence.** Efflorescence is the white or grey powdery deposit of salts on a brick or wall surface. Soluble salts (sulphates and nitrates of sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium) are present in the brick earth, in the mortar or cement, in the mixing water, in the soil or from sea air. When the wall becomes damp, water dissolves the salts and carries them to the surface; on evaporation the salts crystallise there. It is more severe with poor-quality, under-burnt and highly absorbent bricks, with poor drainage and where the wall is exposed to rising damp. It spoils the appearance, and the crystallisation pressure can disintegrate the surface and ruin plaster and paint.

**Laboratory test for efflorescence** (IS 3495 type test)

1. Stand a dry brick on end in a shallow dish containing 25 mm depth of distilled water, at room temperature, in a ventilated room.
2. Allow all the water to be absorbed by the brick and to evaporate. Add a further 25 mm of distilled water and again let it evaporate completely.
3. Examine the brick for the white deposit and classify it by the percentage of area covered:

| Rating | Observation |
|---|---|
| Nil | No perceptible deposit |
| Slight | Deposit covers up to 10 % of the area |
| Moderate | 10 to 50 % of the area, no powdering or flaking |
| Heavy | More than 50 %, no powdering or flaking |
| Serious | Heavy deposit with powdering or flaking of the surface |

Good bricks should show nil or slight efflorescence (ratings up to "moderate" for lower grades).

@s 4
**Quick lime (calcium oxide, CaO).** It is the product obtained by burning (calcining) pure limestone at 900–1000 °C, $\text{CaCO}_3\rightarrow\text{CaO}+\text{CO}_2$. Properties: white, porous lumps; combines violently with water (slaking) with large heat and a volume increase of 2 to 2.5 times, $\text{CaO}+\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightarrow\text{Ca(OH)}_2+\text{heat}$; absorbs moisture and CO$_2$ from air and air-slakes into powder; caustic. Uses: for making hydrated lime and lime putty, in lime mortar and lime-sand bricks, whitewash, as a flux in steel making, soil stabilisation and water softening. It is not used directly in masonry, because it must be slaked first.

**Fat lime (high calcium lime, rich lime, pure lime).** It is made from almost pure limestone (more than 95 % CaCO$_3$, little clay). Properties: pure white colour; slakes vigorously and swells up to 2 to 2.5 times its original volume giving a lot of smooth paste; sets and hardens slowly only by absorbing CO$_2$ from the air (non-hydraulic), so it cannot set under water or in thick walls; low compressive strength; shrinks on drying, so it is mixed with sand (1 : 2 to 1 : 3). Uses: plastering and finishing coats, whitewash and distemper, mortar for ordinary (non-structural) masonry in dry places, as a cement admixture to improve workability, and in lime–surkhi mortar.

| | Quick lime | Fat lime |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical state | CaO (unslaked) | Ca(OH)$_2$ after slaking, from > 95 % pure limestone |
| Reaction with water | Violent slaking, heat | Slakes freely, rich paste |
| Setting | Not used directly | Slow, by CO$_2$ only |

**Consistency test of cement (Vicat apparatus, IS 4031 Part 4).** The *standard consistency* is the percentage of water (by mass of cement) that gives a paste which the Vicat plunger penetrates to a point 5 to 7 mm above the bottom of the mould (33 to 35 mm from the top). It is needed before the initial and final setting time and soundness tests.

Apparatus: Vicat apparatus with the 10 mm diameter, 50 mm long plunger (total moving mass 300 g), Vicat mould (80 mm diameter at base, 40 mm high) on a glass plate, balance, measuring cylinder, trowel, stop watch.

Procedure:

1. Weigh 400 g of cement and place it on a non-porous plate.
2. Add a trial quantity of clean water (say 26 % of the cement mass, i.e. 104 ml) and mix thoroughly by hand with the trowel, the gauging time being 3 to 5 minutes, starting the stop watch when water is added.
3. Fill the paste in the Vicat mould resting on the glass plate, remove excess, and smooth the top.
4. Place the mould under the plunger, bring the plunger to touch the paste surface and fix the scale to zero, then release the plunger quickly and let it sink under its own weight.
5. Read the depth of penetration after the plunger stops. If it is not 33 to 35 mm from the top (5 to 7 mm from the bottom), empty the mould, clean it and repeat with a fresh sample and a different water content (increase by 2 % each trial) until the required penetration is obtained.
6. Standard consistency $P=\dfrac{\text{mass of water}}{\text{mass of cement}}\times100\ \%$; usual values are 26 to 33 %.

@s 6
**Mortar** is a workable paste of a binding material (cement, lime or clay), fine aggregate (sand) and water. It hardens in place and binds bricks or stones into masonry, fills the joints between units and provides a base for plastering and pointing.

**Cement mortar.** Mix of cement and sand, usually 1:3 to 1:6 (richer for plaster and for waterproof work, e.g. 1:3 or 1:4 below damp-proof course, 1:6 for ordinary walls).

- Properties: high strength and early strength gain (the 7-day strength is about 60 % of the 28-day strength), hardens by hydration, so it sets and hardens even in damp conditions and under water, good bond, good durability and water resistance, less workable than lime mortar (plasticizers or lime added), needs curing, costly.
- Uses: load-bearing brick and stone masonry, masonry in foundations and below ground, plastering and pointing, flooring beds, water tanks, drains, reinforced brick work.

**Mud mortar.** Made of clay or soil and sand mixed with water, sometimes with straw or cow dung to resist cracking.

- Properties: very low strength, softens and erodes in water, shrinks and cracks on drying, easily mixed and reworked, cheapest, it is available locally and sets by drying only.
- Uses: the traditional mortar in rural hill houses with stone or sun-dried bricks, low-cost and temporary houses, laying of burnt bricks in dry places (plastered with mud or lime to protect), and the lower, non-structural parts. Not used for foundations in wet soil.

@s 7
**Importance of seasoning of timber.** Seasoning is the controlled removal of moisture from freshly felled (green) timber, down to about 10 to 15 % (the moisture content of air-dry timber), by air or kiln drying. It is important because it:

- reduces shrinkage, warping, twisting, splitting and checks after the timber has been put to use,
- increases strength, hardness and stiffness, and reduces the weight (cheaper transport),
- makes the timber resistant to attack by fungi (rot) and insects, which need a moisture content above 20 %,
- makes it easier to work, to join, to paint, to polish and to preserve (preservatives are absorbed better into dry wood),
- improves electrical resistance and gives a better surface finish.

**Methods of preservation of timber**

1. **Painting or coating:** oil paint, varnish, tar or coal tar, applied after seasoning, protect the surface from moisture, only on the surface.
2. **Charring:** the ends of posts to be buried are charred to a depth of about 1 cm; the carbon layer resists decay.
3. **Creosoting:** impregnation with creosote oil (from coal tar), by brush, spraying or dipping (hot and cold bath), or under pressure; very effective against fungi and termites, used for sleepers, poles and posts, but smells and cannot be painted.
4. **Pressure treatment:** full-cell (Bethell) process, in which the timber is placed in a cylinder, a vacuum is applied and the preservative is forced in under 8 to 14 kg/cm²; empty-cell processes (Lowry and Rueping) leave the cell walls coated.
5. **Salt (water-borne) treatment:** copper sulphate, zinc chloride, copper–chrome–arsenic (CCA), borax and boric acid solutions impregnated by dipping or pressure; the timber is dried afterwards. Suitable for indoor timber that is to be painted.
6. **Hot and cold open tank process, injection** of preservatives with a pump, and **sap displacement** (Boucherie process, preservative drives the sap out of a standing or freshly cut tree).
7. **Anti-termite chemical (pre-treatment of the soil)** and **ASCU** treatment with a mixture of copper, chromium and arsenic salts.

@s 8
**Importance of steel.** Steel (iron with up to about 1.5 % carbon) is the most important engineering metal because it has high strength in both tension and compression, is ductile and tough, can be rolled into sections of any shape (I-beams, angles, channels, bars, plates), can be welded, bolted and riveted, has a high strength-to-weight ratio (long spans, tall buildings), is produced in a factory with uniform quality, allows fast erection and is fully recyclable. It is used for reinforcement of concrete, structural frames, bridges, roofing, pipes, tanks and machinery. Its drawbacks are corrosion and loss of strength at high temperature (it must be painted and fire proofed).

**Types of steel according to composition**

| Type | Carbon / alloying | Properties | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low carbon (mild) steel | 0.05 to 0.25 % C | Soft, ductile, tough, easily welded and machined, moderate strength | Reinforcement bars, structural sections, plates, wires, nails, sheets |
| Medium carbon steel | 0.25 to 0.60 % C | Stronger and harder, less ductile, can be heat-treated | Rails, axles, shafts, gears, bolts, forgings |
| High carbon steel | 0.60 to 1.5 % C | Very hard and strong but brittle, difficult to weld | Cutting tools, springs, wires, dies, drills |
| Alloy steels | Added Ni, Cr, Mn, Mo, V, etc. | Special properties: strength, toughness, hardness, corrosion or heat resistance | Stainless steel (Cr > 10.5 %), high-tensile steel (HYSD bars), structural alloy steels |
| (Related: cast iron / wrought iron) | > 2 % C / < 0.15 % C | Cast iron brittle, strong in compression; wrought iron tough | Pipes, columns bases; ornamental work |

**Uses of copper:** electrical wires, cables and bus bars (very high electrical conductivity), water supply pipes and fittings (plumbing), roofing sheets, flashings and gutters, heat exchangers and boilers (good thermal conductivity), alloys such as brass (Cu + Zn) and bronze (Cu + Sn), bearings, lightning conductors, decorative work and coins; its resistance to corrosion makes it durable.

@s 9
**Ingredients of paint.** Paint is a mixture of a pigment (and base) in a liquid vehicle, which dries to form a hard, protective, decorative film.

| Ingredient | Examples | Role |
|---|---|---|
| Base | White lead, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, iron oxide, red lead, aluminium powder | Main solid constituent; gives body, opacity (covering power), the film, and protects against weather |
| Vehicle (medium) | Linseed oil, tung oil, soya oil, alkyd and acrylic resins | Carries the pigment, spreads the paint, and on oxidation or polymerisation binds the pigment into a tough film |
| Pigment (colouring matter) | Chrome yellow, ultramarine blue, carbon black, iron oxide red, lamp black | Gives the required colour and some opacity and protection against UV |
| Drier | Litharge, red lead, manganese and cobalt salts (up to 8 %) | Speeds up the drying (oxidation) of the oil film |
| Thinner (solvent) | Turpentine, mineral spirit, white spirit, water (emulsion paints) | Reduces the viscosity so the paint is easy to apply, evaporates after application |
| Extender (inert filler) | Barytes, gypsum, silica, china clay, talc | Cheapens the paint, increases the bulk and film hardness, helps suspension |
| Others | Plasticisers, anti-skinning agents, fungicides | Elasticity of the film, prevents skin formation in the can, resists mould |

@s 10
**Asphalt and bitumen**

| Bitumen | Asphalt |
|---|---|
| Black or dark brown, viscous hydrocarbon (almost wholly soluble in carbon disulphide) | Mixture of bitumen with mineral matter (limestone, sand, filler) |
| Obtained by distillation of crude petroleum (the residue), or as natural bitumen | Occurs naturally as rock or lake asphalt (Trinidad) or is made by mixing bitumen with aggregates (asphalt concrete) |
| Pure binder, 99 % bitumen | Natural rock asphalt has about 10 to 15 % bitumen |
| Used as a binder for roads, waterproofing, damp proofing, roofing felts, paints and coatings | Used for pavements, flooring, roof covering, tanking and waterproofing of bridge decks |
| Needs heating (or cutting back and emulsifying) before use | Laid hot (mastic asphalt) |

(In American usage "asphalt" means the bitumen itself.)

**Aerated autoclaved concrete (AAC) blocks.** AAC is a lightweight, precast, cellular building block. Raw materials: fine sand or fly ash, cement, lime, gypsum, water and a small amount (about 0.05 to 0.1 %) of aluminium powder. The aluminium reacts with the lime and water to release hydrogen gas, which makes millions of tiny air cells, so the mix expands to about twice its volume; after cutting into blocks the green mass is cured in an autoclave at about 180 to 190 °C and 12 bar steam for 8 to 12 hours, and forms calcium silicate hydrate (tobermorite) giving the strength.

- Properties: dry density 400 to 800 kg/m³ (one-fourth to one-third of the weight of clay brick), compressive strength 3 to 5 MPa, very good thermal insulation and sound absorption, fire resistant, termite-resistant, accurate dimensions (thin-bed mortar, less plaster), easily cut and sawn, and lowers the dead load and seismic force.
- Drawbacks: low strength for load bearing, absorbs water, needs plaster and special fixings.
- Uses: partition and infill walls of RC-framed buildings, external walls of high-rise buildings, and lintels and floor panels when reinforced.
