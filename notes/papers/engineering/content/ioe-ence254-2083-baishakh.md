@s 1
**Palatable water** is water that is *pleasant to drink*: it is clear, colourless, free from objectionable taste and odour and at a suitable temperature, but it is not necessarily safe. Example: clear, cool water from a well that tastes good but contains pathogenic bacteria (or a colourless water with dissolved arsenic) is palatable but not potable.

**Potable water** is water that is *safe to drink*, free from pathogens, toxic chemicals and excessive minerals, and meeting the drinking-water standard (the Nepal Drinking Water Quality Standard 2062, WHO guidelines). Example: tap water after filtration and chlorination with residual chlorine 0.1 to 0.5 mg/L and zero E. coli per 100 mL. Potable water should also be palatable, but palatability alone does not make a water potable.

@s 2
The **yield** is the quantity of water that a source can deliver safely in the driest period (the dependable yield, e.g. the minimum spring discharge measured in the dry season, the safe yield of a well, or the 7-day 10-year low flow of a stream). Its importance:

- the **source is selected and the scheme is sized** on the yield: the design demand (with the future growth) must be less than the dependable yield; if the yield is **overestimated**, the supply fails in the dry season, the cost is wasted and conflicts arise;
- the yield decides whether **storage, a second source or a smaller coverage** is required, and the **sustainable abstraction** (with the downstream users and the environmental flow, normally $\le$ 50 % of the minimum flow for a spring or a stream);
- it is needed for the water-right permission and for the economic analysis; it should be measured in the **driest month** (March to May) by the bucket, weir or velocity-area method.

@s 3
The **peak factor** is the ratio of the peak (maximum) demand to the average demand: maximum day factor ($1.2$ to $1.5$, or $1.5$ to $2$ in Nepal's rural schemes) and the peak hour factor ($2$ to $3$, higher for small villages, e.g. $3$ for $<$ 2000 persons). Significance:

- the **distribution pipes, the pumps and the service reservoir** are designed for the peak hourly flow, the intake, the treatment plant and the transmission main for the maximum daily demand; so the factor sets the sizes (and the cost) of each component;
- it ensures that the **required residual pressure** is maintained at peak time and in the periods of the highest use (the morning and evening);
- a factor that is too low gives low pressure and a poor supply at peak hours; one that is too high gives an uneconomical design.

@s 4
**Emerging pollutants (contaminants)** are new or newly recognised substances found in water that are not yet covered by the standards: pharmaceuticals and the personal-care products, hormones and endocrine disruptors, pesticides, PFAS ("forever chemicals"), micro- and nano-plastics, antibiotic-resistant bacteria and genes, nanomaterials and industrial additives.

**Environmental significance.** They occur in very small concentrations (ng/L to $\mu$g/L) but they are **persistent and bio-accumulative**, and the conventional treatment (coagulation, sand filtration, chlorination) does not remove them. They disrupt the endocrine and reproductive system of fish and other aquatic life, cause the spread of **antimicrobial resistance**, harm the ecosystem balance and enter the food chain; chronic effects on human health (cancer, hormone effects) are possible. They need monitoring, source control (the waste water treatment, the take-back of drugs) and advanced treatment (activated carbon, ozone/AOP, membranes), and they are a challenge to the standards and the regulation.

@s 5
**Membrane filtration.** A **pressure- or vacuum-driven process in which water is forced through a semi-permeable membrane** that retains the particles, the microorganisms or the dissolved substances according to the pore size: microfiltration (0.1 to 10 $\mu$m: bacteria and turbidity), ultrafiltration (0.01 to 0.1 $\mu$m: viruses and colloids), nanofiltration (about 1 nm: hardness, organics) and reverse osmosis (below 1 nm: dissolved salts, with the pressure higher than the osmotic pressure). The permeate is the treated water, the retentate the concentrate.

**Electrodialysis (ED).** A **desalination process driven by an electric potential** in which ions are moved through alternately arranged **cation-exchange and anion-exchange membranes** between two electrodes. The cations pass through the cation membranes towards the cathode and the anions through the anion membranes towards the anode, so alternate compartments become *dilute* (product water) and *concentrated* (brine). It removes the ionic species only (the salts, nitrate, fluoride), not the neutral particles and organisms, and is used for brackish water; the polarity reversal (EDR) reduces the fouling.

@s 6
A **District Metered Area (DMA)** is a hydraulically isolated zone of the network (about 500 to 3000 connections) with the **metered inlet(s)** (and the pressure monitoring), the boundary valves closed, and the smart sensors and the SCADA. Its primary functions:

1. **Water audit and non-revenue water (NRW) control:** the inflow minus the consumption (the billed meters) gives the losses of each zone;
2. **Leakage detection and location:** the minimum night flow (2 to 4 a.m.) shows the leakage level and the burst; the response is directed to the zone with the highest loss;
3. **Pressure management:** the PRV at the inlet of each DMA reduces the excess pressure and the leaks and bursts;
4. **Demand monitoring and control of the quality** (the chlorine residual, the contamination by isolating the zone), the intermittent-supply scheduling and the planning;
5. **Operation:** a fast isolation of the zone for repair and the reduced customer interruption, the real-time data and the targeting of the rehabilitation.

@s 7
**Spigot (spigot-and-socket) joint:** the plain end (spigot) of one pipe is pushed into the socket (bell) of the next, with a rubber ring (push-fit) or lead and yarn or solvent cement (for PVC, DI, CI and HDPE). **Practicability:** quick and simple, needs no special tool and no power, allows a small angular deflection and expansion, and is good for the **hilly rural** projects with the difficult access, for the PVC/DI pipe, for the **gravity** and the low-pressure lines. But it is **not restrained** (needs the thrust blocks), the ring may fail with a poor jointing, and it is not suitable for the very high pressure or the fast-moving ground (landslide) without anchoring.

**Electrofusion joint:** a coupler or saddle with a built-in heating wire is placed over the HDPE (or PE) pipe ends and an electric current from a control box fuses the pipe and the fitting into one **homogeneous, leak-free and fully end-load-bearing joint**. **Practicability:** very reliable, resistant to the pull-out and to the leakage, suitable for the high pressure and for the unstable terrain and the trenchless work; but it needs a **power supply (a generator), the trained operator, the cleaning and scraping, the clamps and the dry trench**, has a higher cost per joint and is slower in the remote hills. So spigot is the choice for low-pressure routine work and electrofusion for the HDPE mains where the joint security matters (the crossings, the high-pressure and the landslide sections).

@s 8
**Threats of climate change to spring water.**

1. The **changed rainfall**: a shorter and more intense monsoon and the longer dry spell, so the **recharge** reduces and the spring discharge falls (many springs dry up or become seasonal);
2. **Retreat of snow and glacier**, change of the snowmelt, the higher temperature and the **evaporation**, the loss of the soil moisture;
3. **Landslides, floods and earthquakes** that damage the spring boxes and change the flow paths and the recharge zones;
4. **Land-use change** (deforestation, road building, the overgrazing, the urbanisation) that reduces the infiltration and the recharge;
5. Increased turbidity, the contamination and the microbiological risk after the heavy rain, and the rising **demand** and the conflict.

**Conservation strategies.** Protect and manage the **recharge areas**: the afforestation with the broad-leaved native species, the grazing control; build the **recharge ponds, the trenches (the contour trenches), the check dams and the terraces**, and the infiltration pits; rainwater harvesting; fence and protect the spring box and the catchment against the contamination (the sanitary protection); the **spring-shed management** with the community and the user committee; monitor the discharge and the quality (the spring inventory); reduce the leakage and the wastage, use the **storage tanks** to store the night flow; adopt the **multiple use**, the source diversification and the demand management; the climate-resilient design (the intake on the stable ground, the safety factor in the yield).

@s 10
**Standard plate count (pour or spread plate) for E. coli.** The method counts the **viable (colony-forming) bacteria** in a water sample by growing them on an agar medium; for E. coli the *selective and differential* medium is used (e.g. MacConkey, Eosin-Methylene Blue, or a chromogenic agar such as Tryptone Bile X-glucuronide, TBX, or the membrane filter with m-FC/m-TEC at 44.5 °C) so that E. coli appears as characteristic coloured colonies (e.g. a green metallic sheen on EMB, blue-green on chromogenic agar).

*Procedure.*

1. Collect the sample in a sterile bottle (with sodium thiosulphate to remove chlorine), keep cool, and analyse within 6 hours.
2. **Serial dilution:** 1 mL of the sample in 9 mL of sterile buffered dilution water, giving $10^{-1}$, $10^{-2}$, $10^{-3}$ ... (aseptic technique).
3. **Plating:** 1 mL of each dilution is placed in a sterile Petri dish and the molten agar (at 44 to 46 °C) is poured and mixed (**pour plate**), or 0.1 mL is spread on the solidified agar (**spread plate**); the duplicate plates with a blank control. (For clean water, 100 mL is passed through the 0.45 $\mu$m **membrane filter**, which is placed on the agar.)
4. **Incubation:** inverted at 35 ± 0.5 °C for 24 ± 2 h (44.5 °C for the thermotolerant coliform and E. coli).
5. **Counting:** plates with **30 to 300 colonies** are counted (20 to 80 for the membrane filter), the typical E. coli colonies are confirmed (indole positive, the lactose fermentation, or the glucuronidase test).

$$\text{CFU/mL}=\frac{\text{number of colonies}}{\text{volume plated (mL)}\times\text{dilution factor}}$$

*Example:* 150 colonies on the $10^{-2}$ plate of 1 mL: $150/(1\times10^{-2})=1.5\times10^4$ CFU/mL. The standard for drinking water is **0 E. coli per 100 mL**. The method is cheap, standard and quantitative, but takes 24 to 48 h and measures only the culturable cells.

@s 11
**Spring intake (spring box).** A spring intake collects the water from a spring (a seepage or a fracture spring) and protects it from contamination. The main parts are the **collection chamber (the spring box)** built over the eye of the spring, the **gravel or stone filter (the dry masonry) and the impervious cover**, the **perforated (the open-jointed) wall** facing the spring, the **outlet pipe** (with the screen) to the transmission line, the **overflow pipe**, the **washout (drain) pipe**, the **inspection manhole with a lockable cover**, the **diversion ditch** above the box to remove the surface runoff, and the **clay seal and the fence**.

*Working.* The groundwater flows out of the spring face through the gravel packing into the collection chamber (the sedimentation chamber). The water level in the chamber is held at the outlet pipe level; the spring flow in excess of the demand leaves through the **overflow** so that the back-pressure does not drive the water to other exits (the flow around the box is avoided), and the sand and the silt settle in the chamber and are removed through the **washout**. The outlet pipe, with a strainer and a valve, takes the water by gravity to the tank. The impervious clay cover and the diversion drain stop the polluted surface water from entering.

@s 12
**Plain sedimentation (without coagulant).** Removes **discrete, settleable particles** (sand, silt, and the particles larger than about 0.02 to 0.05 mm, SG 2.65) by gravity under quiescent conditions in a tank with the detention time of 3 to 4 h (or the overflow rate $\le$ 12 to 20 m³/m²/day for the plain tank in Nepal). The settling follows Stokes' law, $v_s=\dfrac{g(S_s-1)d^2}{18\nu}$, and the **efficiency is low for the fine silt and the colloids** (the colloids of 1 to 10 $\mu$m would take days). Typically 60 to 70 % turbidity removal for a clay-free water; the bacteria removal about 25 to 75 %. Needs no chemicals, simple and cheap, but the larger tank and the poor removal of the fine turbidity.

**Sedimentation with coagulation (flocculation).** A coagulant (alum, ferric chloride, PAC) is mixed rapidly to **destabilise the colloids**, and slow mixing forms **flocs** (much larger and heavier than the original particles, with $d\approx$ 0.5 to 3 mm and a settling velocity of the order of 0.2 to 1 mm/s). The flocs settle in the clarifier at the overflow rate 30 to 40 m³/m²/day with the detention time of 2 to 4 h, and **sweep up the fine particles by enmeshment**, so 85 to 95 % of the turbidity, 50 to 80 % of the colour and the bacteria and the organic matter are removed; the tank is smaller; but it needs the chemical dose (jar test), the mixing and the flocculation units, the sludge handling and a skilled operator, and a pH/alkalinity adjustment.

| | Plain sedimentation | With coagulation |
|---|---|---|
| Particles removed | discrete, coarse | colloids and fine silt (flocculent) |
| Overflow rate | 12 to 20 m³/m²/d | 30 to 40 m³/m²/d |
| Detention | 3 to 4 h | 2 to 4 h |
| Turbidity removal | 60 to 70 % | above 85 % |
| Needs | the tank only | the chemicals, mixing, flocculator, sludge handling |

Plain sedimentation is used as the **pre-treatment** for turbid river water (the sand-laden flood flows), and the coagulation tank for the final clarification before filtration.

@s 13
**Chick's law (first order):** $\dfrac{dN}{dt}=-kN\Rightarrow\ln\dfrac{N}{N_0}=-kt$.

$$k=\frac1t\ln\frac{N_0}{N}=\frac1{5}\ln\frac{1.0\times10^6}{1.0\times10^3}=\frac{\ln1000}{5}=\frac{6.908}{5}=\mathbf{1.38\ min^{-1}}\ (=82.9\ \text{h}^{-1},\ 0.0230\ \text{s}^{-1})$$

This is a 3-log (99.9 %) inactivation in 5 min. The time for a 4-log (99.99 %) kill at the same chlorine concentration would be $\ln10^4/1.38=6.7$ min.

**Environmental/public-health significance.** $k$ is a measure of the **speed of disinfection** at a given chlorine dose, temperature, pH and organism; it is used to find the **contact time and the dose ($Ct$ value)** needed for the required kill, to design the chlorine contact tank, so that the pathogens (and the resistant organisms) are killed without an excess chlorine that would form the **disinfection by-products** (trihalomethanes, haloacetic acids) and the harm to the aquatic life and the taste. A low $k$ (cold water, the high pH, the turbid or the organic-rich water, the resistant cysts) calls for a larger dose or contact time.

@s 14
**Fittings** are the accessories that are fixed to the pipeline to join, to change the direction or the size, to branch, to control or to terminate the flow, and are not the pipes themselves. Examples: *joining and adapting:* couplings, unions, flanges, reducers, bends, elbows (45°, 90°), tees, crosses, caps and plugs; *control:* valves (gate, globe, butterfly, non-return, air valve, pressure reducing and sluice valves), hydrants, water meters, stop cocks, ferrules and the saddles; *safety:* the air release valve and the pressure relief valve.

**Need for dismantling joints.** A **dismantling (the flanged adaptor) joint** is a flanged joint with the telescopic piece, and it is placed next to valves, pumps, meters and the fittings of the pipe network. It gives the **easy installation and removal** of the valve or the meter for the repair without cutting the pipe, it allows **a small adjustment of the length** (the construction tolerance, the thermal expansion and the settlement), keeps the pipe free of the stress and is used at the chamber walls, at the pump outlets and at the bridge crossings.

**Need for flushing arrangements.** **Washout (blow-off) valves** are fixed at the low points of the pipeline and the **end of the dead ends** of the distribution. They allow the **removal of sediment, silt, slime and the stagnant water** that collects, the **emptying of the pipe for repairs and the maintenance** and the **disinfection after the construction** or the repair, the release of the pipe water after a contamination and the scouring of the biofilm; the water quality (the residual chlorine and the taste) at the end of the network is maintained and the effective pipe diameter and the carrying capacity are kept.

@s 15
**Data.** Tank $11\times5\times3$ m; surface area $A=11\times5=55\ \text{m}^2$, depth 3 m, volume 165 m³. 20 °C: $\nu=1.003\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$, $S_s=2.65$.

**Settling velocity of the 0.025 mm particle (Stokes).**

$$v_s=\frac{g(S_s-1)d^2}{18\nu}=\frac{9.81(1.65)(0.025\times10^{-3})^2}{18(1.003\times10^{-6})}=5.60\times10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}\ (=48.4\ \text{m/day})$$

Check: $Re=v_sd/\nu=0.014<1$, so Stokes' law applies.

**Overflow rate.** In an ideal rectangular settling tank a particle of settling velocity $v_s$ is removed in the fraction $x=v_s/v_0$ when $v_s<v_0$, where $v_0=Q/A$ is the overflow rate. For 93 % removal of the 0.025 mm particles:

$$v_0=\frac{v_s}{0.93}=\frac{5.60\times10^{-4}}{0.93}=6.03\times10^{-4}\ \text{m/s}=\mathbf{52.1\ m^3/m^2\cdot day}\ (=0.60\ \text{mm/s})$$

$$Q=v_0A=6.03\times10^{-4}\times55=0.0331\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}=\mathbf{2860\ m^3/day}\ (=119\ \text{m}^3/\text{h})$$

(The detention time is $V/Q=165/119.3=1.38$ h $=83$ min.)

**Removal of 99 % of the 0.05 mm particles.**

$$v_s=\frac{9.81(1.65)(0.05\times10^{-3})^2}{18(1.003\times10^{-6})}=2.24\times10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}\ (=193.7\ \text{m/day}),\quad Re=0.11<1$$

Since $v_s=2.24\times10^{-3}>v_0=6.03\times10^{-4}$ m/s (the ratio is 3.72), **every** 0.05 mm particle (100 %) settles in the tank at this overflow rate, which is more than the 99 % needed. **The tank dimension is enough.** The 93 % removal of the finer 0.025 mm particle is therefore the controlling condition for the size of the tank.

**Method.** The residual head at every tap must be at least 5 m. The head loss is found by Hazen-Williams:

$$h_f=\frac{10.67\,L\,Q^{1.852}}{C^{1.852}d^{4.87}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad d=\Big[\frac{10.67LQ^{1.852}}{C^{1.852}h_f}\Big]^{1/4.87}$$

with $Q$ in m³/s, $L$ and $d$ in m, $C=150$. The source at A is at RL 1000 m (the HGL at A is 1000 m, i.e. a full spring or tank outlet).

**Flows.** $Q_{AD}=0.2$ L/s, $Q_{BC}=0.1$ L/s, $Q_{AB}=0.2$ L/s (0.1 L/s for Tap 1 and 0.1 L/s passed on to C).

**Line AD (to Tap 3, RL 980).** Available head $=1000-980-5=15$ m over 200 m:

$$d_{req}=\Big[\frac{10.67(200)(2\times10^{-4})^{1.852}}{150^{1.852}(15)}\Big]^{1/4.87}=16.1\ \text{mm}$$

A commercial pipe of 20 mm (the next size, internal about 20 mm): $h_f=5.28$ m (< 15 m). The residual head at D $=1000-5.28-980=14.7$ m $>5$ m. **AD: 20 mm.**

**Line AB (to Tap 1, RL 960).** Available $1000-960-5=35$ m over 150 m:

$$d_{req}=12.8\ \text{mm}$$

Adopt **16 mm** (the commercial size above 12.8 mm): $h_f=\dfrac{10.67(150)(2\times10^{-4})^{1.852}}{150^{1.852}(0.016)^{4.87}}=11.74$ m. HGL at B $=1000-11.74=988.26$ m; the residual head at B $=988.26-960=28.3$ m $>5$ m.

**Line BC (to Tap 2, RL 940).** The available head $=988.26-940-5=43.3$ m over 200 m with $Q=0.1$ L/s:

$$d_{req}=9.9\ \text{mm}$$

A 16 mm pipe (the smallest commonly used in the rural schemes, the minimum practical size; 12.5 mm would also pass the check) gives $h_f=4.34$ m. HGL at C $=988.26-4.34=983.92$ m, residual head at C $=983.92-940=43.9$ m $>5$ m.
