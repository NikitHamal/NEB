**(a) Technical communication and how it differs from general language.** Technical communication is the **delivery of technical, scientific or specialised information clearly, accurately and efficiently to a defined audience** so that they can use it to perform a task, take a decision or understand a product or process (reports, manuals, proposals, specifications, e-mails, presentations).

| Basis | Technical communication | General use of language |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | to inform, instruct, record or persuade for **action**; usually has a practical goal | everyday conversation, entertainment, expression of feelings, social contact |
| Audience | specific and known (client, manager, technician) | general, any listener or reader |
| Content | facts, data, procedures, specifications | opinions, feelings, stories, ideas of every kind |
| Style and tone | objective, impersonal, precise, concise, unambiguous | subjective, personal, may be emotional, humorous and figurative |
| Vocabulary | technical terms, standard units, abbreviations defined for the reader | everyday words, idioms, slang, metaphors |
| Format | standard, structured (headings, numbering, abstract, figures, tables, references) | free, informal, no fixed format |
| Visuals | essential: drawings, charts, tables, graphs | seldom used |
| Accuracy and accountability | very high: errors may cause accidents, cost and legal problems | errors are usually harmless |
| Examples | feasibility report, user manual, lab report, tender document | a chat with a friend, a poem, a story, a speech at a party |

**(b) Common forms of plagiarism.** Plagiarism is the use of another person's words, ideas, data, images or work **without acknowledgement**, passing them as one's own.

1. **Direct (verbatim) plagiarism:** copying text word for word without quotation marks and citation.
2. **Paraphrasing plagiarism:** changing a few words or the sentence structure but using the original idea without credit.
3. **Mosaic (patchwork) plagiarism:** joining phrases and sentences from several sources with small changes.
4. **Self-plagiarism:** re-using one's own earlier published or submitted work without saying so.
5. **Accidental plagiarism:** careless citation, missing quotation marks, or a wrong source.
6. **Source-based plagiarism:** false or invented references; citing a secondary source as if it had been read in the original.
7. **Other forms:** buying or copying a whole paper, copying a classmate's work, using images, data, code, music or designs without permission, and **collusion**.

*Why it is a key concern in professional communication.*

- It is **dishonest and unethical**: it breaks the first principle of professional ethics, honesty and integrity.
- It violates **copyright and intellectual property law** and can lead to a fine, a court case, a withdrawn paper or a cancelled contract.
- It destroys the **credibility and reputation** of the writer and the organisation, and can end a career (dismissal, loss of licence or degree).
- It makes it impossible to trace the facts to their source, which is essential for **verification** in engineering and science, where decisions affect safety.
- It discourages originality and innovation and is unfair to the real authors, who lose credit and income.
- Prevention: cite every source (APA / IEEE), use quotation marks for exact words, paraphrase properly and still cite, keep notes of sources, and check the work with plagiarism-detection software.

**(b) Basic guidelines for precise writing.** Precise writing is exact, specific and free from vagueness: the reader gets one clear meaning.

1. **Use specific and concrete words** rather than general ones: *"a 12 m reinforced concrete bridge"* not *"a structure"*; *"vehicle"* is replaced by *"a 10-tonne truck"* where it matters.
2. **Give exact figures, units and dates:** *"the temperature rose by 15 °C in 10 minutes"*, not *"it became very hot soon"*; avoid *"some, many, a few, soon, large"* without a number.
3. **Choose the right word (denotation):** distinguish words that look alike (*affect/effect, principle/principal, accuracy/precision*); use standard technical terms and **define** new terms and abbreviations at first use.
4. **Avoid ambiguity:** make pronoun references clear (*"The engineer told the manager that **he** was late"* is unclear), place modifiers next to the words they modify, avoid words with several meanings.
5. **Prefer the active voice and strong verbs:** *"The pump delivers 20 L/s"* is more exact than *"20 L/s is the delivery that is made by the pump"*.
6. **Be concise:** delete padding and repetition; every word should carry information.
7. **Be accurate and consistent:** check facts, calculations and spellings; use the same term for the same thing throughout.
8. **Use visuals and examples** (tables, figures) to show exact data and make the meaning concrete.
9. **Revise and proof-read:** read as the reader would and ask whether any statement could be taken in two ways.

**(c) Corrected sentences.**

| No. | Answer | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| i | Neither of the girls **is** wearing a ribbon. | *neither (of)* is singular |
| ii | In our institution, the number of students **is** increasing every year. | *the number of* takes a singular verb (*a number of* takes a plural one) |
| iii | He and I are selling **our** old books. | possessive pronoun for *he and I* |
| iv | The word "friends" refers back to **dogs**. | *They* stands for *dogs* and *friends* is a predicate noun describing *they* (the *humans* are only the object of *of*) |
| v | She advised me to take medicine regularly, **but** I may not recover. (or: *I may not recover, yet she advised me to take medicine regularly.*) | the concessive clause becomes a co-ordinate clause joined by *but / yet* |
| vi | **Although** he has lost weight, he is **still not** fit for recruitment. | *but* becomes the subordinator *although*; the adverb is placed in the correct order (*still not*) |
| vii | **As** he was tired, he went to bed early. (or *Since / Because he was tired ...*) | the first clause becomes a subordinate clause of reason |
| viii | **Having walked a lot**, he felt very tired. (or *After walking a lot, he felt very tired.*) | a participle phrase replaces one clause: one finite verb remains |
| ix | **started** | the finite verb of the main clause; *got* is the finite verb of the subordinate clause, while *writing* is a gerund and *married* a participle |
| x | **finished** (in the participle phrase *having finished*) and also *to play* | non-finite forms do not show tense or agree with a subject: *having finished* (perfect participle) and *to play* (infinitive); *went* is the finite verb |

**(c) Research article: "The ecological cost of using digital platforms" (abstract, findings, discussion).**

> **ABSTRACT**<br>
> Digital platforms such as video streaming, social media, cloud storage and online shopping appear to be "clean" because they are used on a screen, but they rely on data centres, networks and billions of devices that use electricity and raw materials. This article reviews published estimates of the energy, carbon, water and electronic-waste footprint of digital platforms and discusses ways to reduce it. The findings indicate that information and communication technology (ICT) uses about 4% of global electricity and produces about 2-4% of greenhouse gas emissions, similar to aviation, with the share growing every year. Streaming video accounts for the largest part of internet traffic, and the manufacturing of devices accounts for the larger part of a device's life-cycle emissions. The article concludes that digital convenience has a real ecological cost and recommends efficient design, renewable energy, longer device life and responsible use.

> **FINDINGS**<br>
> 1. *Energy:* data centres and transmission networks use roughly 1-1.5% each of global electricity, and the demand is rising with video, cloud computing and artificial intelligence.<br>
> 2. *Carbon:* one hour of video streaming emits tens of grams of CO2 equivalent (the exact value depends on the electricity mix, the device and the resolution); high definition and auto-play increase the traffic.<br>
> 3. *Devices:* about 70-80% of the carbon footprint of a smartphone or laptop comes from manufacturing; mining of metals (cobalt, lithium, copper) causes pollution and habitat loss.<br>
> 4. *Water:* data centres use large volumes of water for cooling.<br>
> 5. *E-waste:* more than 50 million tonnes are produced each year and only about 20% is recycled.<br>
> 6. *Blockchain and AI:* training a large AI model or mining cryptocurrency consumes electricity equal to that of many households.

> **DISCUSSION**<br>
> The findings show that the ecological cost of digital life is large but hidden: users do not see the power plant, the mine or the waste. The cost depends on where the electricity comes from; data centres powered by hydro, solar or wind (such as in Nepal, where hydropower is abundant) have a far lower footprint than those powered by coal. The measures that can reduce the cost are energy-efficient hardware and software, better cooling, renewable energy, lower default video quality, turning off auto-play, deleting unused data, repair, reuse and recycling of devices, and keeping the devices for longer. Digital platforms can also reduce the footprint of other activities (online meetings instead of travel, paperless offices), so the net effect depends on how they are used. The limitation of the study is that the estimates vary between sources and methods. Further research is needed on local data, particularly for developing countries.

**(a) Strategies for handling questions during and after a presentation.**

*During the presentation.*

1. **Decide beforehand** and tell the audience whether questions are welcome at any time (interactive) or at the end.
2. **Listen carefully and completely** to the question, without interrupting, and note it if you cannot answer at once.
3. **Pause, then repeat or rephrase** the question so that everybody has heard it and to check the understanding.
4. **Answer briefly and directly,** then return to the presentation; if the answer will be given on a later slide, say so ("I will come to this in a moment").
5. **Stay polite and calm** even for a rude or irrelevant question; thank the questioner.

*After the presentation (question-and-answer session).*

1. **Anticipate questions** and prepare answers; have back-up slides, data and references ready.
2. **Invite questions positively** ("I would be happy to take your questions") and allow a few seconds of silence.
3. **Answer honestly;** if you do not know, say so, promise to find out and follow up; never bluff.
4. **Address the whole audience,** not only the questioner; keep eye contact with all.
5. **Handle difficult questions tactfully:** break a long question into parts, politely reject an off-topic one ("that is outside the scope today, let's discuss it afterwards"), and do not argue.
6. **Control time and dominating persons;** give turns to different members and keep to the time limit.
7. **Use bridging** to return to the key message, and end with a **short summary** and thanks.
8. **Take feedback:** record questions that show the unclear parts and improve the next presentation.

**(b) Active listening.** Active listening is the **conscious effort to hear, understand, evaluate and respond to a speaker's whole message** (words, tone and feelings) by giving full attention, showing interest through body language, asking questions, paraphrasing and giving feedback. It differs from simply "hearing" and from passive listening.

*Psychological barriers* (inside the listener's mind):

- **Prejudice and stereotypes** about the speaker or the topic, which lead to rejecting the message before hearing it.
- **Emotions** (anger, fear, anxiety, excitement) that distort the meaning or block attention.
- **Pre-judging, defensive attitude and the habit of preparing a reply** (or interrupting) instead of listening.
- **Lack of interest or motivation,** boredom, daydreaming (thinking speed exceeds speaking speed).
- **Ego and the "know-it-all" attitude,** selective listening (hearing only what we want), and information overload and stress.
- **Remedy:** keep an open mind, control emotions, give attention to the speaker, listen to understand, not to reply.

*Cultural barriers* (differences of background):

- **Language, accent, dialect and idioms** that are not understood.
- **Different values, beliefs and customs**: what is polite in one culture (silence, no eye contact to elders, indirect "no") may be rude or confusing in another.
- **Differences in non-verbal signs:** gestures, eye contact, touch and personal space have different meanings.
- **Different attitudes to time, hierarchy and gender** that affect who speaks and how openly.
- **Stereotypes and ethnocentrism**, assuming that one's own culture is the standard.
- **Remedy:** learn about the other culture, ask politely for clarification, paraphrase, avoid slang and be patient and respectful.

**(b) How visual aids enhance understanding.**

1. **Simplify complex or numerical information:** a graph or table shows trends and comparisons at a glance that would take many paragraphs.
2. **Use two channels at once (seeing and hearing),** so that the message is received, understood and **remembered** better (people recall far more of what they see and hear than of what they only hear).
3. **Explain what words cannot:** a drawing, photograph, flow chart or model shows a shape, a process or a circuit exactly.
4. **Clarify structure and relationships** (organisation charts, flow charts, Gantt charts, block diagrams).
5. **Emphasise key points** by colour, size and position, and **hold the attention** of the audience; this reduces boredom and misunderstanding.
6. **Overcome language and cultural barriers,** since pictures and symbols are understood across languages.
7. **Save time and support decisions:** managers can read a dashboard or chart quickly.
8. **Make the speaker or the document look organised and professional** and increase credibility and persuasion.

Good use requires a suitable and simple visual, readable size, a title, labels and units, a mention in the text and avoidance of too much information or distortion.
