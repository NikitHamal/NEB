@s 1
**Artificial recharge of groundwater in the Kathmandu valley.** The shallow and deep aquifers of the valley (lacustrine clay with sand and gravel lenses) are over-pumped; the water table in the deep aquifer has fallen by several metres per year. The main techniques are:

1. *Recharge pits, trenches and shafts:* excavated pits filled with gravel and sand, placed in open fields, along roads and in school and temple grounds, to allow rain run-off to infiltrate.
2. *Roof-top rainwater harvesting* with storage tanks or direct recharge wells for houses, offices and hospitals.
3. *Recharge ponds* (traditional *pokhari*, restored ponds and tanks) and spreading basins in the highly permeable areas near the valley margins (Bhabar-like fans of Gokarna, Sundarijal and Dhobikhola areas).
4. *Injection (recharge) wells* for treated or storm water into the deeper sand and gravel aquifer.
5. *Check dams, bunds and river-bed recharge* across the Bagmati, Bishnumati, Manohara and Dhobikhola for riverbank infiltration.
6. *Stone-lined drains and permeable pavements* in urban areas, and conservation of green space.
7. *Reuse of treated wastewater.*

@s 2
**Mechanical properties of rocks.**

| Property | Meaning and typical values |
|---|---|
| Uniaxial compressive strength $\sigma_c$ | Maximum axial stress on an unconfined cylinder; granite 100 to 250 MPa, sandstone 20 to 170 MPa, shale 5 to 100 MPa, phyllite 20 to 100 MPa |
| Tensile strength $\sigma_t$ | Brazilian test, about $1/10$ of $\sigma_c$ |
| Shear strength | $\tau = c + \sigma\tan\phi$, from direct shear or triaxial tests; discontinuity friction angle 20° to 40° |
| Young's modulus $E$ and Poisson's ratio $\nu$ | Stiffness, $E = \sigma/\varepsilon$; $\nu$ 0.1 to 0.35 |
| Bulk, shear modulus | $K = E/3(1 - 2\nu)$, $G = E/2(1 + \nu)$ |
| Hardness, abrasivity, toughness | Schmidt, Shore, Cerchar, Los Angeles; for drilling and aggregate |
| Density, porosity, water absorption | Unit weight 22 to 30 kN/m³; porosity 0.1 to 30 percent |
| Durability (slake-durability), permeability | Resistance to weathering; seepage |
| Creep and time-dependent behaviour | Deformation under sustained load (salt, shale) |

Rock failure may be brittle (splitting) or ductile at high confining pressure; the strength increases with confinement according to Mohr-Coulomb or Hoek-Brown criteria. These properties control the bearing capacity of foundations, stability of slopes and tunnels, and the choice of excavation and support.

@s 3
**Rock mass.** A rock mass is the in-situ rock together with all its discontinuities (joints, bedding planes, faults, foliation, shear zones), i.e. intact rock blocks separated by discontinuities. Its strength, deformability and permeability are controlled by the discontinuities and are far lower than for intact rock samples, which is why classification (RMR, Q, GSI) is used in design.

**Rock Mass Rating (RMR, Bieniawski 1989).** The rock mass is divided into structural regions and the following six parameters are rated and summed:

| Parameter | Maximum rating |
|---|---|
| 1. Uniaxial compressive strength of intact rock | 15 |
| 2. Rock Quality Designation (RQD) | 20 |
| 3. Spacing of discontinuities | 20 |
| 4. Condition of discontinuities (persistence, aperture, roughness, infilling, weathering) | 30 |
| 5. Groundwater (inflow, water pressure ratio) | 15 |
| 6. Adjustment for orientation of discontinuities (very favourable 0 to very unfavourable $-12$ for tunnels) | 0 to $-12$ |

$RMR = \sum(1 \text{ to } 5) + \text{adjustment}$ ($0$ to $100$).

| RMR | 81 to 100 | 61 to 80 | 41 to 60 | 21 to 40 | $<$ 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Class | I | II | III | IV | V |
| Description | Very good | Good | Fair | Poor | Very poor |
| Cohesion (kPa) | $>$ 400 | 300 to 400 | 200 to 300 | 100 to 200 | $<$ 100 |
| Friction angle | $>$ 45° | 35° to 45° | 25° to 35° | 15° to 25° | $<$ 15° |
| Stand-up time | 20 yr for 15 m span | 1 yr for 10 m | 1 week for 5 m | 10 h for 2.5 m | 30 min for 1 m |

@s 4
**Major hazards in the context of Nepal.** Nepal is among the most hazard-prone countries in the world:

1. *Earthquakes:* the Himalaya is the collision zone of the Indian and Eurasian plates (1934 Bihar-Nepal M8.0, 2015 Gorkha M7.8).
2. *Landslides and debris flows,* especially in the monsoon, in the Siwalik, Lesser Himalaya and along roads.
3. *Floods and inundation* in the Terai, and flash floods in hill rivers.
4. *Glacial lake outburst floods (GLOF)* from the high Himalayan lakes (e.g. Tsho Rolpa, Imja).
5. *Others:* fire, drought, avalanches, windstorms and hailstorms, lightning, epidemics, and soil erosion.

**Magnitude and intensity.**

| Magnitude | Intensity |
|---|---|
| Measure of the energy released at the source | Measure of the effects of shaking at a place |
| Instrumental, logarithmic (Richter, $M_w$) | Descriptive, observed damage (Modified Mercalli, MMI I to XII; EMS) |
| One value for an earthquake | Varies with distance, local soil and building quality |
| No upper limit by definition, depends on fault size | Decreases away from epicentre |

@s 5
**Sub-surface investigation phase of an engineering-geological site investigation.** After the desk study and surface mapping, the sub-surface phase explores the ground below the surface:

1. *Test pits, trenches, shafts and adits:* direct observation of soil and weathered rock, sampling and in-situ tests (up to about 3 to 5 m for pits).
2. *Boring and drilling:* auger, wash boring, rotary core drilling; spacing and depth decided by the project (at least 1.5 times the foundation width below the base, or into sound rock); undisturbed (Shelby tube) and disturbed samples; core logging for RQD and recovery.
3. *In-situ tests:* standard penetration test (SPT), cone penetration test (CPT), vane shear, plate load, pressuremeter, packer (Lugeon) permeability and pumping tests.
4. *Geophysical methods:* seismic refraction, electrical resistivity, MASW and GPR between and beyond the boreholes.
5. *Groundwater observations:* water levels in boreholes and piezometers, seasonal variation, water sampling.
6. *Laboratory testing:* classification, strength, consolidation, rock testing.
7. *Report:* geological sections, borehole logs, design parameters and recommendations.

**(b) Three-point (borehole) problem.** Take $A$ (SW corner) as origin, $x$ east, $y$ north. Then $A(0,0)$, $B(1500,0)$ (SE), $C(0,1500)$ (NW) and $D(1500,1500)$ (NE). The depths to the coal seam are $A = 150$ m, $B = 450$ m, $C = 600$ m. (The seam is taken as a plane.)

The depth $z$ of the seam below the level ground is $z = 150 + ax + by$, with

$$\begin{gathered}a = \frac{450 - 150}{1500} = 0.20\ \text{(per m eastwards)} \\ b = \frac{600 - 150}{1500} = 0.30\ \text{(per m northwards)}.\end{gathered}$$

**(i) Attitude of the seam.** The maximum gradient is $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = \sqrt{0.04 + 0.09} = 0.3606$, so

$$\tan\delta = 0.3606 \ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\text{dip } \delta = 19.8^\circ}$$

The seam deepens towards $(a, b)$: dip direction $= \tan^{-1}(0.20/0.30) = 33.7^\circ$ east of north, i.e. $\mathbf{N33.7^\circ E}$ (azimuth $033.7^\circ$). The strike is perpendicular to this: $\mathbf{N56.3^\circ W}$ (azimuth $303.7^\circ$). Check with a strike line: the $300$ m depth contour passes through $(750, 0)$ on $AB$ and $(0, 500)$ on $AC$, i.e. it runs $750$ m east for $500$ m north, so its bearing is $\tan^{-1}(750/500) = 56.3^\circ$ west of north ✓.

**Attitude: strike N56.3°W, dip 19.8° towards N33.7°E (about 20° NE).**

**(ii) Depth of the borehole at $D$** (NE corner, $x = y = 1500$):

$$z_D = 150 + 0.20(1500) + 0.30(1500) = 150 + 300 + 450 = \boxed{900\ \text{m}}$$

(Check: $z_D = z_B + z_C - z_A = 450 + 600 - 150 = 900$ m, as the corners of a square form a parallelogram.)

**(iii) True thickness.** The borehole is vertical, so the apparent (vertical) thickness is $50$ m. The true thickness is measured perpendicular to the bedding:

$$t = 50\cos\delta = 50\cos19.8^\circ = \boxed{47.0\ \text{m}}$$

@s 6
**Data.** Hill slope N40°W/55° (strike N40°W = azimuth $320^\circ$, dip $55^\circ$); discontinuity N51°W/37° (strike azimuth $309^\circ$, dip $37^\circ$); friction angle $\phi = 28^\circ$. The dip directions are not stated; the scan data are taken as both planes dipping to the south-west (slope face dip direction $230^\circ$, plane dip direction $219^\circ$), which is the case of a road cut in which the discontinuity dips out of the face (the other case, dipping NE and into the hill, would be stable).

**Kinematic test (plane failure)**

1. Dip directions within $\pm20^\circ$: $230^\circ - 219^\circ = 11^\circ$ ✓
2. Plane daylights in the face: $\psi_p = 37^\circ < \psi_f = 55^\circ$ ✓
3. $\psi_p = 37^\circ > \phi = 28^\circ$ ✓

All three conditions are met, so the **possible mode of failure is plane (translational) sliding** along the phyllite discontinuity. Dry factor of safety:

$$FS = \frac{\tan\phi}{\tan\psi_p} = \frac{\tan28^\circ}{\tan37^\circ} = \frac{0.5317}{0.7536} = 0.71 < 1\ \text{(unstable)}$$

**Design of a stable cut slope.** On the stereonet the great circle of the cut face must not fall in the daylight region on the outer side of the discontinuity great circle. In angle terms, the face must be cut no steeper than the dip of the discontinuity so that the plane no longer daylights:

$$\psi_f \le \psi_p = 37^\circ \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{adopt a cut slope of about } 35^\circ\ (\text{slope } 1\mathrm{V} : 1.4\mathrm{H})$$

At $\psi_f = 35^\circ < 37^\circ$ the sliding surface is covered by rock and cannot slide (factor of safety against sliding is then governed by the friction angle, $\tan28^\circ / \tan 37^\circ$ applies only if the plane is exposed). If a steeper face (up to $55^\circ$) is needed, it must be benched, reinforced with rock bolts / anchors inclined to the plane, drained, and the plane cut back.
