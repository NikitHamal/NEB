@s 1
**Polarisation** is the deviation of the potential of an electrode from its equilibrium (reversible) value when a net current passes through it, caused by slow electrode processes (charge transfer, mass transport). **Overvoltage (overpotential)** $\eta$ is the *extra* voltage, above the reversible electrode potential, that must be applied to make an electrode reaction proceed at a given rate:
$$\eta = E_{applied} - E_{rev}$$
It has activation, concentration and ohmic parts; it is large for gases on some metals (hydrogen on Pb or Hg ≈ 1 V; on Pt nearly zero) and follows $\eta = a + b\log j$ (Tafel).

**Cell.** Ni has the lower reduction potential ($-0.25$ V), so Ni is the anode and Ag the cathode.
$$\text{Cell notation: }\text{Ni(s)}\,|\,\text{Ni}^{2+}(0.1\ \text{M})\,\|\,\text{Ag}^+(0.05\ \text{M})\,|\,\text{Ag(s)}$$
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Anode: Ni}\to\text{Ni}^{2+} + 2e^- \\ \text{Cathode: }2\text{Ag}^+ + 2e^-\to2\text{Ag} \\ \text{Cell reaction: Ni} + 2\text{Ag}^+\to\text{Ni}^{2+} + 2\text{Ag},\quad n = 2\end{gathered}$$
$$E^\circ_{cell} = 0.80 - (-0.25) = 1.05\ \text{V}$$
Nernst equation at $T = 303$ K, with $\dfrac{2.303RT}{F} = \dfrac{2.303(8.314)(303)}{96485} = 0.0601$ V:
$$E_{cell} = E^\circ - \frac{0.0601}{2}\log\frac{[\text{Ni}^{2+}]}{[\text{Ag}^+]^2} = 1.05 - 0.03007\log\frac{0.1}{(0.05)^2}$$
$$= 1.05 - 0.03007\log40 = 1.05 - 0.03007(1.602)$$
$$\boxed{E_{cell} = 1.05 - 0.0482 = 1.00\ \text{V}}$$

**Or. Lead–acid battery.** *Construction:* anode (negative plate) spongy Pb; cathode (positive) $\text{PbO}_2$ on a lead grid; electrolyte about 37% (density 1.28 g/cm³) $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$; a cell gives about 2.0 V (six cells for a 12 V battery).
*Working (discharge):*
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Anode: Pb} + \text{SO}_4^{2-}\to\text{PbSO}_4 + 2e^- \\ \text{Cathode: PbO}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + \text{SO}_4^{2-} + 2e^-\to\text{PbSO}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \\ \text{Overall: Pb} + \text{PbO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\rightleftharpoons2\text{PbSO}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}\end{gathered}$$
On *charging* with an external source the reaction is reversed ($\text{PbSO}_4$ is converted back to Pb and $\text{PbO}_2$ and the acid is regenerated); the density of acid shows the state of charge.
*Uses:* starting, lighting and ignition (SLI) in cars, trucks and motorcycles; inverters and UPS; telecom and emergency power back-up; solar storage; submarines; forklifts and electric vehicles (golf carts).
*Advantages:* cheap; high surge current (starter motor); rechargeable, reliable, mature and 99% recyclable; works over a wide temperature range; simple to maintain.
*Disadvantages:* heavy and bulky (low energy density, 30–40 Wh/kg); short cycle life; slow charge; corrosive acid and release of hydrogen (explosive); lead is toxic; sulfation if left discharged.

@s 2
**(a) Benzoic acid + sodium benzoate buffer.** $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$ is a weak acid, only slightly ionised, whereas sodium benzoate is a strong electrolyte that is completely ionised and provides a large concentration of benzoate ions $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}^-$. The common ion suppresses the ionisation of the acid, so the solution has a large reserve of undissociated acid (to neutralise added base) and of benzoate (to neutralise added acid).
- *Addition of a small amount of strong acid:* the extra $\text{H}^+$ combines with benzoate ions, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+\to\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH}$, so $[\text{H}^+]$ stays almost constant.
- *Addition of a small amount of strong alkali:* $\text{OH}^-$ is neutralised by the acid, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COOH} + \text{OH}^-\to\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. The acid replaced is a weak acid, so the pH changes very little.
Quantitatively, $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\dfrac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]}$ ($\text{p}K_a = 4.20$): a small addition changes the ratio only slightly, so the pH is almost constant (the buffer is most effective when the ratio is 1, i.e. $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$, and within $\text{p}K_a\pm1$).

**(b) Sodium formate.**
$$\text{p}K_a = -\log(1.8\times10^{-4}) = 3.745$$
$$\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{HCOONa}]}{[\text{HCOOH}]}\ \Rightarrow\ 3.80 = 3.745 + \log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{0.10}$$
$$\log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{0.10} = 0.055\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{[\text{salt}]}{0.10} = 1.135$$
$$\boxed{[\text{HCOONa}] = 0.114\ \text{M}}$$

@s 3
**Four selection criteria of a catalyst.**
1. **Activity:** it must raise the reaction rate greatly (a high turnover frequency) at moderate temperature and pressure by providing a path of lower activation energy.
2. **Selectivity:** it should direct the reaction towards the desired product with few side-products (e.g. Ni vs Cu for hydrogenation, Ziegler–Natta for stereoregular polymers).
3. **Stability / lifetime:** it must keep its structure and activity over long operation (resist sintering, poisoning by S, CO, Pb, and coking) and be regenerable.
4. **Surface area, porosity and mechanical strength** (support, particle size) and **ease of separation**; also **low cost** and availability, and no toxic by-products.

**Electrocatalytic water splitting.** Water is split by electrolysis into hydrogen and oxygen, $2\text{H}_2\text{O}\to2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$, with a thermodynamic minimum voltage of $E^\circ = 1.23$ V ($\Delta G = +237$ kJ/mol). In practice 1.8–2.0 V is needed because of the overpotentials of the two half-reactions, and an **electrocatalyst** on each electrode lowers them.
*Acidic medium (PEM electrolyser):*
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Cathode (HER): }2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-\to\text{H}_2\qquad(\text{Pt, MoS}_2) \\ \text{Anode (OER): }2\text{H}_2\text{O}\to\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4e^-\qquad(\text{IrO}_2,\ \text{RuO}_2)\end{gathered}$$
*Alkaline medium:*
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Cathode: }2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 2e^-\to\text{H}_2 + 2\text{OH}^-\qquad(\text{Pt, Ni–Mo}) \\ \text{Anode: }4\text{OH}^-\to\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4e^-\qquad(\text{NiFe oxyhydroxide, Co}_3\text{O}_4)\end{gathered}$$
The hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) is a two-electron process that needs one intermediate (adsorbed H); the oxygen evolution reaction (OER) is a four-electron, sluggish reaction and the main source of loss. A good electrocatalyst has a low overpotential (small Tafel slope), a high exchange current density, and stability; non-precious catalysts (Ni, Fe, Co, MoS₂, phosphides) are replacing Pt and Ir. If the power comes from solar or wind, the hydrogen produced is "green hydrogen".

@s 4
**NMR spectroscopy: principle.** Nuclei having spin ($^1\text{H}$, $^{13}\text{C}$, $^{19}\text{F}$, $^{31}\text{P}$; $I = \tfrac12$) behave like tiny magnets. In an applied magnetic field $B_0$, a proton can align with the field (low energy, $\alpha$) or against it (high energy, $\beta$); the energy gap is $\Delta E = \dfrac{\gamma h}{2\pi}B_0$. Irradiation with radio waves of frequency
$$\nu = \frac{\gamma B_0}{2\pi}$$
makes the nuclei flip (**resonance**) and the absorption is recorded. The electrons around a proton shield it, so the effective field is $B_0(1 - \sigma)$; protons in different environments absorb at slightly different frequencies, shown as the **chemical shift**
$$\delta = \frac{\nu_{sample} - \nu_{TMS}}{\nu_{spectrometer}}\times10^6\ \text{ppm}$$
Number of signals = number of kinds of H; integrated area = number of H of each kind; splitting by the $n + 1$ rule = number of neighbouring H.

**Applications:** (i) determination of the structure of organic compounds (functional groups, connectivity); (ii) checking the purity and identifying isomers; (iii) quantitative analysis of mixtures; (iv) study of reaction kinetics and of hydrogen bonding and conformations; (v) **MRI** in medicine (proton NMR of water in tissue); (vi) identification of drugs and natural products, and food analysis (oil content).

**Why tetramethylsilane (TMS) is the reference.** $\text{Si(CH}_3)_4$:
1. All **12 protons are equivalent**, giving one sharp, intense singlet.
2. Silicon is less electronegative than carbon, so the protons are **highly shielded** and absorb at higher field than almost all organic protons: $\delta = 0$ lies at the end of the scale, with a positive $\delta$ for others, avoiding overlap.
3. It is **chemically inert** and does not react with the sample; it is **volatile** (bp 27°C) and easily removed after the measurement, and it is soluble in most organic solvents.

**Or. (a) Paper chromatography.** It is a *partition* chromatography. The stationary phase is the water (about 20%) adsorbed on the cellulose fibres of the paper; the mobile phase is an organic solvent mixture (e.g. butanol–acetic acid–water). A spot of the mixture is applied at the base line and the paper is dipped (its lower edge) in the solvent in a closed jar. The solvent rises by capillary action and the components partition between the stationary water and the moving solvent according to their partition coefficients. A component more soluble in the mobile phase moves farther. The spots are developed (ninhydrin for amino acids) and identified by
$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the solute}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front}}$$

**(b) Hypsochromic and bathochromic shifts.** Both describe a shift of the absorption maximum $\lambda_{max}$ in the UV–visible spectrum caused by substitution or by the solvent.
- **Bathochromic shift (red shift):** the shift of $\lambda_{max}$ to a **longer** wavelength. It is caused by extended conjugation or by an auxochrome (–OH, –NH₂), and by solvent polarity for $\pi\to\pi^*$ transitions. Example: *p*-nitrophenol (317 nm) shifts to 400 nm in alkaline solution (phenoxide ion).
- **Hypsochromic shift (blue shift):** the shift of $\lambda_{max}$ to a **shorter** wavelength, due to loss of conjugation or the effect of a polar solvent on $n\to\pi^*$ transitions. Example: aniline ($\lambda_{max}$ 280 nm) in acid becomes anilinium ion (254 nm), as the lone pair is no longer in conjugation with the ring.
(Hyperchromic and hypochromic effects refer to an increase and a decrease in absorption intensity.)

@s 5
**(a) Complex compound.** A compound in which a central metal atom or ion is bound to a definite number of molecules or ions (ligands) by coordinate (dative) bonds, e.g. $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]\text{Cl}_3$, $\text{K}_4[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]$. The complex ion retains its identity in solution.

**Crystal-field splitting of $d^7$ in an octahedral field.** The $d$ orbitals split into $t_{2g}$ ($-0.4\Delta_o$) and $e_g$ ($+0.6\Delta_o$). The seven electrons of $\text{Co}^{2+}$ can be arranged in two ways:
- **Weak field** ($\Delta_o<P$), high spin: $t_{2g}^5e_g^2$, with 3 unpaired electrons (paramagnetic). CFSE $= 5(-0.4) + 2(0.6) = -0.8\Delta_o$. Example $[\text{Co(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}$ (pink), $\mu = \sqrt{15} = 3.87$ BM.
- **Strong field** ($\Delta_o>P$), low spin: $t_{2g}^6e_g^1$, with 1 unpaired electron. CFSE $= 6(-0.4) + 1(0.6) = -1.8\Delta_o$ (plus one extra pairing energy $P$). Example $[\text{Co(CN)}_6]^{4-}$ (a strong reducing agent since the single $e_g$ electron is easily lost), $\mu = 1.73$ BM.
Configuration: $t_{2g}^5e_g^2$ (high spin) $\rightleftharpoons$ $t_{2g}^6e_g^1$ (low spin). The $e_g^1$ electron in the low-spin form causes Jahn–Teller distortion.

**(b) Alloys.** An alloy is a homogeneous mixture (solid solution, or a mixture of metals with other elements) of two or more metals, or of a metal with a non-metal, with properties better than the pure metal (harder, stronger, less corrosive, lower melting point). Examples: brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), steel (Fe + C), solder (Pb + Sn), duralumin (Al + Cu + Mg + Mn).
**Applications of aluminium alloys:** aircraft and spacecraft structures (duralumin), automobile bodies and engine parts, bicycles and ships (Al–Mg), power transmission lines (ACSR), building frames and roofing, cookware and packaging (foil).

@s 6
**(a) VBT for the two nickel(II) complexes.** $\text{Ni}^{2+}$: $[\text{Ar}]3d^8$, with two unpaired electrons.

**$[\text{NiCl}_4]^{2-}$:** $\text{Cl}^-$ is a weak-field ligand and does not pair the $3d$ electrons, so the two unpaired electrons remain in $3d$. The four ligand lone pairs are accepted by one vacant $4s$ and three $4p$ orbitals:
$$\text{Ni}^{2+}:\ 3d^8\ [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow][\uparrow]\ \ 4s^0\,4p^0\ \xrightarrow{sp^3}\ \text{four }\text{Cl}^-\ \text{pairs}$$
$sp^3$ hybridisation $\Rightarrow$ **tetrahedral**; two unpaired electrons $\Rightarrow$ **paramagnetic** ($\mu = \sqrt8 = 2.83$ BM).

**$[\text{Ni(CN)}_4]^{2-}$:** $\text{CN}^-$ is a strong-field ligand: it pairs the two unpaired $3d$ electrons into the same orbital and leaves one $3d$ orbital ($3d_{x^2-y^2}$) empty. This orbital, the $4s$ and two $4p$ orbitals hybridise to $dsp^2$ and accept four electron pairs:
$$\text{Ni}^{2+}:\ 3d^8\ [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\ \ ]\ \xrightarrow{dsp^2}\ \text{four }\text{CN}^-\ \text{pairs}$$
$dsp^2$ hybridisation $\Rightarrow$ **square planar**; no unpaired electron $\Rightarrow$ **diamagnetic**. (Four-coordinate $d^8$ ions with strong-field ligands prefer square planar geometry.)

**IUPAC names.**
- (i) $\text{Na}_3[\text{AgF}_4]$: the anion $[\text{AgF}_4]^{3-}$ gives $x - 4 = -3$, so Ag is $+1$: **sodium tetrafluoridoargentate(I)**.
- (ii) $[\text{Co(en)}_3]\text{Br}_3$: **tris(ethane-1,2-diamine)cobalt(III) bromide** ($x + 0 = +3$).

@s 7
**(a) Four principles of green chemistry.**
1. **Waste prevention:** it is better to prevent waste than to treat or clean it up (e.g. an ibuprofen synthesis in 3 steps instead of 6).
2. **Atom economy:** synthetic methods should incorporate as much as possible of the reactant atoms into the final product, $\text{AE} = \dfrac{M_{product}}{\sum M_{reactants}}\times100$ (addition and rearrangement reactions are 100%).
3. **Safer solvents and auxiliaries:** avoid hazardous solvents; use water, supercritical $\text{CO}_2$ or ionic liquids or run solvent-free.
4. **Catalysis:** catalytic reagents (selective) are superior to stoichiometric ones; also energy efficiency and renewable feedstock, design for degradation, inherently safer chemistry.

**Heavy metal ions.** Metal ions with a density greater than about 5 g/cm³ that are toxic even at low concentrations and are non-biodegradable and bio-accumulate: $\text{Pb}^{2+}$, $\text{Hg}^{2+}$, $\text{Cd}^{2+}$, $\text{As}^{3+}$, $\text{Cr}^{6+}$, $\text{Ni}^{2+}$, $\text{Cu}^{2+}$, $\text{Zn}^{2+}$. They come from mining, electroplating, tanneries, batteries, paints and pesticide industries, and cause kidney and brain damage, cancer (Cr, As, Cd), and Minamata (Hg) and itai-itai (Cd) disease.

**Removal by chemical (hydroxide/sulfide) precipitation** (the most common method):
1. The effluent is adjusted to pH 8–11 with lime ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$) or NaOH, which precipitates the metals as insoluble hydroxides:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{M}^{n+} + n\text{OH}^-\to\text{M(OH)}_n\downarrow \\ (\text{e.g. Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{OH}^-\to\text{Pb(OH)}_2)\end{gathered}$$
2. $\text{Cr(VI)}$ is first reduced to $\text{Cr(III)}$ at pH 2–3 ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-} + 3\text{SO}_2 + 2\text{H}^+\to2\text{Cr}^{3+} + 3\text{SO}_4^{2-} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$) and then precipitated as $\text{Cr(OH)}_3$.
3. For lower residuals sulfide ($\text{Na}_2\text{S}$) is used: $\text{M}^{2+} + \text{S}^{2-}\to\text{MS}\downarrow$ (HgS, CdS, PbS are far less soluble than the hydroxides).
4. The precipitate is coagulated with alum or $\text{FeCl}_3$, settled in a clarifier, filtered and disposed of safely (sludge).
(Other methods: ion exchange, adsorption on activated carbon, reverse osmosis and electrodialysis, phytoremediation.)

**(b) Air pollutants.** Substances present in the air in a concentration and for a duration that is harmful to humans, animals, plants and materials. *Primary* pollutants are emitted directly ($\text{CO}$, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{NO}_x$, PM, VOC), *secondary* ones form in the air ($\text{O}_3$, PAN, acid rain, smog).

**Particulate matter (PM).** Solid or liquid particles suspended in the air: dust, soot, smoke, fly ash, pollen, aerosols. $\text{PM}_{10}$ ($<10\ \mu$m) and $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ ($<2.5\ \mu$m) are produced by vehicles, brick kilns, burning of biomass and waste, construction and industries.
*Effects:* $\text{PM}_{2.5}$ penetrates deep into the lungs and the blood, causing asthma, bronchitis, lung cancer and heart disease; reduces visibility (haze); settles on plants (lower photosynthesis) and buildings (soiling); and it affects the climate by scattering and absorbing the sunlight.
*Remedies:* cyclone separators, electrostatic precipitators, bag filters and wet scrubbers in industries; clean fuels and vehicle emission standards; dust control, green belts and the stopping of open burning.

**Greenhouse gases (GHGs):** $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, CFCs, water vapour, $\text{O}_3$. They allow the short-wave solar radiation to pass but absorb the long-wave infrared radiation from the earth and re-emit it, trapping heat (the greenhouse effect), and enhanced by burning fossil fuels, deforestation, agriculture (rice paddies, livestock) and landfills.
*Effects:* global warming, melting glaciers (Himalaya) and sea-level rise, climate change with floods and droughts, ocean acidification ($\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\to\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$), loss of biodiversity, and food insecurity.
*Remedies:* renewable energy, efficiency and public transport, reforestation, carbon capture, and methane capture from landfills; lower fossil fuel use (Paris Agreement).

**Carbon nanotube (CNT) vs graphene.** Both are allotropes of carbon with $sp^2$-hybridised atoms in a hexagonal lattice.

| Property | Carbon nanotube | Graphene |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | rolled-up graphene sheet into a hollow cylinder (single or multi-walled), diameter 1–50 nm, length µm–mm | a single flat sheet of carbon atoms, one atom thick (0.34 nm) |
| Dimension | 1-D | 2-D |
| Electrical behaviour | metallic or semiconducting depending on chirality $(n,m)$ | zero-band-gap semimetal, very high electron mobility ($\sim2\times10^5\ \text{cm}^2\text{V}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$) |
| Mechanical | tensile strength about 100 times that of steel, flexible | the strongest known material (130 GPa), stretchable |
| Surface area | about 1300 $\text{m}^2/\text{g}$ | about 2630 $\text{m}^2/\text{g}$ theoretical |
| Use | composites, field-effect transistors, field emitters, sensors, hydrogen storage | transparent electrodes, supercapacitors, batteries, flexible electronics, membranes |

**Nanomaterials of various dimensions.** Materials with at least one dimension of 1–100 nm. Classified by the number of dimensions that are free (not confined to the nanoscale):
- **0-D** (all three dimensions in the nanoscale): nanoparticles, quantum dots, fullerenes: gold and silver nanoparticles, CdSe quantum dots, $\text{C}_{60}$.
- **1-D** (two dimensions in the nanoscale): nanotubes, nanowires, nanorods and nanofibres: carbon nanotubes, ZnO and Si nanowires.
- **2-D** (one dimension in the nanoscale): nanosheets and thin films: graphene, MXene, $\text{MoS}_2$, and nanocoatings.
- **3-D** (bulk, with nano-sized building blocks): nanocomposites, nanocrystalline powders and multilayers, aerogels, and dendrimers.
Properties change because of the quantum size effect (a larger band gap for smaller particles, e.g. colour of quantum dots) and the large surface-to-volume ratio.

@s 9
**(a) Polycarbonate (PC).** *Preparation:* step-growth (condensation) polymerisation of **bisphenol-A** with **phosgene** (interfacial process, NaOH/$\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2$) with elimination of HCl, or trans-esterification with diphenyl carbonate (loses phenol):
$$n\,\text{HO–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–C(CH}_3)_2\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–OH} + n\,\text{COCl}_2\xrightarrow{\text{NaOH}}[\text{–O–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–C(CH}_3)_2\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–O–CO–}]_n + 2n\,\text{HCl}$$
*Uses:* transparent, tough, impact-resistant sheets: bullet-resistant glass, riot shields, helmets, spectacle lenses, CDs and DVDs, electrical and electronic housings, greenhouse panels, car headlamp covers and water bottles.

**Polyurethane (PU).** *Preparation:* addition (step) polymerisation of a **diisocyanate** (toluene diisocyanate, TDI, or MDI) with a **diol/polyol** (ethylene glycol or polyether polyol), with a catalyst (amine, tin compound); no by-product:
$$n\,\text{OCN–R–NCO} + n\,\text{HO–R'–OH}\to[\text{–CO–NH–R–NH–CO–O–R'–O–}]_n$$
Foam is made by adding water, which reacts with –NCO to give $\text{CO}_2$ as the blowing agent: $\text{R–NCO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\to\text{R–NH}_2 + \text{CO}_2$.
*Uses:* flexible foams (mattresses, cushions, car seats), rigid foam insulation (refrigerators, buildings), coatings and paints, adhesives, elastomers (shoe soles, tyres, rollers), synthetic leather and spandex (Lycra) fibre.

**(b) Addition polymerisation.** Polymerisation in which unsaturated monomers (containing a C=C) add to one another, one after another, without loss of any atom, to form a long chain (chain-growth). *Example:* ethylene to polyethylene, $n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2\to[\text{–CH}_2\text{–CH}_2\text{–}]_n$; vinyl chloride to PVC; styrene to polystyrene.

**Free-radical mechanism** (for example the polymerisation of ethylene/vinyl chloride with benzoyl peroxide as initiator, $\text{I}$):
1. **Initiation:** the initiator decomposes on heating to give free radicals, and a radical adds to a monomer molecule:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{(C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO)}_2\xrightarrow{\Delta}2\,\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}\cdot\ (\to2\,\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\cdot + 2\text{CO}_2) \\ R\cdot + \text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHX}\to R\text{–CH}_2\text{–C}\cdot\text{HX}\end{gathered}$$
2. **Propagation:** the new radical adds to further monomer molecules, one at a time (head-to-tail):
$$R\text{–CH}_2\text{C}\cdot\text{HX} + n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHX}\to R\text{–(CH}_2\text{CHX)}_n\text{–CH}_2\text{C}\cdot\text{HX}$$
3. **Termination:** the growing chains end by *combination* of two radicals, $\sim\text{C}\cdot + \cdot\text{C}\sim\to\sim\text{C–C}\sim$, or by *disproportionation* (transfer of a hydrogen atom, giving one saturated and one unsaturated chain end).
Chain transfer to monomer, solvent or polymer limits the molar mass and causes branching (low-density polyethylene).

**Or. Conducting polymers.** Organic polymers with a conjugated backbone (alternating single and double bonds) that conduct electricity when doped (oxidised or reduced): polyacetylene, polyaniline, polypyrrole, polythiophene, PEDOT. Doping (p-type with $\text{I}_2$ or acids, n-type with Na) creates mobile charge carriers (polarons and bipolarons) and raises the conductivity from $10^{-10}$ up to $10^{3}$–$10^{5}$ S/cm.

**Polyaniline (PANI).** *Preparation:* **oxidative polymerisation** of aniline in an aqueous acid (1 M HCl) at 0–5°C using ammonium persulfate as oxidant:
$$n\,\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2\xrightarrow{(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8,\ \text{HCl},\ 0\text{–}5^\circ\text{C}}[\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–NH–}]_n$$
Radical cations form, couple head-to-tail and grow into the dark green precipitate of the **emeraldine salt** (conducting form), which is de-doped by a base to the blue emeraldine base (insulator).
*Uses:* anti-corrosion coatings on steel, antistatic and EMI-shielding coatings, rechargeable batteries and supercapacitors, sensors (gas, pH), electrochromic displays, OLEDs, and as a transparent electrode.

**Cellulose.** A natural linear polysaccharide $(\text{C}_6\text{H}_{10}\text{O}_5)_n$ of $\beta$-D-glucose units joined by $\beta$-(1→4) glycosidic bonds. Hydrogen bonding between chains makes it a strong, insoluble fibre; it is the main constituent of cotton and wood and gives plant cell walls strength. Uses: paper, textiles (cotton and viscose rayon), cellulose acetate/nitrate, and carboxymethyl cellulose.

@s 10
**(a) Solid lubricants.** Solid materials with a layered or low-shear structure, which separate the sliding surfaces and reduce friction without needing oil, effective at very high temperature or load, in vacuum, or where oils would contaminate. Examples: **graphite, molybdenum disulfide ($\text{MoS}_2$)**, talc, boron nitride, PTFE and soft metals (Pb, Sn).

**Why graphite is a lubricant.** Graphite has a **layered structure**: carbon atoms in each layer are bonded in hexagonal rings by strong covalent ($sp^2$) bonds, but the layers are held to one another only by weak van der Waals forces (distance 0.335 nm). The layers therefore **slide easily over one another** under a shear force, which gives a low coefficient of friction. Other reasons: stable up to about 600°C (oxidising atmosphere) and higher in inert conditions, chemically inert, a good conductor of heat and electricity, and it adheres to metal surfaces (as colloidal graphite in oil, "oildag", and as a dry powder for locks and hot machinery). (Its lubricity depends on adsorbed water vapour and it is poor in vacuum.)

**(b) Preparation.**
*TNT (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene):* three-stage nitration of toluene with a mixture of conc. $\text{HNO}_3$ and conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, at progressively higher temperature:
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_3\xrightarrow{30^\circ\text{C}}\text{mono-nitro}\xrightarrow{60^\circ\text{C}}\text{di-nitro}\xrightarrow{100\text{–}115^\circ\text{C}}\text{O}_2\text{N–}(\text{C}_6\text{H}_2)(\text{CH}_3)(\text{NO}_2)_2$$
The sulfuric acid generates $\text{NO}_2^+$ and absorbs the water formed. The crude product is washed with water and aqueous sodium sulfite and crystallised from alcohol.

*GTN (glyceryl trinitrate, nitroglycerine):* esterification of glycerol with a nitrating mixture (conc. $\text{HNO}_3$ + conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) kept below 10–15°C with cooling and stirring (highly exothermic):
$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3 + 3\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ <15^\circ\text{C}}\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{ONO}_2)_3 + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The oily layer is separated, washed with water and sodium carbonate, and handled as a dynamite (absorbed in kieselguhr) for safety.
