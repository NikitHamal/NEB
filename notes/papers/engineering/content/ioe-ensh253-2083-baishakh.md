@s 1
**Range vs standard deviation.**

| | **Range** | **Standard deviation** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | $R = x_{max} - x_{min}$ | $s = \sqrt{\dfrac{\sum(x_i - \bar x)^2}{n - 1}}$ |
| Data used | only the two extreme values | every observation |
| Sensitivity | very sensitive to a single outlier | uses all deviations, so is more stable |
| Use | quick, rough measure (e.g. control charts for small samples) | standard measure of spread in inference |

Both have the units of the data, so they cannot compare two sets with different means; the **coefficient of variation** $CV = \dfrac{s}{\bar x}\times100\%$ is used to judge consistency (the smaller, the more consistent).

**Machine A:** $\bar x = \dfrac{1670}{6} = 278.33$, $\sum(x - \bar x)^2 = 2233.33$, $s = \sqrt{2233.33/5} = 21.13$, $R = 300 - 250 = 50$.

$$CV_A = \frac{21.13}{278.33}\times100 = 7.59\%$$

**Machine B:** $\bar x = \dfrac{1740}{6} = 290$, $\sum(x - \bar x)^2 = 278$, $s = \sqrt{278/5} = 7.46$, $R = 298 - 280 = 18$.

$$CV_B = \frac{7.46}{290}\times100 = 2.57\%$$

| Machine | Mean | Range | SD | CV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 278.33 | 50 | 21.13 | 7.59 % |
| B | 290.00 | 18 | 7.46 | 2.57 % |

$$\boxed{\text{Machine B is more consistent: } CV_B = 2.57\% < CV_A = 7.59\%}$$

Machine B also has the higher mean output and a smaller range and SD. (Using the population SD, $\sigma$, gives $6.93\%$ and $2.35\%$, the same conclusion.)

**Additive law.** For any two events, $P(A\cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A\cap B)$. For **independent** events $P(A\cap B) = P(A)P(B)$, so

$$P(A\cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A)P(B).$$

(For mutually exclusive events $P(A\cap B) = 0$ and the law reduces to $P(A) + P(B)$.)

Let $M$ = man alive, $W$ = wife alive: $P(M) = 0.3$, $P(W) = 0.4$, independent.

**(a)** $P(\text{both alive}) = P(M)P(W) = 0.3\times0.4$

$$\boxed{0.12}$$

**(b)** $P(\text{at least one alive}) = P(M\cup W) = 0.3 + 0.4 - 0.12$

$$\boxed{0.58}$$

Check by complement: $1 - P(M')P(W') = 1 - (0.7)(0.6) = 1 - 0.42 = 0.58$ ✓.

|---|---|---|

**Normal and standard normal distribution.** A normal variable $X\sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$ has density $f(x) = \dfrac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-(x-\mu)^2/2\sigma^2}$, a bell curve symmetric about $\mu$ with spread $\sigma$. Every normal distribution is converted to the **standard normal** $Z\sim N(0, 1)$ by the transformation

$$\begin{gathered}Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma} \\ \phi(z) = \frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-z^2/2}.\end{gathered}$$

So one table of $\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z)$ serves all normal distributions: $P(X \le x) = \Phi\!\left(\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}\right)$. The standard normal is the special case $\mu = 0$, $\sigma = 1$.

**Problem.** $\mu = 3500$, $\sigma = 700$:

$$P(X < 1800) = P\!\left(Z < \frac{1800 - 3500}{700}\right) = P(Z < -2.43) = 1 - \Phi(2.43) = 1 - 0.9925 = 0.0075$$

(With exact $z = -2.4286$ the probability is $0.00758$.)

$$\text{Expected failures} = 3000\times0.0075\ \approx\ 22.7$$

$$\boxed{\approx 23\ \text{bulbs}}$$

**OR**

**Central Limit Theorem.** If $\bar X$ is the mean of a random sample of size $n$ from **any** population with mean $\mu$ and finite variance $\sigma^2$, then as $n$ grows large (practically $n \ge 30$) the sampling distribution of $\bar X$ is approximately normal with

$$\begin{gathered}\mu_{\bar X} = \mu \\ \sigma_{\bar X} = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} \\ Z = \frac{\bar X - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt n}\sim N(0, 1).\end{gathered}$$

If the population itself is normal, $\bar X$ is exactly normal for every $n$.

**(a)** $\mu = 50$, $\sigma = 10$, $n = 25$:

$$\boxed{\mu_{\bar X} = 50\ \text{V},\qquad \sigma_{\bar X} = \frac{10}{\sqrt{25}} = 2\ \text{V}}$$

**(b)**

$$P(\bar X > 52) = P\!\left(Z > \frac{52 - 50}{2}\right) = P(Z > 1) = 1 - 0.8413$$

$$\boxed{P(\bar X > 52) = 0.1587}$$

@s 6
**Parameter vs statistic.**

| | **Parameter** | **Statistic** |
|---|---|---|
| Describes | a **population** | a **sample** |
| Value | fixed, usually unknown | varies from sample to sample, computable |
| Symbols | $\mu,\ \sigma,\ \sigma^2,\ p$ | $\bar x,\ s,\ s^2,\ \hat p$ |
| Role | what we want to know | used to estimate the parameter |

*Example.* The mean length $\mu$ of **all** iron rods produced in a plant is a parameter. The mean $\bar x = 4.6$ ft of the 100 rods actually measured is a statistic, an estimate of $\mu$. Another sample of 100 would give a different $\bar x$.

**Confidence limits.** $n = 100$ (large), $\sigma = 0.6$ known, $\bar x = 4.6$, $z_{0.025} = 1.96$:

$$\bar x \pm z_{\alpha/2}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n} = 4.6 \pm 1.96\times\frac{0.6}{\sqrt{100}} = 4.6 \pm 1.96(0.06) = 4.6 \pm 0.1176$$

$$\boxed{4.482\ \text{ft} < \mu < 4.718\ \text{ft}}$$

We are 95% confident that the population mean length lies between $4.482$ and $4.718$ ft. (The stated population mean of 4 ft is outside this interval, which suggests the sample did not come from a population of mean 4 ft.)

@s 7
**Analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA)** tests whether the means of $k \ge 3$ groups are all equal by comparing the variation *between* groups with the variation *within* groups. The essential steps:

**Step 1. Check the assumptions.** (i) The samples are independent random samples; (ii) each population is (approximately) normal; (iii) the populations have equal variances (homogeneity, e.g. Levene or Bartlett test, or the rule $s_{max}/s_{min} < 2$).

**Step 2. State the hypotheses.**

$$\begin{gathered}H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \cdots = \mu_k \\ H_1: \text{at least one mean differs}\end{gathered}$$

and choose the significance level $\alpha$ (usually $0.05$).

**Step 3. Compute the sums of squares.** With $k$ groups, $n_i$ observations in group $i$, $N = \sum n_i$ observations in all, $T_i$ = group totals, $T$ = grand total, correction factor $CF = T^2/N$:

$$\begin{gathered}SST = \sum_i\sum_j x_{ij}^2 - CF \\ SSB = \sum_i\frac{T_i^2}{n_i} - CF \\ SSW = SST - SSB\end{gathered}$$

**Step 4. Degrees of freedom and mean squares.**

$$\begin{gathered}df_B = k - 1 \\ df_W = N - k \\ MSB = \frac{SSB}{k - 1} \\ MSW = \frac{SSW}{N - k}\end{gathered}$$

**Step 5. Compute the test statistic.** $F = \dfrac{MSB}{MSW}$, which follows the $F$ distribution with $(k - 1,\ N - k)$ degrees of freedom when $H_0$ is true. Summarise in an ANOVA table:

| Source | SS | df | MS | $F$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Between groups | SSB | $k - 1$ | MSB | MSB/MSW |
| Within groups | SSW | $N - k$ | MSW | |
| Total | SST | $N - 1$ | | |

**Step 6. Find the critical value** $F_\alpha(k - 1, N - k)$ from the $F$ table (or compute the $p$-value).

**Step 7. Decision.** If $F > F_\alpha$ (or $p < \alpha$) reject $H_0$: the group means are not all equal. Otherwise do not reject $H_0$.

**Step 8. Conclude, and follow up.** State the conclusion in the context of the problem. If $H_0$ is rejected, use a *post-hoc* test (Tukey HSD, Fisher LSD, Bonferroni) to find which pairs of means differ.

The same 8 road sections are measured before and after the treatment, so the data are **paired**. Use the **paired $t$-test** on the differences $d = \text{after} - \text{before}$ (one-tailed, since we test for an *improvement*).

| Section | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 | 8 | Σ |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $d$ | 3 | 2 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 2 | 17 |
| $d^2$ | 9 | 4 | 4 | 1 | 4 | 4 | 9 | 4 | 39 |

$H_0: \mu_d = 0$ (no improvement) against $H_1: \mu_d > 0$ (treatment improves slip resistance); $\alpha = 0.05$.

$$\begin{gathered}\bar d = \frac{17}{8} = 2.125 \\ s_d^2 = \frac{39 - 17^2/8}{7} = \frac{2.875}{7} = 0.4107 \\ s_d = 0.6409\end{gathered}$$

$$t = \frac{\bar d}{s_d/\sqrt n} = \frac{2.125}{0.6409/\sqrt8} = \frac{2.125}{0.2266} = 9.38$$

Degrees of freedom $= 7$; critical value $t_{0.05,7} = 1.895$ (one-tailed).

Since $t = 9.38 > 1.895$ (the $p$-value is about $1.6\times10^{-5}$), we reject $H_0$.

$$\boxed{\text{The treatment significantly improves slip resistance (mean gain } 2.125\text{ units)}}$$
