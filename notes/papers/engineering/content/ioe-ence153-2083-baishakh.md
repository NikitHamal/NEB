@s 1
**Primary classification of surveying.** Surveying is classified first by the *basis on which the earth's curvature is treated* and then by the *nature of the work*:

| Basis | Plane surveying | Geodetic surveying |
|---|---|---|
| Earth's surface | Taken as a plane; curvature ignored | Curvature of the earth (spheroid) is considered |
| Area | Normally up to about 250 km² | Large areas, over about 250 km² |
| Level surface | Level line taken as straight; plumb lines as parallel | Level line is a curve; plumb lines converge to the earth's centre |
| Triangles | Plane triangles, plane trigonometry | Spherical triangles, spherical excess |
| Accuracy and instruments | Ordinary (chain, compass, theodolite, level) | High (precise theodolites, base-line tapes, EDM, GNSS) |
| Examples | Road, canal, building-site, small topographic surveys | National triangulation and control network; boundary of a country; large-bridge control |

**Why from whole to part.** In a large area a few control points are first fixed with the highest accuracy (the *whole*), and the smaller detail is then fixed *inside* this frame by less precise methods (the *part*). With this order the small errors of the detail work stay inside their own small blocks and cannot build up. If the work went from part to whole, the small errors of each part would accumulate and magnify as the survey is extended, and nothing would be left to check the large frame. The principle therefore (i) controls the accumulation of error, (ii) lets the frame be adjusted once for closing errors, and (iii) allows the detail surveys to be done separately by different parties.

@s 2
**Horizontal distances on sloping ground.**

1. **Direct (stepping) method.** The tape is held horizontally and the distance is measured in steps, with a plumb bob used to transfer the point to the ground (suitable for steep slopes).
2. **Slope measurement and reduction.** The slope distance $L$ is measured along the ground together with the angle of slope $\theta$ (clinometer or theodolite), or the difference of level $h$ between the ends (by levelling). The horizontal distance is $L\cos\theta$, or $\sqrt{L^2-h^2}$, or by the hypotenuse allowance $L-\dfrac{h^2}{2L}$ for flat slopes.
3. **Indirect methods:** tacheometry (stadia), EDM or total-station horizontal distance, and triangulation or trilateration.

**Significant figures.** Distances taken in the field must be recorded with figures that reflect the precision of the instrument. For a tape read to the nearest millimetre or centimetre, 3 to 4 decimals of a metre are *significant* (for example 127.45 m has 5 significant figures). The extra digits produced by a calculator are not significant and must be rounded back to the same decimal place as the least reliable measurement. Distances in the sum of a traverse are written to the same number of decimals, angles to the least count of the instrument.

@s 3
**Principle of chain (linear) survey.** It is a method in which the area is divided into a network of **triangles** (a *well-conditioned* triangle has no angle below $30^\circ$ and none above $120^\circ$) and only *linear* measurements are made, with a chain or tape. A triangle is rigid and is fixed by its three sides alone, so the plan can be drawn from the lengths only. Main (survey) lines run through the area and details are fixed from them by *offsets*, with check lines to control accuracy. Angles are never measured.

**Principle of plane table survey.** The field work and plotting are done at the same time on a drawing board (table) levelled over the station. Rays are drawn from the station with an alidade towards each point and the distance is scaled to plot the points. The **principle** is *parallelism*: the table is oriented at every station so that every line drawn on the sheet is parallel to the corresponding line on the ground.

**Suitability.**

| Chain survey | Plane table survey |
|---|---|
| Small, open, fairly flat areas with little detail | Medium-size areas, detail mapping, topographic work |
| Simple, cheap, no angular instruments needed | Quick; the plan is completed in the field, so missed details are seen at once |
| Unsuitable for crowded, wooded or undulating ground | Unsuitable in rain, dense forests, and where high accuracy is needed |

**Relation for bearings (angles measured to the right).** Let the bearing of the line $AB$ be $\theta_{AB}$ and let $\beta_B$ be the clockwise (right-hand) angle at $B$ between the back line $BA$ and the forward line $BC$. The back bearing of $AB$ is $\theta_{AB}\pm180^\circ$, and the bearing of $BC$ is the back bearing plus the angle to the right:

$$\begin{gathered}\theta_{BC}=\theta_{AB}\pm180^\circ+\beta_B \\ (\text{if the sum exceeds }360^\circ\text{, subtract }360^\circ)\end{gathered}$$

Take $+180^\circ$ if $\theta_{AB}<180^\circ$ and $-180^\circ$ if $\theta_{AB}>180^\circ$. For a closed traverse run clockwise, the interior angle $\alpha_B$ is the angle to the right and $\theta_{BC}=\theta_{AB}\pm180^\circ+\alpha_B$. For a traverse run anti-clockwise (as the data below turn out to be) the interior angle lies on the left and the angle to the right is the exterior angle $360^\circ-\alpha_B$, so $\theta_{BC}=\theta_{AB}+180^\circ-\alpha_B$ (mod $360^\circ$).

**The traverse.** None of the lines shows a difference of exactly $180^\circ$ between F.B. and B.B. (differences $181^\circ30'$, $178^\circ45'$, $179^\circ00'$, $181^\circ45'$), so every line cannot be accepted as free of local attraction. The interior angle at a station is found from the *back bearing of the previous line and the forward bearing of the next line*, $\alpha=\text{B.B.}_{\text{prev}}-\text{F.B.}_{\text{next}}$:

$$\begin{gathered}\angle A=107^\circ45'-33^\circ00'=74^\circ45'\\ \angle B=214^\circ30'-124^\circ30'=90^\circ00'\\ \angle C=303^\circ15'-180^\circ00'=123^\circ15'\\ \angle D=1^\circ00'+360^\circ-289^\circ30'=71^\circ30'\end{gathered}$$

Sum $=359^\circ30'$, but a closed four-sided figure needs $(2\times4-4)\times90^\circ=360^\circ$. Error $=-30'$. The correction is $+30'/4=+7.5'$ for each angle:

@s 5
**Loop levelling.** A levelling that starts at a bench mark (BM), runs through a series of change points and *returns to the same BM* (or to the starting point) is a closed loop. The algebraic sum of rises and falls should be zero, so the closing error is the difference between the computed and the known RL of the starting BM; this is distributed in proportion to the number of set-ups (or distance). It is used to check the accuracy of levelling in a limited area and to establish a bench mark near a site.

**Level circuit.** A level circuit is a network of levelling lines that form closed polygons or connect known BMs, such as: (i) a *closed circuit* (a loop starting and ending on the same BM), (ii) an *open circuit between two BMs* of known RL (a line, often a fly-levelling line), and (iii) a *net* of several interconnected loops adjusted together. Circuits are graded as first-, second-, third-order by the allowable misclosure, for example $\pm k\sqrt{K}$ (mm), where $K$ is the length in km.

**Basic principle.** The circuit must be *closed* (or tied to points of known elevation), so that the sum of the differences of level around the loop must be zero (or equal to the known difference). Without this closure no check on the accuracy of the observed differences in height would be possible. Sights should be balanced (equal back and foresight distances) to cancel the collimation and curvature errors.

@s 8
**Features of a total station.** A total station combines an electronic theodolite, an EDM (electronic distance meter) and a microprocessor with data storage in one instrument.

- Measures horizontal angle, vertical angle and slope distance together; computes horizontal distance, vertical difference, and coordinates $(N,E,Z)$ on the spot.
- Reflector-based (prism) and reflectorless (up to a few hundred metres) distance measurement; accuracy typically 2" to 5" and $\pm(2\text{ mm}+2\text{ ppm})$.
- Built-in programs: resection (free station), remote elevation (RDM), missing line measurement, area and COGO, setting-out (stakeout) of points and alignment.
- Automatic corrections for the curvature and refraction, the earth's curvature, atmospheric pressure and temperature (ppm), prism constant and the dual-axis tilt compensator.
- Internal memory or SD/USB card and Bluetooth for downloading, with direct transfer to CAD/DTM software; a large display and keyboard, coding of features, and a laser plummet.
- Robotic models can be controlled remotely by one person and track the prism.

**Why the back station and occupied station are important in digital recording.** All the coordinates the instrument stores are computed from the *occupied (instrument) station*, whose coordinates are keyed in, and the *orientation* given by the *back station* (back-sight). When the instrument is set on the occupied station $O(N_O,E_O,Z_O)$ and sighted on the back station $B$, the horizontal circle is set to the azimuth $\theta_{OB}$ computed from the known coordinates of $O$ and $B$, so that every later horizontal angle is converted to a true azimuth:

$$\begin{gathered}N=N_O+D\cos\theta \\ E=E_O+D\sin\theta \\ Z=Z_O+h_i+D\sin\alpha-h_t\end{gathered}$$

An error in the occupied station coordinates shifts the whole survey, and an error in the back-sight orientation rotates it. A *wrong or unrecorded* station would make every stored coordinate wrong, and it cannot be corrected afterwards. The instrument and target heights should be recorded together with them, and the orientation is checked on a second known point before leaving the station.

**Principle of stadia.** The stadia method is a tacheometric method of finding distances from the *optical geometry of the telescope*. The line of sight cuts a staff at two stadia hairs; the intercept $s$ of the staff between the upper and lower hairs is proportional to the horizontal distance from the instrument. With the hairs fixed at a small distance $i$ apart in a telescope of focal length $f$, similar triangles give $D=\dfrac{f}{i}s+(f+d)$, i.e. $D=ks+C$, where $k=f/i$ (usually 100) is the multiplying constant and $C$ the additive constant (zero for an anallatic telescope). For an inclined sight at vertical angle $\alpha$: $D=ks\cos^2\alpha+C\cos\alpha$ and $V=\tfrac12ks\sin2\alpha+C\sin\alpha$.

**Problem (fixed-hair vane method, two vertical angles).** The staff vanes are 1 m and 3 m above the foot of the staff, so the vane separation is $s=2$ m. The lower vane is seen at a depression of $1^\circ45'$ and the upper vane at an elevation of $2^\circ30'$, from the horizontal axis. Horizontal distance $D$ from the vertical heights of the two vanes above and below the line of the horizontal axis:

$$s=D\tan2^\circ30'+D\tan1^\circ45'\ \Rightarrow\ D=\frac{2}{0.043661+0.030556}=\frac{2}{0.074217}$$

$$\boxed{D=26.95\ \text{m}}$$

The lower vane is $D\tan1^\circ45'=26.95\times0.030556=0.823$ m below the axis. It lies 1 m above the foot of the staff, so the foot of the staff (station P) is $0.823+1=1.823$ m below the axis:

$$RL_P=1450.000-0.823-1.000=\boxed{1448.18\ \text{m}}$$

Check with the upper vane: $1450.000+26.95\tan2^\circ30'-3=1450.000+1.177-3=1448.177$ m ✓.

**Triangulation.** The area (or the line to be controlled) is covered by a chain of connected triangles or quadrilaterals. In the principle of triangulation, the length of **one side (the base line)** is measured accurately, and *all the angles* of the triangles are measured with a precise theodolite. The lengths of all the other sides then follow by the sine rule:

$$\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}\ \Rightarrow\ b=a\frac{\sin B}{\sin A}$$

and the coordinates of the stations follow from the azimuth of one side (astronomical observation). The accuracy is checked by a **check base** measured at the far end: the computed length is compared with the measured length. The sum of the angles in each triangle must be $180^\circ$ (plus the spherical excess in geodetic work), and the discrepancy (triangular error) is distributed equally among the angles. Well-conditioned triangles (all angles between $30^\circ$ and $120^\circ$, ideally near equilateral) keep the error in the computed sides low, because the sine of a small angle changes rapidly with a small angular error.

**Trilateration.** The same network of triangles is used, but *all the sides* are measured (usually by EDM or total station) and the angles are *computed* using the cosine rule:

$$\cos A=\frac{b^2+c^2-a^2}{2bc}$$

The sum of the computed angles and the closure of the figure check the work. The base and check base are not needed, but the number of redundant sides gives an adjustment (least squares). Modern practice uses a combination of both (**triangulateration**) with GNSS control.

**SW DTM versus generic plotting software.** "SW DTM" is understood as survey software with a digital terrain model (for example *SurvCADD*, *Civil 3D*, *12d*, *Leica Infinity* or *Trimble Business Center*). Compared with a generic drawing tool (a plain CAD or spreadsheet) it is better for engineering survey work because it:

- imports total-station or GNSS coordinate files directly and plots the points with their codes, with no manual typing;
- builds a **triangulated irregular network (TIN)** surface from the spot heights and breaklines and produces contours at any interval, cross-sections, longitudinal profiles and volumes (cut and fill) automatically;
- uses the survey adjustment (traverse and levelling), COGO functions, area and the setting-out data for the stakeout;
- has the symbol, layer and annotation standards of a map, and exports to CAD and GIS.

A generic plotting program can draw lines and symbols but cannot interpolate the terrain surface, and has no survey adjustment, so the work needs much manual calculation. Where only a quick sketch is needed a generic tool is enough, so SW DTM is the better choice for a *terrain model*, but not for everything.

| Manual plotting | Computerized plotting |
|---|---|
| Points are plotted by a protractor, scale and ruler; slow | Points plotted from coordinates; very fast |
| Accuracy is limited to about 0.2 mm on the sheet (graphical error) | Accuracy is that of the coordinates (mm) |
| Scale is fixed when the sheet is drawn; a new scale needs a new drawing | Any scale or sheet size, from one database |
| Contours are interpolated by hand, subjective | Contours by TIN or grid interpolation, repeatable |
| Corrections need an erase and a redraw; paper deforms with time | Easy editing, layers, digital storage and backups |
| Quantities (area, volume) by planimeter and by hand | Quantities automatically |
