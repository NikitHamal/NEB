@s 1
**Daniell cell.** A galvanic cell made of a **zinc rod dipping in $\text{ZnSO}_4$ solution** (anode) and a **copper rod dipping in $\text{CuSO}_4$ solution** (cathode), the two half-cells being joined by a salt bridge (saturated KCl or $\text{KNO}_3$ in agar) and an external wire. Its cell notation and reactions are

$$\begin{gathered}\text{Zn(s)}\,|\,\text{Zn}^{2+}(1\ \text{M})\,||\,\text{Cu}^{2+}(1\ \text{M})\,|\,\text{Cu(s)} \\ E^\circ_{cell} = 0.34-(-0.76) = 1.10\ \text{V}\end{gathered}$$
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Anode (oxidation): } \text{Zn}\to\text{Zn}^{2+}+2e^- \\ \text{Cathode (reduction): } \text{Cu}^{2+}+2e^-\to\text{Cu}\end{gathered}$$

The salt bridge completes the circuit and keeps both half-cells electrically neutral.

**Galvanic vs electrolytic cell.**

| Galvanic (voltaic) cell | Electrolytic cell |
|---|---|
| Converts chemical energy into electrical energy | Converts electrical energy into chemical energy |
| The cell reaction is spontaneous ($\Delta G<0$) | The reaction is non-spontaneous and driven by an external source ($\Delta G>0$) |
| Anode is negative, cathode is positive | Anode is positive, cathode is negative |
| Two half-cells in separate vessels joined by a salt bridge | Both electrodes dip in one electrolyte (usually one vessel) |
| Example: Daniell cell, dry cell | Example: electrolysis of molten NaCl, electroplating |

**emf of the cell.** Sn (lower $E^\circ$) is the anode, Ag$^+$ is reduced at the cathode. To balance the electrons, the silver half-reaction is doubled:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Sn}+2\text{Ag}^+\to\text{Sn}^{2+}+2\text{Ag} \\ n = 2\end{gathered}$$
$$E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{cathode}-E^\circ_{anode} = 0.80-(-0.14) = 0.94\ \text{V}$$
Nernst equation at $25^\circ$C (the concentration of Ag$^+$ is squared in the reaction quotient):
$$E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell}-\frac{0.0591}{n}\log\frac{[\text{Sn}^{2+}]}{[\text{Ag}^+]^2} = 0.94-0.02955\log\frac{0.15}{(0.03)^2}$$
$$\begin{gathered}\frac{0.15}{9\times10^{-4}} = 166.7 \\ \log 166.7 = 2.222\end{gathered}$$
$$E_{cell} = 0.94-0.02955\times2.222 = 0.94-0.0657 = 0.8743$$
$$\boxed{E_{cell}\approx0.874\ \text{V}}$$

@s 2
**Basic buffer.** A basic buffer is a solution of a **weak base and its salt with a strong acid**, for example $\text{NH}_4\text{OH}+\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$. It resists a change in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added.

**Mechanism** (ammonium hydroxide-ammonium chloride). The salt is fully ionised and the weak base only slightly, so the solution holds a large reserve of $\text{NH}_4^+$ and $\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}\to\text{NH}_4^++\text{Cl}^-\ (\text{complete}) \\ \text{NH}_4\text{OH}\rightleftharpoons\text{NH}_4^++\text{OH}^-\ (\text{slight})\end{gathered}$$

- *On adding a little acid ($\text{H}^+$):* the $\text{OH}^-$ of the base removes it, $\text{OH}^-+\text{H}^+\to\text{H}_2\text{O}$, and more $\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$ ionises to replace the $\text{OH}^-$. The ratio $[\text{NH}_4^+]/[\text{NH}_4\text{OH}]$ hardly changes, so pOH (and pH) stays almost constant.
- *On adding a little base ($\text{OH}^-$):* the $\text{NH}_4^+$ of the salt removes it, $\text{NH}_4^++\text{OH}^-\to\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$, so the free $[\text{OH}^-]$ stays nearly constant.

$$\text{pOH} = \text{p}K_b+\log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{base}]}$$

Other examples: $\text{Mg(OH)}_2+\text{MgCl}_2$, and any weak base with its chloride. (Acidic buffer for comparison: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}+\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$, $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a+\log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]}$.)

**Calculation.** The required buffer is of the acetic acid-acetate type (pH $5.8$ is acidic, near $\text{p}K_a$), so the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation is used:
$$\text{p}K_a = -\log(1.8\times10^{-5}) = 4.745$$
$$\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a+\log\frac{[\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}]}{[\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}]}\ \Rightarrow\ 5.8 = 4.745+\log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{0.2}$$
$$\log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{0.2} = 1.055\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{[\text{salt}]}{0.2} = 11.36\ \Rightarrow\ [\text{salt}] = 2.271\ \text{M}$$
Moles needed in $100$ ml (the added solid does not change the volume appreciably):
$$n = 2.271\times0.100 = 0.2271\ \text{mol}\ \ (\text{equal to }11.36\times0.02\ \text{mol of acid})$$
Molar mass of $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa} = 12+3+12+32+23 = 82\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$:
$$m = 0.2271\times82 = 18.6\ \text{g}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Sodium acetate required}\approx0.227\ \text{mol}\approx18.6\ \text{g}}$$

@s 3
**Catalytic poisoning.** The loss (partial or complete) of the activity of a catalyst caused by a trace of a foreign substance, the **poison**, which is strongly adsorbed on the active sites of the catalyst surface. Examples: $\text{As}_2\text{O}_3$ poisons the Pt catalyst of the contact process; $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ and CO poison the Fe catalyst of the Haber process; CO and sulphur compounds poison the Pt catalyst of hydrogenation and of car exhaust converters; lead (from leaded petrol) poisons the Pt-Rh converter.

**Promoters.** Substances which are **not catalysts themselves but increase the activity of the catalyst** when added in small quantities. Examples: in the Haber process $\text{N}_2+3\text{H}_2\rightleftharpoons2\text{NH}_3$, Fe is the catalyst and Mo (or $\text{K}_2\text{O}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) is the promoter; in the water-gas shift reaction $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ is promoted by $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$; in the Fischer-Tropsch process Co is promoted by thoria.

**Intermediate compound formation theory.** According to this theory, the catalyst first **combines with one of the reactants to form an unstable intermediate compound**; this intermediate has a lower-energy path, so it reacts with the second reactant to give the products and **regenerates the catalyst**. Thus the reaction $A + B\to AB$ (slow, high $E_a$) is replaced by
$$\begin{gathered}A + C\to AC\ (\text{intermediate}) \\ AC + B\to AB + C\end{gathered}$$
each step having a lower activation energy than the uncatalysed reaction.

**Examples.**

1. *Contact process (old lead-chamber / nitric oxide process):* $2\text{NO}+\text{O}_2\to2\text{NO}_2$ (intermediate); $\text{NO}_2+\text{SO}_2\to\text{SO}_3+\text{NO}$ (NO regenerated).
2. *Preparation of diethyl ether:* $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}+\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\to\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{HSO}_4+\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (intermediate ethyl hydrogen sulphate); then $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{HSO}_4+\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}\to\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OC}_2\text{H}_5+\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ (acid regenerated).
3. *Decomposition of $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ with $\text{MnO}_2$* or oxidation of $\text{SO}_2$ over $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$: $\text{SO}_2+\text{V}_2\text{O}_5\to\text{SO}_3+\text{V}_2\text{O}_4$, then $\text{V}_2\text{O}_4+\tfrac12\text{O}_2\to\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$.

Limitation: the theory cannot explain heterogeneous catalysis in which no stable intermediate can be isolated (there the adsorption theory is used).

@s 4
**Water pollution.** The contamination of water bodies (rivers, lakes, ground water, seas) by the addition of undesirable physical, chemical or biological substances so that its quality is degraded and it becomes harmful to living beings and unfit for its intended use (drinking, agriculture, industry, recreation).

**Sources.**

| Type | Source | Typical pollutants |
|---|---|---|
| Point sources | Domestic sewage, municipal drains | pathogens, organic matter, detergents |
| | Industrial effluents (tanneries, paper, dye, sugar, distillery, metal plating) | heavy metals ($\text{Hg, Pb, Cd, Cr}$), acids, alkalis, cyanide, oils, organic load |
| | Thermal power plants and nuclear plants | hot water (thermal pollution), radioactive isotopes |
| Non-point sources | Agricultural run-off | fertilisers (nitrate, phosphate), pesticides, herbicides |
| | Mining, landfill leachate, urban storm water | acid mine drainage, heavy metals, solid waste |
| | Oil spills, ships | petroleum hydrocarbons |
| Natural | Weathering, soil erosion, decay of vegetation | turbidity, arsenic, fluoride, iron |

**Effects.**

1. **Health:** cholera, typhoid, dysentery, hepatitis (pathogens); Minamata disease (Hg), itai-itai (Cd), blue-baby syndrome (nitrate), fluorosis and arsenicosis; cancer from pesticides.
2. **Eutrophication:** nitrates and phosphates cause algal bloom; on decay the dissolved oxygen (DO) falls, and fish and aquatic life die.
3. **Low DO and high BOD/COD** from organic waste kill aquatic life.
4. **Bioaccumulation and biomagnification** of heavy metals and DDT along the food chain.
5. **Thermal pollution** lowers the solubility of oxygen and disturbs breeding of aquatic life.
6. **Economic loss:** corrosion and scaling of pipes and boilers, unfit water for industry and irrigation, lost tourism and fisheries.

**Control measures.**

1. *Treat sewage and industrial wastes before discharge:* primary (screening, sedimentation), secondary (activated-sludge or trickling filter, biological oxidation), tertiary (chemical precipitation, adsorption on activated carbon, reverse osmosis, disinfection by chlorine/ozone/UV).
2. Recycle and reuse of water; cleaner production and waste minimisation in industries; zero-liquid discharge.
3. Use of organic manure, bio-fertilisers and integrated pest management instead of heavy fertiliser and pesticide use.
4. Ban of detergents containing phosphates, use of biodegradable detergents.
5. Cooling ponds / towers for heated effluent; safe storage of radioactive waste.
6. Strict laws and monitoring (water-quality standards, WHO/Nepal drinking water standard), public awareness, protection of catchments and riverbanks, sanitation (toilets, septic tanks), and proper solid-waste management.

@s 5
**a) Cause and effect of ozone layer depletion.**

*Ozone layer.* Ozone ($\text{O}_3$) in the stratosphere ($15$-$35$ km) absorbs the harmful UV-B and UV-C radiation of the sun. It is formed and destroyed continuously, and the natural balance is
$$\begin{gathered}\text{O}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu\ (<240\text{ nm})}2\text{O} \\ \text{O}+\text{O}_2\to\text{O}_3 \\ \text{O}_3\xrightarrow{h\nu}\text{O}_2+\text{O}\end{gathered}$$

*Causes of depletion.* Man-made chemicals, chiefly **chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs, freons)** from refrigerators, air-conditioners, aerosol sprays, foam and solvents; also halons (fire extinguishers), carbon tetrachloride, methyl bromide and nitrogen oxides from supersonic aircraft. In the stratosphere UV breaks CFCs into chlorine atoms, which destroy ozone in a chain reaction:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}\cdot+\text{Cl}\cdot \\ \text{Cl}\cdot+\text{O}_3\to\text{ClO}\cdot+\text{O}_2 \\ \text{ClO}\cdot+\text{O}\to\text{Cl}\cdot+\text{O}_2\end{gathered}$$
One chlorine atom can destroy about $10^5$ ozone molecules. Polar stratospheric clouds over Antarctica speed up the process, giving the **ozone hole** each spring.

*Effects.* (i) More UV-B reaches the earth, causing skin cancer, cataract, eye damage and weakening of immunity in humans. (ii) Reduced crop yield and damage to plants and phytoplankton, which affects the aquatic food chain. (iii) Damage to materials (paints, plastics, rubber fade and crack). (iv) Climate change, since CFCs are also greenhouse gases. *Control:* Montreal Protocol (1987) phased out CFCs; HCFC/HFC and hydrocarbon substitutes are used.

**b) Acid rain and its effects.**

*Acid rain.* Rain (or snow, fog, dry deposition) whose **pH is below $5.6$** (the pH of rain-water in equilibrium with atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$) because of dissolved acids of $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{NO}_x$.

*Formation.* $\text{SO}_2$ (burning of coal and oil, smelters) and $\text{NO}_x$ (vehicles, power plants, lightning) are oxidised and dissolved in cloud water:
$$\begin{gathered}2\text{SO}_2+\text{O}_2\to2\text{SO}_3,\qquad \text{SO}_3+\text{H}_2\text{O}\to\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \\ 4\text{NO}_2+2\text{H}_2\text{O}+\text{O}_2\to4\text{HNO}_3\end{gathered}$$

*Effects.* (i) Acidifies lakes and streams (pH $<5$), killing fish and aquatic organisms. (ii) Damages forests and crops: leaches nutrients ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$) from the soil and releases toxic $\text{Al}^{3+}$. (iii) Corrodes metals and attacks marble and limestone buildings and statues (Taj Mahal, stone monuments):
$$\text{CaCO}_3+\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\to\text{CaSO}_4+\text{H}_2\text{O}+\text{CO}_2$$
(iv) Health: respiratory problems and dissolved metals in drinking water. *Control:* flue-gas desulphurisation, low-sulphur fuel, catalytic converters, use of renewable energy, liming of lakes.

@s 6
**Characteristic properties of inorganic polymers.** Inorganic polymers have a backbone built of **elements other than carbon** (Si, P, N, S, B, etc.) and they

1. have **high thermal stability** and resist high temperatures far better than organic polymers (the Si-O, P-N bonds are strong);
2. are **chemically inert** and resistant to oxidation, acids, bases and weathering;
3. have a high degree of **flexibility of the backbone** (low glass-transition temperature in silicones, phosphazenes) and are elastomeric;
4. are often **non-flammable** and good electrical insulators (some, such as $(\text{SN})_x$, are conducting);
5. have high density and hardness (ceramic-like, e.g. glass, silicates), but are often poor in tensile strength and may be brittle;
6. have good water-repellent and low-temperature properties (silicones), and many are hydrolysed only slowly.

**Preparation of polyphosphonitrilic chloride** (polyphosphazene, $[\text{NPCl}_2]_n$, "inorganic rubber"). Phosphorus pentachloride reacts with ammonium chloride in tetrachloroethane (or chlorobenzene) at about $120$-$150^\circ$C to give the cyclic trimer (hexachlorocyclotriphosphazene), together with some tetramer:
$$n\,\text{PCl}_5+n\,\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}\xrightarrow{120-150^\circ\text{C}}(\text{NPCl}_2)_n+4n\,\text{HCl}$$
When the purified trimer $(\text{NPCl}_2)_3$ is **heated at $250^\circ$C** in a sealed tube (ring-opening polymerisation) it changes into the long-chain elastomer, polyphosphonitrilic chloride:
$$(\text{NPCl}_2)_3\xrightarrow{250^\circ\text{C}}\left[\!-\text{N}{=}\text{P(Cl}_2)-\right]_n$$
Replacing Cl by OR, OAr or NHR groups (nucleophilic substitution) gives stable, water-resistant polyphosphazenes.

**Uses of silicones.** Silicones, $[\text{R}_2\text{SiO}]_n$ are made by hydrolysis and condensation of $\text{R}_2\text{SiCl}_2$. Uses: (i) silicone oils as lubricants, hydraulic fluids, and in diffusion pumps (stable from $-50$ to $250^\circ$C); (ii) silicone greases and sealants for glass, bathroom and building joints; (iii) silicone rubber for gaskets, insulation of wires and cables, medical implants, tubing; (iv) water-repellent coating of fabrics, paper and masonry; (v) antifoam agents, polishes, cosmetics; (vi) electrical insulating varnishes and resins.

@s 7
**Conducting polymer.** An organic polymer with a **conjugated backbone (alternating single and double bonds)** that conducts electricity, usually after *doping* (oxidation or reduction, e.g. with $\text{I}_2$, $\text{AsF}_5$, Na). Examples: polyacetylene, polyaniline, polypyrrole, polythiophene. Conduction arises from the delocalised $\pi$-electrons and the charge carriers (polarons, bipolarons) created by doping; conductivity can rise to $10^{3}$-$10^{5}\ \text{S m}^{-1}$. They are used in rechargeable batteries, LEDs, sensors, anti-static coatings and electromagnetic shielding.

**Teflon (polytetrafluoroethylene, PTFE).**

*Preparation.* Free-radical addition polymerisation of tetrafluoroethylene (TFE), initiated by ammonium persulphate (or $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) in water at about $70^\circ$C and $70$ atm pressure:
$$n\,\text{CF}_2{=}\text{CF}_2\xrightarrow[\text{(NH}_4)_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8,\ 70\text{ atm}]{}\left[\!-\text{CF}_2-\text{CF}_2-\right]_n$$
(The monomer is made from chloroform: $\text{CHCl}_3\xrightarrow{\text{HF, SbF}_3}\text{CHClF}_2\xrightarrow{600-800^\circ\text{C}}\text{CF}_2{=}\text{CF}_2$.)

*Uses.* Non-stick coating of cooking utensils; gaskets, seals, packings, valve linings and bearings in chemical plants (inert to almost all chemicals); electrical insulation (cables, insulators) because of its high dielectric strength; self-lubricating bearings, pipes and tape (plumber's tape); surgical implants and vascular grafts.

**Nylon 6,6.**

*Preparation.* Condensation polymerisation of **adipic acid** ($\text{HOOC(CH}_2)_4\text{COOH}$) with **hexamethylenediamine** ($\text{H}_2\text{N(CH}_2)_6\text{NH}_2$). The two react first to form nylon salt, which is heated at about $250^\circ$C under $15$-$20$ atm in an inert atmosphere, eliminating water:
$$n\,\text{HOOC(CH}_2)_4\text{COOH}+n\,\text{H}_2\text{N(CH}_2)_6\text{NH}_2\xrightarrow{250^\circ\text{C}}\left[\!-\text{CO(CH}_2)_4\text{CO–NH(CH}_2)_6\text{NH}-\right]_n+2n\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The molten polymer is spun into fibres and cold-drawn to align the chains (strong hydrogen bonding between the amide groups).

*Uses.* Textile fibres (clothes, socks, stockings), ropes, fishing nets, tyre cords, parachutes, carpets, brushes (bristles), and moulded engineering parts such as gears, bearings and bushes.

@s 8
**d-block elements.** The elements in which the **last electron enters the $(n-1)d$ orbital**; they occupy groups $3$-$12$ of the periodic table (the $3d$ series Sc-Zn, $4d$ series Y-Cd, $5d$ series La-Hg, $6d$ series Ac-Cn). General electronic configuration: $(n-1)d^{1-10}\,ns^{1-2}$. The **transition elements** are those that have an *incomplete $d$ subshell in the atom or in one of its common ions*.

**Zinc is not a true transition element.** Zn has the configuration $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^{10}4s^2$, and its only common ion $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ is $3d^{10}$. Both the atom and the ion have a **completely filled $d$ subshell**, so it lacks the typical properties of transition elements: it shows a single oxidation state ($+2$), forms colourless diamagnetic compounds, has no catalytic activity worth mentioning, forms fewer complexes, and has a low melting point ($420^\circ$C) and low enthalpy of atomisation. (Cd and Hg behave similarly.)

**Variable oxidation states.** In transition elements the $ns$ and $(n-1)d$ electrons have **nearly the same energy** (the energy gap is small), so after losing the $ns$ electrons, one or more $(n-1)d$ electrons can also take part in bonding. For example Mn ($3d^54s^2$) shows $+2$ to $+7$ ($\text{Mn}^{2+},\text{MnO}_2,\text{MnO}_4^{2-},\text{MnO}_4^-$), Fe shows $+2,+3$, Cr shows $+2,+3,+6$. The oxidation states differ by one unit ($\text{Fe}^{2+},\text{Fe}^{3+}$; $\text{Cu}^+,\text{Cu}^{2+}$), unlike the $p$-block where they differ by two. The higher states are stabilised with the electronegative elements O and F ($\text{MnO}_4^-$, $\text{CrO}_4^{2-}$).

**Formation of stable complexes.** Transition metal ions form a large number of stable complexes because they have (i) **small size and high charge density** (strong attraction to ligands), (ii) **vacant, low-energy $(n-1)d$, $ns$ and $np$ orbitals** which accept lone pairs from the ligands to form coordinate bonds, and (iii) variable oxidation states with high charge. Examples: $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-}$, $[\text{Cu(NH}_3)_4]^{2+}$, $[\text{Ni(CO)}_4]$.

@s 9
**a) $\text{CuSO}_4$ is coloured but $\text{ZnSO}_4$ is colourless.**

- In $\text{CuSO}_4$ the copper is $\text{Cu}^{2+}$: $3d^9$, with **one unpaired electron** and a vacant place in the $d$ subshell. In water (or the crystal) the ion is surrounded by ligands, e.g. $[\text{Cu(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}$, and the five $d$ orbitals split into $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ sets of different energy. The ion absorbs a photon of **visible light (the orange-red region, about $600$-$800$ nm)** to excite an electron from $t_{2g}$ to the half-empty $e_g$ level ($d$-$d$ transition). The complementary colour that is transmitted is **blue**.
- In $\text{ZnSO}_4$ the ion is $\text{Zn}^{2+}$: $3d^{10}$. All $d$ orbitals are **completely filled**, so there is **no vacant $d$ orbital** for an electron to jump to and no $d$-$d$ transition is possible. Visible light is not absorbed, so the compound is **colourless** (and diamagnetic).
$$\Delta E = h\nu = \frac{hc}{\lambda}\quad(\text{energy of the absorbed light} = \text{crystal-field splitting})$$

**b) Transition elements form complexes very well.**

1. Their ions are **small and highly charged**, so they have high charge density and attract the ligands strongly.
2. They have **vacant $(n-1)d$ orbitals of low energy** (together with $ns$ and $np$) which can accept the lone pairs donated by the ligands and so form coordinate (dative) covalent bonds.
3. They can show **variable oxidation states** and thus give ions with a suitable charge to bind the ligands.
4. **Extra stability** is gained from crystal-field stabilisation energy (CFSE), which is why e.g. $\text{Fe}^{2+}$, $\text{Co}^{3+}$, $\text{Ni}^{2+}$, $\text{Cu}^{2+}$, $\text{Pt}^{4+}$ give many stable complexes.

By contrast, the $s$-block ions (e.g. $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Ca}^{2+}$) are large, have low charge and no $d$ orbitals, so they form few complexes (some only with chelating agents like EDTA).

**Double salts vs complex salts.**

| Double salt | Complex salt (coordination compound) |
|---|---|
| Formed by the combination of two simple salts in stoichiometric ratio, stable only in the solid state | Formed by a metal ion and ligands, stable in the solid state **and in solution** |
| Dissociates completely into **all of its simple ions** in water | Gives a **complex ion that does not dissociate** further into the original metal ion and ligands (only slightly) |
| Loses its identity in solution: its tests are those of the constituent ions | Keeps its identity; the metal ion is not detected by ordinary tests |
| Example: Mohr's salt $\text{FeSO}_4\cdot(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4\cdot6\text{H}_2\text{O}$, potash alum $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4\cdot\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3\cdot24\text{H}_2\text{O}$, carnallite | Example: $\text{K}_4[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]$ (no $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ or $\text{CN}^-$ tests), $[\text{Cu(NH}_3)_4]\text{SO}_4$ |

**Postulates of Werner's theory.**

1. A metal in a complex shows **two types of valence**: the **primary (ionisable) valence**, equal to the oxidation state of the metal, satisfied by negative ions and shown by dotted lines; and the **secondary (non-ionisable) valence**, equal to the **coordination number**, satisfied by ligands (neutral molecules or anions) and shown by thick lines.
2. Every metal tends to satisfy both its primary and secondary valences. A negative ion can satisfy both (e.g. $\text{Cl}^-$ in $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_5\text{Cl}]\text{Cl}_2$).
3. The **secondary valences are directed in space** towards fixed positions around the metal, so the complex has a definite geometry (coordination number $6$: octahedral; $4$: tetrahedral or square planar). This explains the isomerism.
4. The ions satisfying the primary valence are outside the coordination sphere (precipitated by $\text{Ag}^+$ if they are $\text{Cl}^-$), and the ligands of the secondary valence lie inside the sphere (written in square brackets).

*Illustration:* $\text{CoCl}_3\cdot6\text{NH}_3$ gives $3$ AgCl, $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]\text{Cl}_3$; $\text{CoCl}_3\cdot5\text{NH}_3$ gives $2$ AgCl, $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_5\text{Cl}]\text{Cl}_2$.

**Valence bond theory (VBT).** The ligand donates a lone pair into a vacant hybrid orbital of the metal ion; the geometry is fixed by the hybridisation, and the magnetism by the number of unpaired electrons, $\mu=\sqrt{n(n+2)}$ BM.

**(i) $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-}$.** Oxidation state of Fe: $x-6=-4$, so $\text{Fe}^{2+}$, configuration $3d^6$. $\text{CN}^-$ is a **strong-field ligand**, so it forces the pairing of the $3d$ electrons (all six $d$ electrons occupy three $3d$ orbitals in pairs) and frees **two $3d$ orbitals**. Six $\text{F}^-$ donate pairs (**outer-orbital complex**).

@s 12
**Characteristics of a good (important) explosive.**

1. It must be **stable** to heat, shock, friction and storage, so that it can be handled and transported safely, and it must not deteriorate on storage.
2. It must be **sensitive to a suitable detonator** (primary explosive) and explode only when needed.
3. The decomposition must be **highly exothermic**, giving a large amount of heat (high temperature).
4. It must produce a **large volume of gaseous products** ($\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CO}$, $\text{N}_2$, steam) per unit mass, so that a high pressure builds up.
5. The reaction must be **very fast (high velocity of detonation, $\sim$ $6$-$9$ km s$^{-1}$)**.
6. It should be **cheap, easily and safely manufactured** from available raw materials, and should be non-hygroscopic and not poisonous.
7. It should have a high density, a suitable power (brisance) and low freezing point.

**TNT (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene).**

*Preparation.* Toluene is nitrated in three steps using a mixture of concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$ and concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ with increasing temperature and acid strength (mono- at $30^\circ$C, di- at $60^\circ$C, tri- at $100$-$115^\circ$C):
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_3\xrightarrow[\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4]{3\text{HNO}_3}\text{CH}_3\text{C}_6\text{H}_2(\text{NO}_2)_3+3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The crude product is washed with water and with sodium sulphite solution (to remove unsymmetrical isomers) and crystallised from alcohol.

*Uses.* A military high explosive (shells, bombs, torpedoes, grenades), civil blasting in mining and quarrying (often mixed with ammonium nitrate as *amatol*), tunnelling and demolition. Its decomposition: $2\text{C}_7\text{H}_5\text{N}_3\text{O}_6\to12\text{CO}+5\text{H}_2+3\text{N}_2+2\text{C}$.

**Gun cotton (cellulose hexanitrate; guncotton).**

*Preparation.* Pure cellulose (cotton wool) is treated with a nitrating mixture of concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$ and concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($1:3$) at $\le30^\circ$C for about $24$ h, then washed with water to remove the acid and dried:
$$[\text{C}_6\text{H}_7\text{O}_2(\text{OH})_3]_n+3n\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4}[\text{C}_6\text{H}_7\text{O}_2(\text{ONO}_2)_3]_n+3n\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
It contains about $13$-$13.4\%$ nitrogen.

*Uses.* Main raw material of **smokeless powder** (with nitroglycerine it gives cordite and ballistite); propellant for guns and rockets; blasting explosive in mines and torpedoes (when wet it is safe to handle); making of collodion and celluloid.

@s 13
**Lubricants.** Substances that are applied between two moving or sliding surfaces to **reduce friction and wear** between them. They may be liquid (mineral oils, vegetable and animal oils, synthetic oils), semi-solid (greases) or solid (graphite, $\text{MoS}_2$).

**Functions of lubricants.**

1. Reduce friction and wear and tear of moving parts, which increases machine life.
2. Reduce the loss of energy as heat, so the efficiency of the machine improves.
3. Act as a **coolant**, carrying away the heat produced.
4. Act as a **seal** (e.g. in piston rings, cylinders), prevent leakage and keep out dust and moisture.
5. Reduce corrosion and the rate of surface damage, flush out dirt and debris, and reduce noise and vibration.

**When are grease and solid lubricants used?**

- **Grease (semi-solid lubricant: oil thickened by soap):** used where a liquid oil cannot be retained, i.e. in bearings and parts that **work at low speed and high load**, in **intermittent operation**, where frequent lubrication is not possible or the **machine parts are inaccessible** (gears, chassis, rolling bearings), where there is leakage or dripping to avoid (food, textile machines), and where the surfaces must be protected from dust and moisture.
- **Solid lubricants (graphite, $\text{MoS}_2$, talc, mica, soapstone):** used at **very high temperature and heavy load** where oil would decompose or be squeezed out, in **vacuum**, in the presence of **corrosive chemicals**, in clean environments where oil must be avoided (food, textile, paper machines), and in the running-in of new engines.

**Requisites of a good paint.**

1. It should be easily applied and **spread uniformly** (suitable viscosity), and should give a thin, smooth, uniform film.
2. It should **dry quickly** with a hard, tough film, with good **adhesion** to the surface and good covering power (opacity).
3. It should be **weather- and water-resistant**, not crack, peel or blister, and resist chemicals and corrosion.
4. It should have a **bright, lasting colour** that does not fade, and a pleasing appearance (gloss).
5. It should be **non-toxic**, inexpensive, and give a **good protection** to the surface (anti-corrosive pigment, e.g. red lead).
6. It should stay stable in storage without settling or skinning.

**Geometrical (cis-trans) isomerism.** A type of **stereoisomerism** in which compounds have the same structural formula but differ in the **relative arrangement of atoms or groups in space** about a double bond or a ring, because rotation about it is restricted. The isomer with the similar groups on the same side is **cis** (or *Z*), on opposite sides is **trans** (or *E*). Examples: cis- and trans-but-2-ene, maleic acid (cis) and fumaric acid (trans), cis- and trans-$[\text{Pt(NH}_3)_2\text{Cl}_2]$.

**Conditions for geometrical isomerism.**

1. There must be **restricted rotation** about a bond: a C=C (or C=N, N=N) double bond, a ring, or a rigid metal complex.
2. **Each of the doubly bonded atoms must carry two different groups** ($a\ne b$ on one carbon and $c\ne d$ on the other). If either carbon has two identical groups (e.g. $\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHCl}$, $\text{R}_2\text{C}{=}\text{CHR}'$), no geometrical isomers exist.

**Stereoisomers of 2-bromo-3-chloropentane**, $\text{CH}_3-\text{C}^*\text{HBr}-\text{C}^*\text{HCl}-\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_3$.

It has **two chiral (asymmetric) carbons**, C-2 (H, Br, $\text{CH}_3$, and the C-3 residue) and C-3 (H, Cl, $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5$, and the C-2 residue). The two centres are **not identical**, so there is no internal mirror plane and no meso form: the number of stereoisomers is $2^n = 2^2 = 4$, which form **two pairs of enantiomers**:

| Isomer | C-2 | C-3 | Relation |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | R | R | enantiomer of II |
| II | S | S | enantiomer of I |
| III | R | S | enantiomer of IV |
| IV | S | R | enantiomer of III |

Each of (I, II) is a **diastereomer** of each of (III, IV) (they have different physical properties, different melting and boiling points, and are not mirror images).

**Hydrolysis of methyl bromide with aqueous NaOH: S$_\text{N}$2 mechanism.** $\text{CH}_3\text{Br}$ is a **primary (methyl) halide** with practically no steric hindrance and cannot form a stable carbocation, so the substitution goes by a **bimolecular, one-step (concerted) mechanism**:
$$\text{CH}_3\text{Br}+\text{OH}^-\to\text{CH}_3\text{OH}+\text{Br}^-$$
$$\text{rate} = k\,[\text{CH}_3\text{Br}][\text{OH}^-]\quad(\text{second order})$$

*Mechanism.* The nucleophile $\text{OH}^-$ attacks the electron-poor carbon **from the side opposite to the leaving group Br** (back-side attack, $180^\circ$). In the **transition state** the carbon is five-coordinate (trigonal bipyramid): the C-O bond is forming while the C-Br bond is breaking, and the three H atoms lie in a plane perpendicular to the O-C-Br axis. Br$^-$ leaves, and the three hydrogens flip over like an umbrella in a wind (**Walden inversion** of configuration, not visible for $\text{CH}_3$ itself).

$$\text{HO}^-+\text{H}_3\text{C–Br}\to\left[\text{HO}^{\delta-}\cdots\text{CH}_3\cdots\text{Br}^{\delta-}\right]^\ddagger\to\text{HO–CH}_3+\text{Br}^-$$

**Elimination reaction.** A reaction in which **two atoms or groups are removed from adjacent carbon atoms of a substrate** (without being replaced) to form a **multiple bond** (a double bond). Dehydrohalogenation of an alkyl halide, loss of $\text{H}$ and $\text{X}$ using a base such as alcoholic KOH, is the commonest example:
$$\text{R–CH}_2\text{–CH}_2\text{–X}+\text{KOH(alc.)}\to\text{R–CH}{=}\text{CH}_2+\text{KX}+\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**Dehydrohalogenation of tert-butyl bromide with caustic potash (alcoholic KOH).** The tertiary halide $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}$ has nine equivalent $\beta$-hydrogens, and the strong base removes one of them. The product is **isobutene (2-methylpropene)**:
$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–Br}+\text{KOH}\xrightarrow{\text{alcohol},\ \Delta}(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C}{=}\text{CH}_2+\text{KBr}+\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

$$\text{rate} = k[(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}][\text{OH}^-]\quad(\text{second order})$$
