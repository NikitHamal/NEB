@s 1
**Bar pendulum.** A bar pendulum is a **compound (physical) pendulum** made of a long uniform metal bar with holes drilled at equal intervals along its length. It can be suspended from any hole and made to oscillate in a vertical plane about a horizontal axis through that hole.

**Time period.** Let the bar of mass $m$ be suspended at a point $O$ at distance $l$ from the centre of gravity $G$, and let $k$ be the radius of gyration about the parallel axis through $G$. For a small angular displacement $\theta$ the restoring torque is $mgl\theta$, and the moment of inertia about $O$ is $I = m(k^2 + l^2)$. So
$$I\ddot\theta = -mgl\,\theta\ \Rightarrow\ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{k^2+l^2}{gl}}$$
The length of the equivalent simple pendulum is $L = \dfrac{k^2+l^2}{l}$.

**Four points of equal period.** For a given $T$, $l$ satisfies
$$l^2 - \frac{gT^2}{4\pi^2}\,l + k^2 = 0$$
This is a quadratic in $l$ with two roots $l_1$ and $l_2$:
$$\begin{gathered}l_1 + l_2 = \frac{gT^2}{4\pi^2} = L \\ l_1l_2 = k^2\end{gathered}$$
So for one period $T$ there are **two distances $l_1$ and $l_2$ from $G$** on one side of $G$ (with $l_1 + l_2 = L$ and $l_1l_2 = k^2$, i.e. $l_1 > k > l_2$). The same two distances measured on the **opposite side** of $G$ also satisfy the equation since $T$ depends only on $l^2$ and $l$. Hence there are **four points, $A$, $A'$, $B$, $B'$, collinear with $G$, two on each side at distances $l_1$ and $l_2$ from $G$, about which the bar has the same time period** $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$.

**OR: average energies in SHM.** Let $x = a\sin(\omega t + \phi)$, $v = a\omega\cos(\omega t+\phi)$ and $k = m\omega^2$.

$$\begin{gathered}\text{KE} = \tfrac12mv^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2a^2\cos^2(\omega t+\phi) \\ \text{PE} = \tfrac12kx^2 = \tfrac12m\omega^2a^2\sin^2(\omega t+\phi)\end{gathered}$$
Averaging over one period $T$, $\langle\cos^2\rangle = \langle\sin^2\rangle = \dfrac1T\displaystyle\int_0^T\dfrac{1\pm\cos2(\omega t+\phi)}{2}dt = \dfrac12$. Hence
$$\begin{gathered}\langle\text{KE}\rangle = \tfrac14m\omega^2a^2 \\ \langle\text{PE}\rangle = \tfrac14m\omega^2a^2\end{gathered}$$
$$\boxed{\langle\text{KE}\rangle = \langle\text{PE}\rangle = \tfrac14m\omega^2a^2 = \tfrac12E}$$
where $E = \tfrac12m\omega^2a^2$ is the total (constant) energy.

@s 3
**Acoustics of a hall.** A hall has good acoustics if every listener hears the sound clearly, with adequate loudness and without distortion. The common **defects** are:

1. **Excessive reverberation.** Sound persists after the source stops because of repeated reflections. If the reverberation time is too long, successive syllables overlap and speech becomes unintelligible. If it is too short the hall sounds dead. Sabine's formula gives
$$T = \frac{0.161\,V}{\sum\alpha_iS_i}$$
where $V$ is the volume (m³), $S_i$ the area of surface $i$ and $\alpha_i$ its absorption coefficient. The optimum value is about $0.5$ s for small rooms and $1$–$1.5$ s for concert halls.
2. **Echoes.** A reflected sound reaching the listener more than about $0.1$ s after the direct sound (path difference greater than about $17$ m) is heard as a separate echo, typically from a distant high wall or dome.
3. **Focusing and dead spots.** Concave walls and domes focus sound at some places (too loud) and leave other places with almost none (dead spots).
4. **Insufficient or non-uniform loudness.** The intensity is too low at the far end of a large hall or the sound is not evenly distributed.
5. **Resonance.** Some of the hall's components (panels, windows, enclosed air) vibrate at certain frequencies of the sound and boom or drown the remaining frequencies.
6. **Echelon effect and external or internal noise.** Repeated reflections from regularly spaced surfaces (steps, railings) produce a musical note; noise from outside, ventilation or the audience masks speech.

**Remedies.**

- Choose the volume and shape properly (avoid concave domes and parallel bare walls; use a splayed ceiling and side walls) so that the sound is distributed uniformly and the reverberation time computed from Sabine's formula is the optimum value.
- Increase absorption to reduce reverberation: heavy curtains, carpets, upholstered seats, acoustic tiles, perforated boards and felt panels on walls and ceiling. A packed audience also absorbs strongly (each person about $0.4$ m² of open window).
- Cover the wall facing the speaker with absorbing material to avoid echoes; use a reflecting sounding board behind and above the speaker, curved to throw sound toward the audience.
- Use rough, irregular, or fluted surfaces (diffusers) to scatter sound and remove focusing.
- Use public-address loudspeakers placed in the right positions to give uniform loudness.
- Isolate the hall from outside noise by double walls, double doors and windows, rubber-padded doors and floors; carpets or rubber covering on the floor reduce footstep noise. Remove loose panels that resonate.

**Formation of Newton's rings.** A plano-convex lens of large radius of curvature $R$ is placed with its convex surface on a flat glass plate, so a thin air film of varying thickness $t$ is formed, with $t = 0$ at the point of contact and $t$ increasing symmetrically outwards. Monochromatic light is allowed to fall nearly normally (through a $45^\circ$ glass plate) on the lens. Rays reflected from the lower surface of the lens and from the upper surface of the glass plate interfere. The locus of constant film thickness is a circle, so a system of **alternating dark and bright concentric rings** is seen, with a **dark spot at the centre**.

The ray reflected at the glass plate (rare to dense) suffers a phase change of $\pi$, so the effective path difference for normal incidence is $2\mu t + \dfrac\lambda2$ with $\mu = 1$ for air:

- Bright ring: $2t + \dfrac\lambda2 = n\lambda\ \Rightarrow\ 2t = \left(n - \tfrac12\right)\lambda$
- **Dark ring:** $2t + \dfrac\lambda2 = \left(n+\tfrac12\right)\lambda\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{2t = n\lambda}$, $n = 0,1,2,\dots$ ($n = 0$ gives the central dark spot).

**Diameters of the dark rings.** Let a dark ring of radius $r_n$ correspond to a film thickness $t$. From the geometry of the circle (lens surface), $r_n^2 = R^2 - (R-t)^2 = 2Rt - t^2\approx2Rt$ (since $t\ll R$), so $t = \dfrac{r_n^2}{2R} = \dfrac{D_n^2}{8R}$ where $D_n$ is the diameter. The dark ring condition $2t = n\lambda$ gives
$$\begin{gathered}\frac{D_n^2}{4R} = n\lambda\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{D_n^2 = 4n\lambda R} \\ D_n = 2\sqrt{\lambda R}\,\sqrt n\end{gathered}$$
$$\boxed{D_n\propto\sqrt n}$$
So the diameters of the dark rings are proportional to the square roots of natural numbers $1, 2, 3, \dots$ (so $D_1 : D_2 : D_3 = 1 : \sqrt2 : \sqrt3$), and the rings get closer together outwards. $\blacksquare$ The same proof gives $D_n\propto\sqrt{2n-1}$ for the bright rings.

**OR: second maximum of the single slit.** For a slit of width $a$ the amplitude at angle $\theta$ is $R = R_0\dfrac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}$ with $\alpha = \dfrac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}$, so
$$I = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}\alpha\right)^2$$
where $I_0$ is the central intensity ($\alpha\to0$).

**Positions of the secondary maxima.** $\dfrac{dI}{d\alpha} = 0$ gives $\dfrac{2\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\cdot\dfrac{\alpha\cos\alpha - \sin\alpha}{\alpha^2} = 0$, so either $\sin\alpha = 0$ (minima, $\alpha = n\pi$) or $\tan\alpha = \alpha$ (maxima). The roots of $\tan\alpha = \alpha$ are close to $\alpha\approx\pm\left(n+\tfrac12\right)\pi$ with $n = 1,2,\dots$, namely $\alpha_1\approx\tfrac{3\pi}2$ (first secondary), $\alpha_2\approx\tfrac{5\pi}2$ (second secondary), etc.

At the **second-order maximum** $\alpha\approx\dfrac{5\pi}2$, $\sin\alpha = \sin\dfrac{5\pi}2 = 1$:
$$\frac I{I_0} = \left(\frac{\sin(5\pi/2)}{5\pi/2}\right)^2 = \left(\frac2{5\pi}\right)^2\approx0.0162$$
$$\boxed{I_2 = \left(\frac2{5\pi}\right)^2I_0}$$
(The exact root $\alpha = 7.725$ gives $0.0165$, so the approximation is excellent.) $\blacksquare$

@s 6
A **quarter-wave plate** converts plane polarized light (vibration at $45^\circ$ to the optic axis) into circularly polarized light. The phase difference between the e- and o-rays must be an odd multiple of $\pi/2$, i.e. the path difference is $(2m+1)\dfrac\lambda4$:
$$\begin{gathered}(\mu_o - \mu_e)\,t = \frac\lambda4 \\ \text{(minimum thickness)}\end{gathered}$$
For calcite (negative crystal) $\mu_o = 1.658$, $\mu_e = 1.486$ (at about $589$ nm; the small variation at $580$ nm is neglected), $\mu_o - \mu_e = 0.172$:
$$t = \frac{\lambda}{4(\mu_o-\mu_e)} = \frac{580\times10^{-9}}{4(0.172)} = 8.43\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}$$
$$\boxed{t\approx0.84\ \mu\text{m}\ \text{(or odd multiples: }t = (2m+1)\times0.84\ \mu\text{m)}}$$
The emerging light is circularly polarized only if the incident plane-polarized light has its vibration at $45^\circ$ to the optic axis (equal amplitudes of e- and o-components).

@s 7
**Step-index fibre.** The core (refractive index $n_1$) is surrounded by a cladding of lower index $n_2$ with an abrupt change at the interface. A ray entering within the acceptance angle strikes the interface at an angle greater than the critical angle $\theta_c = \sin^{-1}(n_2/n_1)$ and is **totally internally reflected**, travelling in a zig-zag path. Different rays (modes) travel different path lengths, so a pulse spreads (intermodal dispersion).

**Graded-index fibre.** The refractive index of the core decreases **gradually and parabolically** from the axis, $n(r) = n_1\sqrt{1 - 2\Delta(r/a)^2}$, to $n_2$ at the cladding. A ray bends continuously toward the axis (a refraction curving each ray back as it moves into a region of lower index) and follows a **smooth sinusoidal path** around the axis.

**Importance of self-focusing (graded-index fibre).** In a graded-index fibre the rays travelling along longer, oblique paths spend more time in the lower-index outer region where the speed $c/n$ is higher, so they travel faster and catch up with the axial ray: all rays arrive at nearly the same time and are repeatedly refocused toward the axis. Thus:

- **Intermodal dispersion is greatly reduced**, so pulses spread much less and **a much higher bandwidth (bit rate) over longer distances** is possible compared with step-index fibre.
- The light is kept away from the core-cladding boundary, so losses due to scattering and bending at the interface are small.
- The self-focusing action makes the fibre a continuous distributed lens, so coupling from sources (LEDs and laser diodes) is efficient with a larger acceptance for light.
- Graded-index multimode fibre is therefore widely used for LANs and short-haul telecommunication links.

@s 8
Two thin lenses, $f_1 = f_2 = f = 8$ cm, separated by $d = 4$ cm.

**Equivalent focal length.**
$$\frac1F = \frac1{f_1} + \frac1{f_2} - \frac d{f_1f_2} = \frac18 + \frac18 - \frac4{64} = \frac{12}{64} = \frac3{16}$$
$$\boxed{F = \frac{16}3 = 5.33\ \text{cm}}$$

**Object distance for an image at infinity.** Final image at infinity means the rays leaving lens 2 are parallel, so the intermediate image $I_1$ formed by lens 1 must lie at the **first focal point of lens 2**, i.e. $8$ cm in front of lens 2. Lens 2 is $4$ cm beyond lens 1, so $I_1$ is $8 - 4 = 4$ cm **in front of lens 1** (on the object side), a virtual image: $v_1 = -4$ cm.

For lens 1 (real-is-positive convention $\dfrac1v - \dfrac1u = \dfrac1f$ with $u$ negative for a real object):
$$\frac1u = \frac1{v_1} - \frac1{f} = -\frac14 - \frac18 = -\frac38\ \Rightarrow\ u = -\frac83\ \text{cm}$$
$$\boxed{\text{Object distance} = \frac83\approx2.67\ \text{cm in front of the first lens}}$$
(Using $\dfrac1v + \dfrac1u$ conventions: lens 1 acts as a magnifier with the object inside its focal length, $u = 2.67$ cm, forming a virtual, enlarged image at $4$ cm, which lies exactly at the focal point of lens 2.)

Take a ring of radius $r$ and width $dr$: charge $dq = \sigma(2\pi r\,dr)$. Its field at $P$ (distance $x = \sqrt{r^2+z^2}$ from every element of the ring) has only an axial component (the perpendicular components cancel in pairs):
$$dE = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq\,z}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\sigma z}{2\varepsilon_0}\frac{r\,dr}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$$
Integrating from $r = 0$ to $R$ (put $s = r^2 + z^2$, $ds = 2r\,dr$):
$$E = \frac{\sigma z}{2\varepsilon_0}\int_0^R\frac{r\,dr}{(r^2+z^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\sigma z}{4\varepsilon_0}\left[-\frac2{\sqrt{r^2+z^2}}\right]_0^R = \frac{\sigma z}{2\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac1z - \frac1{\sqrt{R^2+z^2}}\right]$$
$$\boxed{E = \frac\sigma{2\varepsilon_0}\left[1 - \frac z{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\right]}$$
directed away from the disc if $\sigma>0$.

*Limits.* For $z\ll R$: $E\to\dfrac\sigma{2\varepsilon_0}$ (infinite sheet). For $z\gg R$: $\dfrac z{\sqrt{z^2+R^2}}\approx1 - \dfrac{R^2}{2z^2}$ so $E\approx\dfrac{\sigma R^2}{4\varepsilon_0z^2} = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0z^2}$ with $Q = \sigma\pi R^2$ (point charge).

**Charging of a capacitor through a resistor.** A capacitor $C$ is connected in series with a resistor $R$ and a battery of emf $V_0$ through a switch closed at $t = 0$, with the capacitor initially uncharged. At an instant $t$ let the charge on the capacitor be $q$ and the current $i = \dfrac{dq}{dt}$. By Kirchhoff's voltage law
$$V_0 = iR + \frac qC = R\frac{dq}{dt} + \frac qC$$
$$\frac{dq}{dt} = \frac{CV_0 - q}{RC}\ \Rightarrow\ \int_0^q\frac{dq}{CV_0 - q} = \frac1{RC}\int_0^tdt$$
$$-\ln\frac{CV_0 - q}{CV_0} = \frac t{RC}$$
$$\boxed{q(t) = CV_0\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right) = q_0\left(1 - e^{-t/RC}\right)}$$
where $q_0 = CV_0$ is the final charge. The current is $i = \dfrac{dq}{dt} = \dfrac{V_0}Re^{-t/RC}$ and the voltage across the capacitor is $V_C = V_0(1 - e^{-t/RC})$.

**Biot-Savart law vs Ampere's law.**

| Biot-Savart law | Ampere's circuital law |
|---|---|
| $d\vec B = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{i\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2}$ gives the field of a current element | $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0i_{enc}$ relates the line integral of $B$ to the current enclosed |
| Works for any current distribution, symmetric or not | Easy to use only when the current has high symmetry (long straight wire, solenoid, toroid) |
| Needs an integration over the whole conductor | Needs just the choice of a suitable Amperian loop |
| Analogue of Coulomb's law | Analogue of Gauss's law |

Both give the same result where both apply (e.g. $B = \dfrac{\mu_0i}{2\pi r}$ for a long straight wire).

**Axis of a circular coil.** A circular loop of radius $R$ carries a current $i$. Consider a point $P$ on the axis at distance $x$ from the centre. An element $d\vec l$ is perpendicular to the line $r = \sqrt{R^2+x^2}$ to $P$, so
$$dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{i\,dl}{R^2+x^2}$$
The component perpendicular to the axis cancels around the loop. The axial component is $dB\sin\phi$ with $\sin\phi = \dfrac R{\sqrt{R^2+x^2}}$:
$$B = \int dB\sin\phi = \frac{\mu_0i}{4\pi}\frac{R}{(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\oint dl = \frac{\mu_0i}{4\pi}\frac{R(2\pi R)}{(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}$$
$$\boxed{B = \frac{\mu_0iR^2}{2\,(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}}$$
At the centre $x = 0$, $B = \dfrac{\mu_0i}{2R}$; for $x\gg R$, $B\approx\dfrac{\mu_0iR^2}{2x^3}$. For a coil of $N$ turns multiply by $N$.

**OR: cyclotron.** A **cyclotron** is a device that accelerates charged particles (protons, deuterons, $\alpha$-particles) to high energies by repeatedly passing them through a small alternating potential difference between two hollow semicircular metal dees placed in a strong uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the dees. The particle moves in a semicircle inside each dee (no electric field there) and gains energy $qV$ every time it crosses the gap.

**Cyclotron frequency.** In the dee, $qvB = \dfrac{mv^2}r$, so $r = \dfrac{mv}{qB}$ and the time for half a revolution is $\dfrac{\pi r}{v} = \dfrac{\pi m}{qB}$, independent of $v$ and $r$. For resonance the oscillator frequency equals the revolution frequency
$$\begin{gathered}\boxed{f = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}} \\ \omega = \frac{qB}m\end{gathered}$$

**Energy independent of the oscillating field.** At the final radius $R$ (the radius of the dees), $v_{max} = \dfrac{qBR}m$, so
$$K_{max} = \frac12mv_{max}^2 = \frac{q^2B^2R^2}{2m}$$
This depends only on $q$, $m$, $B$ and $R$ and **not on the accelerating voltage $V$ of the oscillator**. A larger $V$ only means that fewer revolutions are needed ($N = K_{max}/2qV$) but the final energy is the same. $\blacksquare$

**Physical significance of the wave function $\psi$.**

- $\psi(\vec r,t)$ is the complex amplitude of the matter wave associated with a particle. It has **no direct physical meaning by itself** and is not measurable.
- The quantity $|\psi|^2 = \psi\psi^*$ is the **probability density**: $|\psi|^2dV$ is the probability of finding the particle in the volume $dV$ at the point $\vec r$ at time $t$ (Born's interpretation).
- Since the particle must be somewhere, $\psi$ is **normalized**: $\displaystyle\int|\psi|^2dV = 1$ over all space.
- A physically acceptable $\psi$ must be **single-valued, finite, continuous**, with a continuous first derivative, and must vanish at infinity (so that it is square-integrable).

**Derivation of the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.** Take a free particle of mass $m$, momentum $p$ and energy $E$ described by a plane wave travelling along $x$:
$$\psi(x,t) = Ae^{i(kx - \omega t)} = Ae^{\frac i\hbar(px - Et)}$$
where $k = p/\hbar$, $\omega = E/\hbar$. Differentiate:
$$\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{iE}\hbar\psi\ \Rightarrow\ E\psi = i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}$$
$$\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} = -\frac{p^2}{\hbar^2}\psi\ \Rightarrow\ p^2\psi = -\hbar^2\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2}$$
For a particle moving in a potential $V(x,t)$ the total energy is $E = \dfrac{p^2}{2m} + V$. Multiplying by $\psi$:
$$E\psi = \frac{p^2}{2m}\psi + V\psi$$
Substituting the two operator relations:
$$\boxed{i\hbar\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\psi}{\partial x^2} + V\psi}$$
In three dimensions $i\hbar\dfrac{\partial\psi}{\partial t} = -\dfrac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V\psi = \hat H\psi$. This is the **time-dependent Schrödinger wave equation**. When $V$ depends on position only, putting $\psi = \phi(x)e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ gives the time-independent form $\dfrac{d^2\phi}{dx^2} + \dfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)\phi = 0$.
