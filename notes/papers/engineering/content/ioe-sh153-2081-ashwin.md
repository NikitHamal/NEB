@s 1
**Construction of the cell.** A galvanic cell has a positive standard emf when the electrode with the **higher** reduction potential is the cathode:
$$E^\circ(\text{Ni}^{2+}/\text{Ni}) = -0.25\ \text{V}>E^\circ(\text{Cd}^{2+}/\text{Cd}) = -0.403\ \text{V}$$
So Cd is the anode (oxidation) and Ni the cathode (reduction):
$$\text{Cd(s)}\,|\,\text{Cd}^{2+}(c)\,\|\,\text{Ni}^{2+}(0.01\ \text{M})\,|\,\text{Ni(s)}$$
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Cd} + \text{Ni}^{2+}\to\text{Cd}^{2+} + \text{Ni} \\ n = 2\end{gathered}$$
$$E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{cathode} - E^\circ_{anode} = -0.25 - (-0.403) = +0.153\ \text{V}$$
**Concentration of Cd²⁺ for zero emf.** Nernst equation at 25°C:
$$E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0591}{2}\log\frac{[\text{Cd}^{2+}]}{[\text{Ni}^{2+}]} = 0$$
$$\log\frac{[\text{Cd}^{2+}]}{0.01} = \frac{2(0.153)}{0.0591} = 5.178\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{[\text{Cd}^{2+}]}{0.01} = 1.51\times10^5$$
$$\boxed{[\text{Cd}^{2+}]\approx1.5\times10^3\ \text{M}}$$
(This is far beyond the solubility of any cadmium salt, so with a $\text{Ni}^{2+}$ concentration of 0.01 M the emf cannot practically be reduced to zero by raising $[\text{Cd}^{2+}]$; the result only shows the equilibrium condition $E = 0$, i.e. $K = 10^{2E^\circ/0.0591} = 1.5\times10^{5}$ at equilibrium, $Q = K$.)

**Or. Lithium-ion battery.** A rechargeable (secondary) battery in which $\text{Li}^+$ ions shuttle between two intercalation electrodes through a non-aqueous electrolyte (a $\text{LiPF}_6$ solution in organic carbonates), with a separator. *Anode:* graphite ($\text{LiC}_6$ when charged); *cathode:* lithium cobalt oxide $\text{LiCoO}_2$ (or $\text{LiFePO}_4$, NMC); cell voltage 3.6–3.7 V.
*Discharge (spontaneous):*
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Anode: LiC}_6\to6\text{C} + \text{Li}^+ + e^- \\ \text{Cathode: Li}_{1-x}\text{CoO}_2 + x\text{Li}^+ + xe^-\to\text{LiCoO}_2\\ \text{Overall (for }x = 1):\ \text{LiC}_6 + \text{CoO}_2\to6\text{C} + \text{LiCoO}_2\end{gathered}$$
*Charging* (external current) reverses these reactions: $6\text{C} + \text{LiCoO}_2\to\text{LiC}_6 + \text{CoO}_2$; $\text{Li}^+$ moves back from the cathode through the electrolyte into the graphite.
*Advantages:* high energy density (150–250 Wh/kg), high voltage per cell, lightweight, no memory effect, low self-discharge (~2% per month), long cycle life (500–2000 cycles), no maintenance.
*Disadvantages:* expensive (Co, Li); thermal runaway, fire and explosion on overcharge or damage, so protection circuits are needed; ageing (capacity fades with time and with deep discharge); sensitive to high temperature; recycling problems.

@s 2
**Basic buffer** is a solution of a weak base and its salt with a strong acid; for example $\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$ + $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$. $\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$ is a weak base, only slightly ionised, whereas $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ is completely ionised and supplies a large reserve of $\text{NH}_4^+$.
- *On adding a small amount of acid* ($\text{H}^+$): it is neutralised by the weak base, $\text{NH}_4\text{OH} + \text{H}^+\to\text{NH}_4^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O}$, so $[\text{OH}^-]$ and the pH hardly change.
- *On adding a small amount of base* ($\text{OH}^-$): it is removed by the ammonium ions, $\text{NH}_4^+ + \text{OH}^-\to\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$, a weak base which ionises very little. The pH therefore changes very little.
$$\text{pOH} = \text{p}K_b + \log\frac{[\text{NH}_4^+]}{[\text{NH}_4\text{OH}]}$$

**Numerical.** 0.2 g NaOH $= \dfrac{0.2}{40} = 0.005$ mol in 1 L (volume change neglected). NaOH reacts with the salt: $\text{NaOH} + \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}\to\text{NaCl} + \text{NH}_4\text{OH}$.
$$\begin{gathered}[\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}] = 0.200 - 0.005 = 0.195\ \text{M} \\ [\text{NH}_4\text{OH}] = 0.100 + 0.005 = 0.105\ \text{M}\end{gathered}$$
$$\text{pOH} = 4.74 + \log\frac{0.195}{0.105} = 4.74 + 0.269 = 5.009$$
$$\boxed{\text{pH} = 14 - 5.009 = 8.99}$$
(The initial pH was $14 - \left(4.74 + \log\dfrac{0.2}{0.1}\right) = 8.96$, so the pH changes by only $+0.03$.)

@s 3
**Catalysts in pollution control.** Catalysts lower the activation energy of reactions that convert pollutants into harmless products at low temperatures, so they remove pollutants cheaply and efficiently.
1. **Automobile catalytic converter (three-way catalyst):** Pt, Pd and Rh on an alumina/ceria washcoat on a ceramic honeycomb convert the exhaust gases:
$$\begin{gathered}2\text{CO} + \text{O}_2\to2\text{CO}_2\ (\text{oxidation, Pt, Pd}) \\ \text{C}_x\text{H}_y + \text{O}_2\to\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \\ 2\text{NO} + 2\text{CO}\to\text{N}_2 + 2\text{CO}_2\ (\text{reduction, Rh})\end{gathered}$$
2. **Selective catalytic reduction (SCR)** of $\text{NO}_x$ in power plants with $\text{NH}_3$ over $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5/\text{TiO}_2$: $4\text{NO} + 4\text{NH}_3 + \text{O}_2\to4\text{N}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
3. **Photocatalysis:** $\text{TiO}_2$ under UV light generates $\cdot\text{OH}$ radicals that mineralise organic pollutants, dyes, pesticides and bacteria in water and air ($\text{self-cleaning}$ surfaces).
4. **Catalytic oxidation of VOCs and CO**, Fenton catalysts ($\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) for wastewater, and the hydrodesulfurisation of fuels with Co–Mo/$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ (to cut $\text{SO}_2$).
5. **Biocatalysts (enzymes):** biodegradation of oil, phenols and dyes in effluents.

**Two biocatalysts used in dye degradation.**
- **Laccase** (a copper-containing oxidase from white-rot fungi, e.g. *Trametes versicolor*): oxidises azo, anthraquinone and triphenylmethane dyes using $\text{O}_2$, forming water; a mediator (e.g. ABTS) extends the range.
- **Azoreductase** (from bacteria, e.g. *Pseudomonas*): cleaves the –N=N– azo bond of azo dyes (reductive) into colourless aromatic amines (later degraded aerobically).
(Peroxidases, such as lignin peroxidase and horseradish peroxidase with $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$, are other examples.)

@s 4
**Chromatography.** A physical method for the separation (and analysis) of the components of a mixture by distribution between a stationary phase and a mobile phase that moves through it; the components separate because of differences in adsorption, partition, charge or size.

**Thin-layer chromatography (TLC).** *Principle:* mainly **adsorption**. The stationary phase is a thin (0.25 mm) layer of silica gel or alumina on a glass or aluminium plate; the mobile phase is a solvent. A spot of the sample is applied near the bottom of the plate, which is then stood in a closed chamber in a shallow pool of solvent. The solvent climbs the plate by capillary action. Each component is adsorbed on the layer with a different strength and dissolved in the solvent to a different extent, so strongly adsorbed (polar) components move slowly, and weakly adsorbed ones move fast. The spots are visualised (UV lamp, iodine vapour, ninhydrin) and described by
$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance travelled by the component}}{\text{distance travelled by the solvent front}}$$
*Applications:* checking the purity of compounds and monitoring the progress of a reaction; identification of components by comparing $R_f$ with standards; analysis of amino acids, drugs, alkaloids, pigments, dyes, pesticides, and food additives; selection of the solvent for column chromatography, and small-scale preparative separations; forensic and clinical analysis.

**TLC vs paper chromatography.**

| Basis | TLC | Paper chromatography |
|---|---|---|
| Stationary phase | thin layer of an adsorbent (silica gel, alumina) on a plate | water held on the cellulose fibres of filter paper |
| Principle | mainly adsorption | mainly partition |
| Speed | fast (10–30 min) | slow (hours) |
| Separation | sharper spots, better resolution, high sensitivity | diffuse spots, lower resolution |
| Detection | corrosive reagents, heating, UV possible | restricted (paper is destroyed by corrosive reagents) |
| Use | wide range, including preparative | mainly hydrophilic compounds (amino acids, sugars) |

**Or. IR spectroscopy: principle.** Molecules absorb infrared radiation (wavenumbers 4000–400 cm⁻¹) when its frequency matches the frequency of a vibration (stretching or bending) of a bond **and** the vibration changes the dipole moment of the molecule (IR-active). The absorption excites the molecule from the ground to the first excited vibrational level, $\Delta E = h\nu$. The frequency of vibration of a bond is
$$\bar\nu = \frac{1}{2\pi c}\sqrt{\frac k\mu}$$
so stronger bonds and lighter atoms absorb at higher wavenumbers (C≡C > C=C > C–C; O–H > C–H > C–Cl). The spectrum is a plot of % transmittance against wavenumber.
*Applications:* (i) identification of functional groups (O–H 3600–3200 broad, N–H 3500–3300, C=O 1800–1650 strong, C≡N and C≡C 2260–2100); (ii) identification of an unknown compound by its fingerprint region (1500–400 cm⁻¹) compared with a standard; (iii) checking purity and the progress of reactions (disappearance of a band); (iv) distinguishing intra- and inter-molecular hydrogen bonding; (v) quantitative analysis of mixtures; (vi) study of polymers, pollutants (gas analysers for CO, $\text{CO}_2$), forensic and pharmaceutical analysis.

@s 5
**(a) Inner and outer orbital octahedral complexes (VBT).** In an octahedral complex the metal uses six hybrid orbitals to accept six ligand pairs.
- **Inner-orbital (low-spin) complex:** the metal uses the inner $(n-1)d$ orbitals, two $3d$, one $4s$, three $4p$: **$d^2sp^3$ hybridisation**. It requires pairing of the $d$ electrons (strong-field ligands such as $\text{CN}^-$, CO, en), so the complex is low spin, with few or no unpaired electrons (diamagnetic or weakly paramagnetic). Example: $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-}$, $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{3+}$.
- **Outer-orbital (high-spin) complex:** the metal uses the outer $nd$ orbitals, $4s$, three $4p$, two $4d$: **$sp^3d^2$ hybridisation**. The $d$ electrons are not paired (weak-field ligands such as $\text{F}^-$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{Cl}^-$), so the complex is high spin and paramagnetic. Example: $[\text{FeF}_6]^{3-}$, $[\text{CoF}_6]^{3-}$.

**$[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-}$:** $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ($x - 6 = -4$): $3d^6$ with 4 unpaired electrons in the free ion. The strong field $\text{CN}^-$ forces the six electrons to pair in the three $3d$ orbitals ($t_{2g}^6$), freeing two $3d$ orbitals:
$$\text{Fe}^{2+}:\ 3d^6\ [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\ \ ][\ \ ]\ \underline{4s}\ \underline{4p\,4p\,4p}\ \xrightarrow{d^2sp^3}\ \text{six CN}^-\text{ pairs}$$
- **Geometry: octahedral** ($d^2sp^3$, inner-orbital complex).
- **Magnetism: diamagnetic** (no unpaired electron, low spin, $\mu = 0$).

**(b) Formulae.**
(i) Potassium tetraiodomercurate(II): $\boxed{\text{K}_2[\text{HgI}_4]}$ (Hg²⁺ with four I⁻ gives $[\text{HgI}_4]^{2-}$, so two K⁺).
(ii) Dichloro-bis(ethylenediamine)nickel(IV) sulfate: $\boxed{[\text{Ni(en)}_2\text{Cl}_2]\text{SO}_4}$ ($+4 + 0 - 2 = +2$, balanced by $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$).
(iii) Tetraaquadichlorocobalt(III) chloride: $\boxed{[\text{Co(H}_2\text{O)}_4\text{Cl}_2]\text{Cl}}$ ($+3 - 2 = +1$).

**Metallic alloys.** An alloy is a homogeneous mixture (usually a solid solution) of a metal with one or more other metals or non-metals, made by melting the components together and cooling. Alloying improves hardness, strength, corrosion resistance, melting point and appearance.

| Alloy | Composition | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| Brass | Cu 60–70%, Zn 30–40% | utensils, valves, musical instruments, screws |
| Bronze | Cu 90%, Sn 10% | statues, coins, bearings, bells |
| Stainless steel | Fe, Cr 12–20%, Ni | cutlery, surgical tools, kitchen and chemical plants |
| Duralumin | Al 95%, Cu 4%, Mg, Mn | aircraft and vehicle bodies |
| Solder | Pb 50%, Sn 50% | joining electrical wires |
| Nichrome | Ni 60%, Cr 15–25%, Fe | heating elements |

**Colour and magnetism of $[\text{Cu(NH}_3)_6]^{3+}$ by CFT** (as printed: $\text{NH}_3$ is neutral, so Cu is in the +3 state, a $3d^8$ ion). In the octahedral field the $d$ orbitals split into $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ (gap $\Delta_o$). $3d^8$ configuration: $t_{2g}^6e_g^2$, with **two unpaired electrons** in the $e_g$ orbitals (one in each, by Hund's rule), whether the field is weak or strong:
$$t_{2g}^{\,6}\ e_g^{\,2}\ [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow\downarrow]\ [\uparrow][\uparrow]$$
- **Magnetic property: paramagnetic**, $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = \sqrt8 = 2.83$ BM.
- **Colour:** because the $t_{2g}$ level is full and the $e_g$ is only half-filled, an electron can be excited from $t_{2g}$ to $e_g$ by absorbing visible light of energy $\Delta_o = h\nu$ (d–d transition). The complex absorbs that colour and shows its complement. For $\text{NH}_3$ (fairly strong field) $\Delta_o$ is large, light in the yellow–green is absorbed and the complex appears deep **blue–violet**.
*Note:* the common, stable ammonia complex of copper is $[\text{Cu(NH}_3)_4(\text{H}_2\text{O})_2]^{2+}$ ($d^9$, one unpaired electron, paramagnetic, deep blue by the $t_{2g}^6e_g^3$ d–d transition). The Jahn–Teller effect distorts it, with the single hole in $e_g$.

@s 7
**(a) Goal of green chemistry.** To design chemical products and processes that **reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances**, protecting human health and the environment (pollution prevention at the source, sustainable chemistry), with economic benefits.

**Four principles of green chemistry (of the 12 of Anastas and Warner).**
1. **Prevention:** it is better to prevent waste than to treat or clean it afterwards.
2. **Atom economy:** use syntheses that put most of the reactants' atoms into the product: $\text{AE} = \dfrac{M_{product}}{\sum M_{reactants}}\times100$ (a Diels–Alder or hydrogenation has 100%).
3. **Use of safer solvents and auxiliaries:** avoid hazardous solvents (benzene, CCl₄); use water, supercritical $\text{CO}_2$, ionic liquids or no solvent.
4. **Catalysis:** use selective catalysts, which can be reused, instead of stoichiometric reagents. Other principles: less hazardous synthesis, design of safer chemicals, energy efficiency, renewable feedstocks, reduced derivatives, design for degradation, real-time analysis and inherently safer chemistry.

**(b) Degree of hardness.** Hardness of water is caused by dissolved calcium and magnesium salts (bicarbonates, chlorides, sulfates). The **degree of hardness** is the concentration of these salts, expressed in terms of an equivalent amount of $\text{CaCO}_3$ in mg/L (ppm): soft water 0–60, moderately hard 60–120, hard 120–180, very hard > 180 ppm.

**Scale formation in boilers.** When hard water is used in a boiler, continuous evaporation raises the concentration of dissolved salts until they exceed their solubility and form hard, adherent deposits (**scale**) on the inner surface:
- $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\xrightarrow{\Delta}\text{CaCO}_3\downarrow + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$ (temporary hardness; soft scale);
- $\text{CaSO}_4$, whose solubility falls with temperature (a very hard scale at high temperature and pressure);
- $\text{Mg(HCO}_3)_2\to\text{Mg(OH)}_2\downarrow$ (via $\text{MgCO}_3$ hydrolysis); silica as $\text{CaSiO}_3$, $\text{MgSiO}_3$.
Scale is a poor conductor of heat, so it wastes fuel and overheats the plates (risk of explosion), and it clogs pipes. It is prevented by softening the feed water (lime–soda, zeolite, ion exchange) and by internal conditioning (phosphate, calgon).

**Chemical oxygen demand (COD).** The amount of oxygen (mg O₂/L) required to oxidise **all** oxidisable organic matter (biodegradable and non-biodegradable) and some inorganic matter in a water sample chemically, with a strong oxidant, usually boiling acidified potassium dichromate with $\text{Ag}_2\text{SO}_4$ catalyst (2 h reflux); the unused dichromate is titrated against ferrous ammonium sulfate (FAS):
$$\text{COD} = \frac{(V_b - V_s)\times N_{FAS}\times8\times1000}{V_{sample}}\ \text{mg O}_2/\text{L}$$
where $V_b$ and $V_s$ are the volumes of FAS for the blank and sample.
*Consequences:* a high COD indicates a large load of organic and chemical pollutants. When such water is discharged, it depletes the dissolved oxygen; aquatic life (fish) suffers or dies, the water turns anaerobic with a foul odour ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{CH}_4$), toxic organics may enter the food chain and the water is unfit for drinking, irrigation and recreation. It leads to eutrophication, and treatment costs rise. (COD $\ge$ BOD; the ratio BOD/COD shows the biodegradability.)

@s 8
**Nanofibres.** Fibres whose diameter lies in the nanometre range (about 1–100 nm; up to several hundred nm in practice) and whose length is micrometres to metres (a very high aspect ratio, > 100). They are made by electrospinning (the main method), template synthesis, phase separation, self-assembly, or CVD, from polymers (PAN, PVA, chitosan, PLA), carbon, metal oxides ($\text{TiO}_2$, ZnO) or ceramics. They have a very large specific surface area, high porosity, flexibility and strength, and tunable surface chemistry.

**Applications in health.**
- **Tissue engineering:** nanofibre scaffolds (PLA, collagen, PCL) mimic the extracellular matrix and support the growth of skin, bone, nerve and cartilage cells.
- **Wound dressing:** the porous mats let air pass, absorb exudate, keep out bacteria and carry antibiotics or silver.
- **Drug delivery:** drugs encapsulated in the fibres are released in a controlled way (and in implants).
- **Biosensors and diagnostics:** high surface area for glucose and DNA sensing; face masks and medical filters that capture viruses and bacteria.

**Applications in environment.**
- **Air and water filtration:** nanofibre membranes capture PM$_{2.5}$, aerosols, and bacteria, and remove heavy metals and dyes by adsorption.
- **Photocatalytic degradation** of pollutants and dyes (TiO₂, ZnO nanofibres); oil–water separation (superhydrophobic mats).
- **Energy and sensors:** gas sensors for pollutants; nanofibre electrodes in batteries, supercapacitors and fuel cells; carbon nanofibres for $\text{CO}_2$ capture and hydrogen storage.

@s 9
**(a) Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA).** *Preparation:* **free-radical addition polymerisation** of methyl methacrylate (MMA), in bulk, suspension or emulsion, with benzoyl peroxide or AIBN as initiator at 60–80°C:
$$n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)\text{COOCH}_3\xrightarrow{\text{peroxide},\ \Delta}[\text{–CH}_2\text{–C(CH}_3)(\text{COOCH}_3)\text{–}]_n$$
(MMA itself is made from acetone cyanohydrin, or by oxidation of isobutylene.) PMMA is a hard, transparent thermoplastic (92% light transmission), lighter than glass, shatter-resistant and weather-resistant.
*Uses:* aircraft canopies and windows, aquaria, skylights and signboards, lenses, light covers and car tail-lights, dentures and bone cement, and as the glass substitute (Perspex, Plexiglas, Lucite).

**Cationic polymerisation** (initiated by an electrophile, for monomers with electron-donating groups). *Example:* isobutylene (2-methylpropene) with $\text{BF}_3$ and a trace of water (co-catalyst), in a non-polar solvent at $-80^\circ$C:
1. **Initiation:** $\text{BF}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\to\text{H}^+[\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-$; the proton adds to the monomer, forming a tertiary carbocation:
$$\text{H}^+ + \text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)_2\to\text{(CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+\ [\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-$$
2. **Propagation:** the carbocation adds to further monomer molecules, with a new carbocation at the chain end each time:
$$\text{(CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+ + n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)_2\to\text{(CH}_3)_3\text{C–[CH}_2\text{–C(CH}_3)_2]_n\text{–CH}_2\text{–C}^+(\text{CH}_3)_2$$
3. **Termination:** loss of a proton from the chain end to the counter-ion gives a terminal double bond and regenerates the catalyst, or the carbocation combines with the counter-ion, $\text{–C}^+ + [\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-\to\text{–C–OH} + \text{BF}_3$. Product: polyisobutylene (used in butyl rubber and sealants).

**(b) Kevlar (poly-*p*-phenylene terephthalamide, an aramid).** *Preparation:* condensation (step) polymerisation of *p*-phenylenediamine with terephthaloyl chloride in an amide solvent (N-methylpyrrolidone + $\text{CaCl}_2$), at low temperature, with loss of HCl; the polymer is spun from concentrated sulfuric acid solution (liquid crystal) into fibre:
$$n\,\text{H}_2\text{N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–NH}_2 + n\,\text{ClOC–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–COCl}\to[\text{–NH–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–NH–CO–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–CO–}]_n + 2n\,\text{HCl}$$
Rigid rod-like chains with strong inter-chain hydrogen bonds give a tensile strength five times that of steel (by weight), heat resistance up to 450°C, and low weight.
*Applications:* bullet-proof vests and helmets, protective gloves and fire-fighters' clothing, tyre cords, ropes and cables, brake pads and gaskets, aerospace and sports composites.

**Polyurethane.** *Preparation:* **polyaddition** of a diisocyanate (TDI or MDI) with a polyol or diol (no by-product):
$$n\,\text{OCN–R–NCO} + n\,\text{HO–R'–OH}\to[\text{–CO–NH–R–NH–CO–O–R'–O–}]_n$$
Water added to the mixture releases $\text{CO}_2$ to form foam.
*Applications:* flexible foam (mattresses, furniture, car seats), rigid foam insulation (refrigerators, roofs), elastomers (shoe soles, wheels, tyres), coatings, adhesives and sealants, synthetic leather, and spandex fibre.

**Or. (i) Carbon fibre reinforced plastics (CFRP).** Composites in which carbon fibres (from PAN, 5–10 µm diameter, > 90% carbon) are embedded in a polymer matrix (usually epoxy). The fibres carry the load, the matrix binds them and transfers stress. CFRP has a very high strength-to-weight ratio, stiffness, fatigue and corrosion resistance (but is costly, and brittle). *Uses:* aircraft (Boeing 787) and spacecraft parts, racing cars, wind-turbine blades, sports goods (rackets, bicycles), prosthetics, and strengthening of concrete bridges.

**Collagen.** The most abundant protein in animals (about 30% of body protein): the main structural protein of skin, tendons, bone, cartilage and connective tissue. It is a **triple helix** of three polypeptide chains, rich in glycine (every third residue), proline and hydroxyproline, held by interchain hydrogen bonds. It is a natural, biodegradable and biocompatible polymer, and boiling it gives gelatin. *Uses:* wound dressings, artificial skin, sutures, bone grafts, tissue-engineering scaffolds, drug delivery, cosmetics, and food (gelatin) and leather.

**(ii) Hydration of cement.** Portland cement contains tricalcium silicate ($\text{C}_3\text{S} = 3\text{CaO·SiO}_2$), dicalcium silicate ($\text{C}_2\text{S}$), tricalcium aluminate ($\text{C}_3\text{A}$) and tetracalcium aluminoferrite ($\text{C}_4\text{AF}$). On mixing with water they hydrate, giving a gel that sets and hardens:
$$\begin{gathered}2\,(3\text{CaO·SiO}_2) + 6\,\text{H}_2\text{O}\to3\text{CaO·2SiO}_2\text{·3H}_2\text{O} + 3\,\text{Ca(OH)}_2\ (\text{fast, early strength}) \\ 2\,(2\text{CaO·SiO}_2) + 4\,\text{H}_2\text{O}\to3\text{CaO·2SiO}_2\text{·3H}_2\text{O} + \text{Ca(OH)}_2\ (\text{slow, later strength}) \\ 3\text{CaO·Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 6\,\text{H}_2\text{O}\to3\text{CaO·Al}_2\text{O}_3\text{·6H}_2\text{O}\ (\text{very rapid; gypsum retards it})\end{gathered}$$
The hydrated calcium silicate (C–S–H gel) is the main binding phase and gives the strength; the reactions are exothermic (heat of hydration).

**(a) Requisites of a good paint.**
1. **Good spreading and covering power (opacity):** it should cover the surface with a thin, uniform coat, from a pigment of high refractive index ($\text{TiO}_2$, white lead).
2. **Good adhesion** to the surface, flexibility without cracking, peeling or blistering.
3. **Quick drying** to form a hard, smooth, durable and glossy film (drying oil as the vehicle, with a drier).
4. **Resistance** to weather, sunlight, moisture, chemicals, heat, abrasion and fungi, and **protection** from corrosion (rust-inhibiting pigments).
5. **Colour permanence** (no fading, chalking), a proper viscosity for application, non-toxic with a low VOC content and low cost.

**Impact of paint on human health.** Many paints contain volatile organic compounds (VOCs: toluene, xylene, benzene, formaldehyde), solvents, and pigments with heavy metals (lead chromate, cadmium, mercury, chromium). *Effects:*
- inhalation of VOC vapours causes headache, dizziness, nausea, eye, nose and throat irritation, and damage to liver, kidneys and nervous system on long exposure (painters);
- **lead** in old paints damages the brain and nervous system of children (lower IQ), and causes anaemia and kidney disease; cadmium and chromium(VI) are carcinogenic; benzene causes leukaemia;
- skin irritation and allergic dermatitis; asthma and respiratory problems; and paint dust and flakes enter the environment and water.
*Remedies:* low-VOC, water-based and lead-free paints, good ventilation, and protective equipment.

**(b) Preparation of TNG (trinitroglycerine, glyceryl trinitrate, nitroglycerine).** It is prepared by **nitration of glycerol** with a mixture of conc. $\text{HNO}_3$ and conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ (the nitrating mixture; $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ takes up the water and keeps the acid strong), at a temperature kept below 10–15°C by cooling and stirring, since the reaction is highly exothermic and the product is explosive:
$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3 + 3\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ <15^\circ\text{C}}\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{ONO}_2)_3 + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The mixture separates into an upper oily layer of nitroglycerine, which is washed with water and dilute sodium carbonate to remove acid, then dried. Because it is very shock-sensitive, it is absorbed in kieselguhr to make dynamite.
*Uses:* **explosive** in dynamite, blasting gelatin and in mining, quarrying, tunnelling and demolition; a constituent of smokeless propellants (double-base powder, cordite); in **medicine** as a vasodilator for angina pectoris (as tablets, patches or spray).
