@s 1
**Electrolytic cell vs galvanic cell.**

| Basis | Galvanic (voltaic) cell | Electrolytic cell |
|---|---|---|
| Energy change | chemical $\to$ electrical | electrical $\to$ chemical |
| Reaction | spontaneous ($\Delta G<0$, $E_{\text{cell}}>0$) | non-spontaneous ($\Delta G>0$), driven by an external source |
| Anode / cathode | anode is negative (−), cathode is positive (+) | anode is positive (+), cathode is negative (−) |
| Electrolyte | two half-cells joined by a salt bridge | usually a single vessel and electrolyte |
| Examples | Daniell cell, dry cell | electrolysis of NaCl, electroplating |

**Numerical.** The more negative couple (Zn, $-0.763$ V) is oxidised at the anode, and Cd$^{2+}$ is reduced at the cathode. Cell: $\text{Zn}|\text{Zn}^{2+}(0.05\,\text{M})\,\|\,\text{Cd}^{2+}(0.1\,\text{M})|\text{Cd}$, with reaction $\text{Zn} + \text{Cd}^{2+}\to\text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cd}$, $n = 2$.
$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = -0.403 - (-0.763) = 0.360\ \text{V}$$
$$E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ - \frac{0.0591}{2}\log\frac{[\text{Zn}^{2+}]}{[\text{Cd}^{2+}]} = 0.360 - 0.02955\log\frac{0.05}{0.1} = 0.360 + 0.02955(0.301)$$
$$\boxed{E_{\text{cell}} = 0.360 + 0.0089 = 0.369\ \text{V}}$$

**Or.** **Polarisation** is the shift of an electrode potential from its equilibrium value when current flows (causes: slow electron transfer, concentration changes at the electrode surface, ohmic resistance, gas bubble films). **Overvoltage (overpotential)** is the extra voltage beyond the reversible value needed to drive an electrode reaction at a finite rate, $\eta = E_{\text{applied}} - E_{\text{rev}}$ (e.g. about 1 V for H₂ evolution on mercury).

**Butler–Volmer equation.**
$$\boxed{j = j_0\left[e^{\alpha_aF\eta/RT} - e^{-\alpha_cF\eta/RT}\right]}$$
$j$ is the net current density, $j_0$ the exchange current density, $\alpha_a$, $\alpha_c$ the transfer coefficients. At small $\eta$ it becomes linear, $j = j_0F\eta/RT$; at large $\eta$ it gives the Tafel law $\eta = a + b\log j$.

*Applications:* (i) determining the kinetic parameters $j_0$ and $\alpha$ of electrode reactions; (ii) predicting corrosion rates; (iii) designing electrolysers, electroplating baths and the energy needed for electrolysis; (iv) improving fuel cells and batteries; (v) selecting electrocatalysts (e.g. low overvoltage Pt for H₂).

@s 2
**Henderson–Hasselbalch equation for an acidic buffer.** An acidic buffer is a weak acid HA and its salt $\text{A}^-$ (e.g. $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ + $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$).
$$\text{HA}\rightleftharpoons\text{H}^+ + \text{A}^-,\qquad K_a = \frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}\ \Rightarrow\ [\text{H}^+] = K_a\frac{[\text{HA}]}{[\text{A}^-]}$$
The salt is completely ionised and suppresses the ionisation of the weak acid, so $[\text{A}^-]\approx[\text{salt}]$ and $[\text{HA}]\approx[\text{acid}]$. Taking $-\log$ of both sides:
$$\boxed{\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]}}$$

**Numerical.** Moles of acid $= 0.1\times0.150 = 0.015$ mol. Moles of NaOH $= 0.005$ mol, which converts acid to acetate: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{OH}^-\to\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.
$$\begin{gathered}n_{\text{acid}} = 0.015 - 0.005 = 0.010\ \text{mol} \\ n_{\text{salt}} = 0.005\ \text{mol}\end{gathered}$$
(Both are in the same volume, so the ratio of moles equals the ratio of concentrations.)
$$\text{pH} = 4.74 + \log\frac{0.005}{0.010} = 4.74 - 0.301 = \boxed{4.44}$$

@s 3
**Selection criteria of a catalyst.**
1. **Activity**: it should greatly increase the reaction rate (high turnover frequency) under mild conditions.
2. **Selectivity**: it should favour the desired product over side products.
3. **Stability**: it must withstand the temperature, pressure and reaction medium, without sintering or leaching.
4. **Resistance to poisoning** by impurities (S, CO, Cl compounds) and to fouling (coking), and **regenerability** for reuse.
5. **High surface area and porosity**, and adequate mechanical strength.
6. It should be **cheap and readily available**, easily separated from the products (heterogeneous), and non-toxic.

**Hydrogen production from fossil fuels.**
1. **Steam methane reforming (SMR)**, the main industrial route (about 50% of the world's H₂). Natural gas reacts with steam over a Ni/Al₂O₃ catalyst at 700–1000°C and 3–25 bar:
$$\text{CH}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{CO} + 3\text{H}_2\qquad(\Delta H = +206\ \text{kJ/mol})$$
The CO is then converted by the **water–gas shift reaction** over Fe₃O₄/Cr₂O₃ (high T) and Cu/ZnO (low T): $\text{CO} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2$ ($\Delta H = -41$ kJ/mol). CO₂ is removed by amine scrubbing or PSA to give pure H₂.
2. **Partial oxidation** of hydrocarbons (heavy oil, natural gas) with limited oxygen at 1200–1500°C: $\text{CH}_4 + \tfrac12\text{O}_2\to\text{CO} + 2\text{H}_2$, followed by the shift reaction. (**Autothermal reforming** combines this with SMR.)
3. **Coal gasification**: hot coke or coal reacts with steam at 1000°C to give **water gas**: $\text{C} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\to\text{CO} + \text{H}_2$ ($\Delta H = +131$ kJ/mol), followed by the shift reaction.
4. **Methane pyrolysis** (thermal cracking): $\text{CH}_4\xrightarrow{\ >800^\circ\text{C}\ }\text{C(s)} + 2\text{H}_2$ gives H₂ and solid carbon with no CO₂.

All routes except pyrolysis emit CO₂ ("grey hydrogen"); capturing it gives "blue hydrogen".

@s 4
**Chromatography** is a physical method of separating the components of a mixture based on their different distribution between a stationary phase and a mobile phase that moves over it.

**Thin-layer chromatography (TLC).**
*Principle:* mainly **adsorption** chromatography. The stationary phase is a thin layer (0.2 mm) of an adsorbent (silica gel or alumina) spread on a glass, plastic or aluminium plate; the mobile phase is a solvent or solvent mixture. The mixture is spotted near the bottom of the plate and developed in a closed chamber, with the solvent moving up by capillary action. Components are adsorbed to different degrees: the weakly adsorbed (more soluble in the solvent) travel farther, the strongly adsorbed travel less. Spots are located under UV light, iodine vapour or a spray reagent, and identified by
$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance travelled by the component}}{\text{distance travelled by the solvent front}}$$
*Applications:* (i) checking the purity of a compound and monitoring the progress of a reaction; (ii) identification of amino acids, drugs, dyes and food colours; (iii) analysis of plant extracts, pesticides and pollutants; (iv) selecting the solvent system for column chromatography; (v) small-scale preparative separation. It is fast, cheap and needs very little sample.

**Or. Spectroscopy** is the study of the interaction of electromagnetic radiation with matter, in which the absorption or emission of radiation is used to find the structure and composition of the matter.

**Principle of IR spectroscopy.** Infrared radiation (wavenumber 4000–400 cm⁻¹) is absorbed when its frequency equals a **vibrational frequency** of a bond and the vibration changes the dipole moment of the molecule. The molecule goes from the vibrational level $v = 0$ to $v = 1$. The frequency is $\bar\nu = \dfrac1{2\pi c}\sqrt{k/\mu}$, so stronger bonds (higher force constant $k$) and lighter atoms absorb at higher wavenumber.

**IR spectrum of ethanol** ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$):

| Band (cm⁻¹) | Assignment |
|---|---|
| 3200–3550 (strong, broad) | O–H stretching, broadened by hydrogen bonding |
| 2850–2980 (strong) | C–H stretching of CH₃ and CH₂ (sp³) |
| 1380–1470 (medium) | C–H bending (CH₃, CH₂) |
| 1050–1100 (strong) | C–O stretching of the primary alcohol |
| about 1640–1700 | no band: no C=O is present |

**VBT for $[\text{FeF}_6]^{3-}$.**
- Oxidation state of Fe: $x + 6(-1) = -3$, so $x = +3$. Fe³⁺ is $[\text{Ar}]3d^5$, with five unpaired electrons.
- F⁻ is a **weak-field** ligand, so it cannot pair the electrons in 3d. Six F⁻ donate lone pairs into the vacant 4s, three 4p and two 4d orbitals.
- Hybridisation: **$sp^3d^2$** (outer orbital complex). Hybrid orbitals point to the corners of an octahedron.

| | 3d | 4s | 4p | 4d |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fe³⁺ | ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ | ○ | ○ ○ ○ | ○ ○ |
| $[\text{FeF}_6]^{3-}$ | ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ | ⇅ | ⇅ ⇅ ⇅ | ⇅ ⇅ |

**Structure:** octahedral. **Magnetic behaviour:** five unpaired electrons, so **paramagnetic** (high spin), $\mu = \sqrt{5\times7} = 5.92$ BM.

**IUPAC names.**
- (i) $\text{Na}_2[\text{NiCl}_4]$: **sodium tetrachloridonickelate(II)**.
- (ii) $[\text{Co(NO}_3)_6]^{3-}$: **hexanitrato-$\kappa O$-cobaltate(III) ion** (nitrate bound through oxygen).
- (iii) $[\text{Cr(en)}_2\text{Cl}_2]^+$: **dichloridobis(ethane-1,2-diamine)chromium(III) ion**.
- (iv) $[\text{Pt(NH}_3)_4\text{Cl}_2]\text{Cl}_2$: **tetraamminedichloridoplatinum(IV) chloride**.

@s 6
**Postulates of crystal field theory.**
1. The metal–ligand interaction is purely **electrostatic**. Ligands are point negative charges (or the negative ends of dipoles), and the metal ion is a positive point charge; there is no covalent overlap.
2. The five $d$ orbitals of the metal ion are degenerate in the free ion, but in the field of the ligands the electrons in the orbitals pointing toward the ligands are repelled more, and the **degeneracy is lifted**.
3. In an **octahedral** field, $d_{z^2}$ and $d_{x^2-y^2}$ ($e_g$) go up by $0.6\Delta_o$ and $d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{zx}$ ($t_{2g}$) go down by $0.4\Delta_o$. In a **tetrahedral** field the splitting is reversed and smaller ($\Delta_t = \tfrac49\Delta_o$), and in a **square planar** field the $d_{x^2-y^2}$ orbital is highest.
4. The splitting $\Delta$ depends on the ligand (spectrochemical series, weak field $\text{I}^-<\text{Br}^-<\text{Cl}^-<\text{F}^-<\text{H}_2\text{O}<\text{NH}_3<\text{en}<\text{CN}^-<\text{CO}$ strong field), and on the metal ion and its charge.
5. Electrons fill the orbitals following the balance between $\Delta_o$ and the pairing energy $P$: $\Delta_o<P$ gives **high-spin**, $\Delta_o>P$ gives **low-spin** complexes.
6. The colour of complexes is due to $d$–$d$ transitions ($t_{2g}\to e_g$), and the stability to the crystal field stabilisation energy $\text{CFSE} = (-0.4n_{t_{2g}} + 0.6n_{e_g})\Delta_o + (\text{pairing})$.

**Applications of neodymium.**
- **NdFeB permanent magnets**, the strongest commercial magnets, used in electric-vehicle motors, wind-turbine generators, hard-disk drives, headphones, speakers and MRI machines.
- **Nd:YAG lasers** (1064 nm) for cutting, welding, surgery and range-finding.
- **Glass and ceramics**: violet colour, and didymium glass in welders' goggles that filters the sodium yellow flare; also in capacitors.
- Alloying additive in magnesium and aluminium alloys for aerospace parts, and in catalysts for polymerisation (Nd-based catalyst for butadiene rubber).

@s 7
**(a) Green chemistry** is the design of chemical products and processes that **reduce or eliminate the use and generation of hazardous substances**, so that pollution is prevented at its source. It was formulated by Anastas and Warner as 12 principles.

**Four major principles:**
1. **Prevention of waste**: it is better to prevent waste than to treat it after it has been created. *E-factor* = kg waste per kg product should be small.
2. **Atom economy**: syntheses should incorporate the maximum amount of the reactant atoms into the final product:
$$\text{atom economy} = \frac{\text{molecular mass of the desired product}}{\text{sum of molecular masses of all reactants}}\times100\%$$
3. **Use of safer solvents and auxiliaries**: avoid VOC and chlorinated solvents, or replace them with water, supercritical CO₂ or ionic liquids.
4. **Catalysis**: selective catalytic reagents are superior to stoichiometric reagents, because they are used in small amounts and give less waste.

(Also: less hazardous synthesis, safer chemicals, energy efficiency, renewable feedstocks, design for degradation, real-time analysis, inherently safer chemistry.)

**(b) BOD vs COD.**

| Basis | BOD | COD |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | oxygen needed by **microorganisms** to oxidise **biodegradable** organic matter | oxygen needed to oxidise **all** organic matter **chemically** |
| Oxidant | dissolved oxygen, with bacteria | strong chemical oxidant ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ + $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) |
| Time and temperature | 5 days at 20°C | about 2–3 hours at 150°C |
| Value | lower | higher: COD $\ge$ BOD |
| Shows | the biodegradable load | the total organic load, including non-biodegradable matter |

**Adverse effects of excess COD.** A high COD means a large amount of oxidisable pollutants in the water.
- It **depletes the dissolved oxygen** as the organic matter is oxidised, so fish and other aquatic organisms suffocate (fish kill).
- With very low DO, **anaerobic** conditions develop, giving foul odour ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{CH}_4$, NH₃) and black, unfit water.
- The water has poor taste and colour and is unfit for drinking and irrigation, and the aquatic ecosystem is damaged.
- Non-biodegradable organics (dyes, pesticides, phenols) that raise COD may be toxic and bioaccumulate in the food chain; nutrients that accompany them can cause eutrophication.

@s 8
Nanomaterials (1–100 nm) can be prepared by **top-down** (breaking down bulk material) or **bottom-up** (building from atoms) methods. Ball milling and exfoliation are top-down methods.

**Ball milling.** A mechanical attrition method. The bulk powder is placed in a hardened steel, tungsten carbide or zirconia **jar** with hard **balls** (ball-to-powder ratio about 10:1–20:1), usually in an inert atmosphere (Ar) or with a liquid. The jar is rotated, or shaken (planetary or high-energy mill), at high speed, so the balls fall on the powder and **impact, shear and crush** it. Repeated fracturing and cold welding reduces the particle size to the nanometre range (5–100 nm) over hours, and can form alloys and composites (mechanical alloying).
*Advantages:* simple, cheap, scalable, works for metals, ceramics and polymers. *Limitations:* contamination from the balls and jar, broad particle-size distribution, irregular shapes, and amorphisation.

**Exfoliation.** A top-down method for **2-D nanomaterials**: layered materials with strong in-plane bonds but weak van der Waals forces between layers (graphite, MoS₂, h-BN, clays) are separated into one or a few layers.
- *Mechanical exfoliation* (the "Scotch tape" method): adhesive tape is repeatedly pressed on graphite and peeled off to leave single-layer **graphene** on the tape, which is transferred to a substrate.
- *Liquid-phase exfoliation*: the layered powder is sonicated in a solvent (NMP, DMF) or a surfactant solution; the ultrasound overcomes the interlayer attraction and the layers are stabilised by the liquid, then centrifuged to select thin flakes.
- *Chemical exfoliation*: graphite is oxidised (Hummers' method: $\text{KMnO}_4$/$\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) into graphite oxide, which swells and exfoliates in water into graphene oxide, and is then reduced (hydrazine or heat) to graphene.

@s 9
**(a) Addition vs condensation polymers.**

| Addition polymer | Condensation polymer |
|---|---|
| Formed by the repeated addition of unsaturated monomers (C=C) | Formed by the reaction between monomers with two functional groups (–OH, –COOH, –NH₂, –COCl) |
| No by-product is lost | A small molecule (H₂O, HCl, NH₃) is eliminated at each step |
| Chain-growth mechanism, high molecular mass formed at once | Step-growth mechanism, molecular mass rises slowly |
| Repeating unit has the same formula as the monomer | Repeating unit differs from the monomer(s) |
| Examples: polythene, PVC, polystyrene | Examples: nylon-6,6, polyester (terylene), Bakelite |

**Free-radical mechanism** (example: vinyl chloride $\to$ PVC, with benzoyl peroxide as the initiator).
1. **Initiation**: the initiator breaks homolytically on heating, and the radical adds to a monomer.
$$(\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO})_2\xrightarrow{\Delta}2\,\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO}\cdot\to2\,\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\cdot + 2\,\text{CO}_2,\qquad \text{R}\cdot + \text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHCl}\to\text{R–CH}_2\text{–C}\cdot\text{HCl}$$
2. **Propagation**: the radical end adds successive monomers rapidly, $\text{R–CH}_2\text{–C}\cdot\text{HCl} + n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHCl}\to\text{R–(CH}_2\text{CHCl)}_n\text{–CH}_2\text{–C}\cdot\text{HCl}$.
3. **Termination**: two chain radicals combine, $\sim\text{CHCl}\cdot + \cdot\text{CHCl}\sim\to\sim\text{CHCl–CHCl}\sim$, or disproportionate (hydrogen transfer to give one saturated and one unsaturated chain).

The product is poly(vinyl chloride), $[\text{–CH}_2\text{–CHCl–}]_n$.

**(b) Conducting polymers** are polymers whose backbone has conjugated (alternating) double bonds, so that after doping (oxidation or reduction) they conduct electricity, e.g. polyacetylene, polyaniline, polypyrrole, polythiophene.

**Epoxy resin.** *Preparation:* **bisphenol-A** and excess **epichlorohydrin** are condensed in the presence of NaOH (50–60°C), giving the diglycidyl ether prepolymer (DGEBA, with terminal epoxide groups and –OH groups, $n = 0$–$25$) with loss of NaCl. It is cured (hardened) by adding a polyamine such as $\text{H}_2\text{N(CH}_2)_2\text{NH(CH}_2)_2\text{NH}_2$ or an acid anhydride, which opens the epoxide rings to form a three-dimensional cross-linked network.
*Uses:* adhesives (Araldite), surface coatings and paints for metals and floors, matrices for glass- and carbon-fibre composites, electrical insulation and encapsulation of circuits, laminates (printed circuit boards).

**Polyphosphazene.** *Preparation:* $\text{PCl}_5 + \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}\to(\text{NPCl}_2)_3 + 4\text{HCl}$, then ring-opening polymerisation of the cyclic trimer at 250°C gives $[\text{–N=PCl}_2\text{–}]_n$, and the chlorine is replaced with $-\text{OR}$ or $-\text{NHR}$ by treating it with sodium alkoxide or amine to give stable poly(organophosphazenes).
*Uses:* fire-resistant materials, low-temperature elastomers and seals, biomedical materials (drug delivery, bone regeneration), and solid polymer electrolytes.

**Or. (i) Polyaniline.** *Preparation:* aniline is oxidatively polymerised in 1 M HCl at 0–5°C using ammonium persulphate (or chemically or electrochemically); the head-to-tail coupling gives the green conducting **emeraldine salt**, and deprotonation with NH₄OH gives the blue insulating emeraldine base: $n\,\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2\xrightarrow{(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8}[\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–NH–}]_n$.
*Uses:* anticorrosion paints, antistatic coatings, EMI shielding, chemical and biosensors, batteries and supercapacitors, and electrochromic displays.

**(ii) Hardening of cement.** When water is added to cement, the compounds hydrate and form a rigid mass in two stages: **setting** (loss of plasticity in 30 min to 10 h) and then **hardening** (strength gain over days and weeks, with 90% of the strength in 28 days).
- Tricalcium aluminate (the fastest) reacts first, and would cause a flash set, so gypsum is added to the clinker to control it:
$$3\text{CaO}\cdot\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}\to3\text{CaO}\cdot\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3\cdot6\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
- Tricalcium silicate (early strength) and dicalcium silicate (later strength) hydrate to calcium silicate hydrate gel (C–S–H) and calcium hydroxide:
$$2(3\text{CaO}\cdot\text{SiO}_2) + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}\to3\text{CaO}\cdot2\text{SiO}_2\cdot3\text{H}_2\text{O} + 3\text{Ca(OH)}_2$$
$$2(2\text{CaO}\cdot\text{SiO}_2) + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}\to3\text{CaO}\cdot2\text{SiO}_2\cdot3\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{Ca(OH)}_2$$
The gel hardens by crystallisation (interlocking needle-like crystals) and by gradual loss of water; $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$ crystals fill the pores and slowly absorb CO₂ from air. The heat evolved is the heat of hydration. The concrete must be **cured** (kept moist) so that hydration continues, otherwise it cracks.

**(a) Primary explosive.** A primary explosive is one that is extremely **sensitive** to heat, shock or friction and detonates readily from a small stimulus, giving a shock wave that sets off less sensitive (secondary) explosives. *Examples:* lead azide $\text{Pb(N}_3)_2$, mercury fulminate $\text{Hg(ONC)}_2$, lead styphnate. Its main use is in **detonators and primers**.

**TNG (trinitroglycerine, glyceryl trinitrate).**
*Preparation:* glycerol is nitrated by slow addition to a cooled mixture of concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄ (about 1:2), in a nitrator with brine cooling and stirring, at a temperature **below 10–15°C** (the reaction is strongly exothermic, and the product decomposes explosively when hot):
$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3 + 3\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ <15^\circ\text{C}}\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{ONO}_2)_3 + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The oily layer of nitroglycerine rises above the acid and is separated, washed with water and with sodium carbonate solution to remove acid, and dried.
*Uses:* manufacture of **dynamite** (absorbed in kieselguhr), blasting gelatine and smokeless propellants (cordite); mining, tunnelling and demolition; and, in dilute form, as a vasodilator drug for angina.

**(b) Requisites of a good paint.**
- Good **covering power** (opacity), easy spreading, and a smooth, uniform, glossy film.
- Strong **adhesion** to the surface, with a film that is hard, tough and flexible, and does not crack, peel or blister.
- Quick and proper **drying**, and the pigment must remain uniformly suspended in the vehicle.
- **Durability**: resistance to weather, sunlight, water, chemicals and abrasion; the colour must not fade.
- It should protect against **corrosion**, be non-toxic, have low VOC and be cheap.
