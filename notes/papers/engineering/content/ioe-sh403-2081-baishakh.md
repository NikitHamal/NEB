@s 1
**Galvanic cell vs electrolytic cell.**

| Galvanic (voltaic) cell | Electrolytic cell |
|---|---|
| Converts chemical energy into electrical energy | Converts electrical energy into chemical energy |
| The cell reaction is spontaneous ($\Delta G<0$) | The reaction is non-spontaneous and is forced by an external source ($\Delta G>0$) |
| Anode is negative, cathode is positive | Anode is positive, cathode is negative |
| Two half-cells in separate vessels joined by a salt bridge | Both electrodes dip in the same electrolyte (one vessel), no salt bridge |
| Example: Daniell cell, dry cell, lead storage battery during discharge | Example: electrolysis of molten NaCl, electroplating, charging a battery |

**Calculation.** The cell is $\text{Cr}\,|\,\text{Cr}^{3+}(0.1\ \text{M})\,||\,\text{Fe}^{2+}(0.01\ \text{M})\,|\,\text{Fe}$. Cr has the lower reduction potential, so it is the anode and $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is reduced at the cathode. Balancing the electrons ($6$ in all):
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Anode: } 2\text{Cr}\to2\text{Cr}^{3+}+6e^- \\ \text{Cathode: } 3\text{Fe}^{2+}+6e^-\to3\text{Fe} \\ \text{Cell: } 2\text{Cr}+3\text{Fe}^{2+}\to2\text{Cr}^{3+}+3\text{Fe},\qquad n = 6\end{gathered}$$
$$E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{cathode}-E^\circ_{anode} = -0.44-(-0.74) = 0.30\ \text{V}$$
Nernst equation at $25^\circ$C:
$$E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell}-\frac{0.0591}{n}\log\frac{[\text{Cr}^{3+}]^2}{[\text{Fe}^{2+}]^3} = 0.30-\frac{0.0591}{6}\log\frac{(0.1)^2}{(0.01)^3}$$
$$\begin{gathered}\frac{(0.1)^2}{(0.01)^3} = \frac{10^{-2}}{10^{-6}} = 10^4 \\ \log10^4 = 4\end{gathered}$$
$$E_{cell} = 0.30-0.00985\times4 = 0.30-0.0394 = 0.2606$$
$$\boxed{E_{cell}\approx0.26\ \text{V}}$$

@s 2
**Henderson equation (acidic buffer).** Consider a buffer of a weak acid HA and its salt $\text{A}^-$ (from NaA). The acid dissociates, $\text{HA}\rightleftharpoons\text{H}^++\text{A}^-$, with
$$K_a = \frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}\ \Rightarrow\ [\text{H}^+] = K_a\,\frac{[\text{HA}]}{[\text{A}^-]}$$
Taking the negative logarithm of both sides,
$$-\log[\text{H}^+] = -\log K_a-\log\frac{[\text{HA}]}{[\text{A}^-]}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{pH} = \text{p}K_a+\log\frac{[\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}$$
In the buffer the salt is fully ionised and supplies almost all of the $\text{A}^-$, while the acid, whose ionisation is suppressed by the common ion, supplies almost all of the HA. Hence $[\text{A}^-]\approx[\text{salt}]$ and $[\text{HA}]\approx[\text{acid}]$:
$$\boxed{\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a+\log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]}}$$

**Calculation.** Moles of each component (the common total volume $10$ ml cancels in the ratio):
$$\begin{gathered}n_{\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}} = 0.2\times6 = 1.2\ \text{mmol} \\ n_{\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}} = 0.2\times4 = 0.8\ \text{mmol}\end{gathered}$$
$$\text{pH} = 4.74+\log\frac{0.8}{1.2} = 4.74+\log0.6667 = 4.74-0.176 = 4.564$$
$$\boxed{\text{pH}\approx4.56}$$

@s 3
**Catalytic promoter.** A substance that is **not itself a catalyst but increases the activity of a catalyst** when added in a small amount. Examples: Mo (or $\text{K}_2\text{O}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) in the Fe catalyst of the Haber process $\text{N}_2+3\text{H}_2\rightleftharpoons2\text{NH}_3$; $\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_3$ in the $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$ catalyst of the water-gas shift reaction; Cu promotes Ni in hydrogenation.

**Adsorption theory of (heterogeneous) catalysis.** It explains the action of **solid catalysts on gaseous reactants**. The surface atoms of the solid are unsaturated and hold the reactant molecules by adsorption. The steps are:

1. **Diffusion** of the reactant molecules to the catalyst surface.
2. **Adsorption** of the reactants on the active sites of the surface (chemisorption); the molecules become concentrated on the surface.
3. **Activation:** adsorbed molecules are weakened (bonds stretched or broken) and the heat of adsorption supplies the energy, so the **activation energy is lowered**.
4. **Surface reaction** between the adjacent activated molecules gives the product, which is still adsorbed.
5. **Desorption** of the product, leaving the active sites free for fresh reactant molecules.

**Example: hydrogenation of ethene on finely divided nickel.**
$$\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2+\text{H}_2\xrightarrow{\text{Ni},\ 150^\circ\text{C}}\text{CH}_3\text{–CH}_3$$
Hydrogen is adsorbed on the Ni surface as atoms ($\text{H}_2\to2\text{H}$ bonded to Ni), ethene is adsorbed through its $\pi$ electrons. A hydrogen atom adds to one carbon (half-hydrogenated state), a second H adds to the other carbon, and ethane (which is only weakly held) leaves the surface.

@s 4
**Formation of the ozone layer.** In the **stratosphere** ($15$-$35$ km), short-wavelength UV radiation ($\lambda<240$ nm) splits oxygen molecules into atoms, which combine with other $\text{O}_2$ molecules in the presence of a third body M:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{O}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}2\text{O} \\ \text{O}+\text{O}_2+\text{M}\to\text{O}_3+\text{M}\end{gathered}$$
Ozone is also decomposed by UV of longer wavelength, $\text{O}_3\xrightarrow{h\nu}\text{O}_2+\text{O}$, and the formation and destruction keep a steady-state ozone concentration (about $300$ Dobson units overall).

**Significance of the ozone layer.** It **absorbs the harmful UV-B (280-315 nm) and all UV-C radiation** of the sun, protecting life from skin cancer, cataract, damage of immunity, loss of crop yield and damage to phytoplankton; it also warms the stratosphere and keeps the atmospheric temperature profile.

**Depletion by CFCs.** Chlorofluorocarbons (freons, e.g. $\text{CCl}_2\text{F}_2$, $\text{CCl}_3\text{F}$), used in refrigerators, air-conditioners, aerosols and foams, are chemically inert in the troposphere, so they drift up to the stratosphere, where UV breaks them to give chlorine atoms, which catalyse the destruction of ozone in a chain reaction:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{CCl}_2\text{F}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}\cdot\text{CClF}_2+\text{Cl}\cdot \\ \text{Cl}\cdot+\text{O}_3\to\text{ClO}\cdot+\text{O}_2 \\ \text{ClO}\cdot+\text{O}\to\text{Cl}\cdot+\text{O}_2 \\ \text{Net: }\text{O}_3+\text{O}\to2\text{O}_2\end{gathered}$$
Chlorine is regenerated, so **one Cl atom can destroy about $10^5$ ozone molecules**. The result is a thinning of the layer, the Antarctic ozone hole. The Montreal Protocol (1987) banned CFCs; HFCs and hydrocarbons are used as substitutes.

@s 5
**Main sources of water pollution.**

1. **Domestic sewage:** human waste, detergents, food wastes (pathogens, organic matter, phosphates).
2. **Industrial effluents:** tanneries, paper and pulp, textile and dyeing, sugar, distilleries, electroplating, mining, which discharge acids, alkalis, heavy metals (Hg, Pb, Cd, Cr), cyanide, oils and organic load.
3. **Agriculture:** run-off with fertilisers (nitrate, phosphate), pesticides and herbicides, animal wastes.
4. **Thermal pollution** from power plants and **radioactive wastes** from nuclear plants and mines.
5. **Oil spills, ships, landfill leachate, urban storm water**, and natural sources (soil erosion, decaying vegetation, geological arsenic and fluoride).

**Control measures.**

1. Treatment of sewage and industrial wastes before discharge: primary (screening, sedimentation), secondary (activated sludge, trickling filter), tertiary (chemical precipitation, adsorption, reverse osmosis, disinfection).
2. Reuse and recycling of water, cleaner production and zero-liquid-discharge technology in industries.
3. Use of organic manure and integrated pest management instead of excess fertiliser and pesticides; buffer strips along rivers.
4. Biodegradable and phosphate-free detergents; proper sanitation and solid-waste management.
5. Cooling ponds for hot effluents; safe disposal of radioactive wastes.
6. Strict laws and effluent standards, regular monitoring of water quality, and public awareness.

@s 6
**Characteristics of inorganic polymers.** The backbone is formed by atoms other than carbon (Si, P, N, S, B, O). They

1. have **high thermal stability** (strong Si-O, P-N bonds) and keep their properties at high temperature;
2. are **chemically inert** and resistant to oxidation, weathering, acids and many solvents;
3. are **flexible** (low glass-transition temperature) and elastomeric in many cases, such as silicones and polyphosphazenes;
4. are **non-flammable** and good electrical insulators (a few are conductors, such as $(\text{SN})_x$);
5. are hard, with high density and high melting point, but are often brittle (glass, silicates, ceramics);
6. repel water (silicones) and have good low-temperature performance.

**Preparation of silicones** (polysiloxanes, $[\text{R}_2\text{SiO}]_n$).

1. *Dimethyldichlorosilane* is made by the direct (Rochow) process: silicon is heated with methyl chloride in the presence of copper at about $300^\circ$C.
$$\text{Si}+2\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}\xrightarrow{\text{Cu},\ 300^\circ\text{C}}(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{SiCl}_2$$
2. *Hydrolysis* gives the silanediol, which is unstable:
$$(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{SiCl}_2+2\text{H}_2\text{O}\to(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{Si(OH)}_2+2\text{HCl}$$
3. *Condensation polymerisation* (loss of water between the OH groups) gives the linear silicone polymer:
$$n\,(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{Si(OH)}_2\to\left[\!-\text{O–Si(CH}_3)_2-\right]_n+n\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
A little $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{SiCl}$ is added to **end-cap and control the chain length** (liquid oils); $\text{CH}_3\text{SiCl}_3$ gives **cross-links** (resins and rubbers).

**Uses of silicones.** Silicone oils and greases as lubricants and hydraulic fluids (stable between $-50$ and $250^\circ$C); silicone rubber for gaskets, seals, wire insulation, medical implants and tubes; sealants and caulks in buildings; water-repellent coatings for fabrics, paper and masonry; anti-foaming agents; electrical insulating varnishes and resins; polishes, cosmetics and heat-resistant paints.

@s 7
**a) Epoxy resin.**

*Preparation.* Condensation of **bisphenol-A** (from phenol and acetone) with **epichlorohydrin** ($\text{ClCH}_2\text{–CH(O)CH}_2$) in the presence of NaOH at about $60$-$70^\circ$C; HCl is lost (taken up by the NaOH), the chain grows and ends with epoxide groups. A prepolymer with an average $n = 0$-$20$ is obtained:
$$n\,\text{HO–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–C(CH}_3)_2\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–OH}+(n+1)\,\text{ClCH}_2\text{CH(O)CH}_2\xrightarrow{\text{NaOH}}\text{epoxy prepolymer}+\text{NaCl}+\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
It is cured (cross-linked) at room temperature by a **hardener**, a polyamine such as $\text{H}_2\text{N(CH}_2)_2\text{NH(CH}_2)_2\text{NH}_2$ (triethylenetetramine) or an acid anhydride.

*Applications.* Strong **adhesives** (Araldite), protective surface coatings and paints (corrosion-resistant), matrix of fibre-glass and carbon-fibre composites (aircraft, boats, sports goods), electrical insulation and encapsulation of electronic components, printed circuit boards, flooring and civil repair of concrete.

**Nylon 6,6.**

*Preparation.* Condensation polymerisation of **adipic acid** and **hexamethylenediamine** (via nylon salt) at about $250^\circ$C under $15$-$20$ atm, eliminating water:
$$n\,\text{HOOC(CH}_2)_4\text{COOH}+n\,\text{H}_2\text{N(CH}_2)_6\text{NH}_2\xrightarrow{250^\circ\text{C}}\left[\!-\text{CO(CH}_2)_4\text{CO–NH(CH}_2)_6\text{NH}-\right]_n+2n\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
*Applications.* Textile fibres (clothes, socks, stockings), ropes, fishing nets, tyre cords, parachutes, carpets, bristles, and moulded engineering parts such as gears and bearings.

**b) Biodegradable and non-biodegradable polymers.**

| Biodegradable (decomposed by micro-organisms) | Non-biodegradable (persist in the environment) |
|---|---|
| Polyhydroxybutyrate-co-valerate (PHBV), polylactic acid (PLA), polycaprolactone (PCL), polyglycolic acid, starch- and cellulose-based plastics, nylon-2-nylon-6 | Polyethylene (PE), polypropylene (PP), polyvinyl chloride (PVC), polystyrene (PS), Teflon (PTFE), nylon-6,6, Bakelite |
| Used for surgical sutures, drug-delivery capsules, packaging, mulch films | Used for pipes, bags, bottles, cables, utensils, fibres |

@s 8
**(a) Colour of the compounds of transition elements.**

Most transition-metal compounds are coloured because their ions have **partly filled $d$ orbitals**. In a compound or a complex the ligands split the five degenerate $d$ orbitals into two sets ($t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ in an octahedral field), with an energy difference $\Delta_o$ that falls in the **visible region**. The ion absorbs light of the energy $h\nu = \Delta_o$ and an electron jumps from the lower to the higher set (**$d$-$d$ transition**); the colour seen is the **complementary** colour of the light absorbed.

- $[\text{Cu(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}$ ($3d^9$) absorbs in the orange-red and appears **blue**; $[\text{Ti(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{3+}$ ($3d^1$) absorbs green-yellow and appears **purple**; $\text{Cr}^{3+}$ ($3d^3$) green or violet; $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ yellow-brown; $\text{Ni}^{2+}$ green; $\text{Co}^{2+}$ pink.
- Ions with **no electrons ($d^0$: $\text{Sc}^{3+}$, $\text{Ti}^{4+}$) or a full $d$ shell ($d^{10}$: $\text{Zn}^{2+}$, $\text{Cu}^+$)** have no $d$-$d$ transition and are **colourless**.
- Some intensely coloured species ($\text{MnO}_4^-$ purple, $\text{CrO}_4^{2-}$ yellow) have **charge-transfer** transitions (ligand to metal), not $d$-$d$.

**(b) Catalytic property of transition elements.**

Transition metals and their compounds are good catalysts because (i) they show **variable oxidation states**, so they can form unstable intermediate compounds by giving or taking electrons and then regenerate (e.g. $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$: $\text{V}^{5+}\rightleftharpoons\text{V}^{4+}$ in the contact process; $\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}$); and (ii) they have **vacant or partly filled $d$ orbitals** and a large, active surface where the reactants are adsorbed and held in the proper orientation (chemisorption), which lowers the activation energy. Examples: Fe in the Haber process, $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ in the contact process, Ni in the hydrogenation of oils, Pt and Pt-Rh in the oxidation of $\text{NH}_3$ (Ostwald process) and in catalytic converters, $\text{MnO}_2$ in the decomposition of $\text{KClO}_3$ or $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$, $\text{TiCl}_4$ in Ziegler-Natta polymerisation.

**Correlation with electronic configuration.**

1. From Sc to Mn, the **maximum oxidation state equals the total number of $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ electrons**: Sc $3+$ ($d^1s^2$), Ti $4$, V $5$, Cr $6$, Mn $7$ (as in $\text{MnO}_4^-$, all of $3d^5 4s^2$). After Mn the maximum state falls (Fe $+6$ rare, Co $+4$, Ni $+4$, Cu $+2$) because the $d$ electrons are increasingly pulled into the nucleus and are held strongly (paired), so fewer can take part in bonding.
2. The common lower states $+2$ result from the loss of only the two $4s$ electrons; higher states involve $3d$ electrons.
3. **Stability of half-filled and fully-filled $d$ subshells:** $\text{Mn}^{2+}$ ($3d^5$) and $\text{Fe}^{3+}$ ($3d^5$) are especially stable ($\text{Fe}^{2+}$ is oxidised easily to $\text{Fe}^{3+}$, whereas $\text{Mn}^{3+}$ is reduced to $\text{Mn}^{2+}$); $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ ($3d^{10}$) and $\text{Cu}^+$ ($3d^{10}$) are stable.
4. The **higher oxidation states are found in the oxides and fluorides** (e.g. $\text{MnO}_4^-$, $\text{CrO}_4^{2-}$, $\text{VO}_4^{3-}$), because the small, electronegative O and F stabilise them; the lower states are ionic and typically basic, the higher states covalent and acidic. Zn has only $+2$ since its $3d$ shell is complete.

@s 10
**a) Definitions.**

- **(i) Chelating ligand.** A ligand that has **two or more donor atoms** and attaches to the same metal ion through two or more coordinate bonds, forming a **ring (chelate)**. Examples: ethylenediamine (en, bidentate), oxalate, EDTA (hexadentate), dimethylglyoxime.
- **(ii) Effective atomic number (EAN).** The **total number of electrons around the central metal atom in a complex**, including those donated by the ligands. Sidgwick rule: stable complexes often have an EAN equal to that of the next noble gas.
$$\text{EAN} = Z-(\text{oxidation number})+2\times(\text{coordination number})$$
For $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-}$: $26-2+2(6) = 36$ (Kr); $[\text{Ni(CO)}_4]$: $28-0+8 = 36$; $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{3+}$: $27-3+12 = 36$; but $[\text{Cu(NH}_3)_4]^{2+}$: $29-2+8 = 35$ (an exception).
- **(iii) Coordination sphere.** The central metal ion together with the ligands directly bonded to it, written **inside the square brackets**, e.g. $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{3+}$ in $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]\text{Cl}_3$. The ions outside the brackets (the counter ions) are in the ionisation sphere.

**Assumptions (postulates) of valence bond theory (VBT).**

1. The central metal ion provides a **number of vacant orbitals equal to its coordination number**; these are $(n-1)d$, $ns$, $np$ (and $nd$) orbitals.
2. The vacant orbitals **hybridise** to give equivalent hybrid orbitals directed in space towards the ligands, and the type of hybridisation fixes the **geometry** ($sp^3$ tetrahedral, $dsp^2$ square planar, $sp^3d^2$ or $d^2sp^3$ octahedral).
3. Each ligand has at least one **filled orbital with a lone pair**; it donates this pair into a hybrid orbital of the metal, forming a **coordinate (dative) $\sigma$ bond** $\text{L}\to\text{M}$.
4. The metal can use its inner $(n-1)d$ orbitals (**inner-orbital** or low-spin complexes, strong-field ligands cause pairing) or the outer $nd$ orbitals (**outer-orbital** or high-spin complexes, weak-field ligands).
5. The **magnetic behaviour** depends on the unpaired electrons: no unpaired electron means diamagnetic; one or more unpaired electrons means paramagnetic, with $\mu=\sqrt{n(n+2)}$ BM. The non-bonding $d$ electrons occupy the remaining $d$ orbitals according to Hund's rule unless forced to pair by the ligand.
6. In some complexes there is back-donation ($\pi$ bonding) from metal to ligand.

**$\text{K}_3[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]$ (potassium hexacyanoferrate(III)).** The complex ion is $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{3-}$: $x-6 = -3$, so $\text{Fe}^{3+}$, $[\text{Ar}]\,3d^5$.

- $\text{CN}^-$ is a **strong-field ligand**, so the five $3d$ electrons are forced to pair in three $3d$ orbitals ($\uparrow\downarrow,\uparrow\downarrow,\uparrow$): **one unpaired electron** remains and **two $3d$ orbitals become vacant**.

@s 12
**Primary explosives.** Explosives that are **extremely sensitive to heat, shock, friction or a spark** and detonate with a very fast, violent explosion ($6$-$8$ km s$^{-1}$). They are used in small quantities as **initiators (detonators and percussion caps)** to set off the less sensitive high explosives. Examples: lead azide $\text{Pb(N}_3)_2$, mercury fulminate $\text{Hg(ONC)}_2$, lead styphnate, diazodinitrophenol.

**Low explosives.** Substances that **burn (deflagrate) rapidly** rather than detonate when ignited, giving a large volume of hot gases with a moderate rate of burning (a few hundred metres per second or less); they **push and propel** and are used as propellants and for blasting in soft rocks. Examples: **black (gun) powder** (75 % $\text{KNO}_3$, 15 % charcoal, 10 % sulphur), smokeless powders (nitrocellulose-based), fireworks, safety fuse. (High explosives such as TNT, TNG, RDX detonate with a velocity of $4$-$9$ km s$^{-1}$.)

**Trinitroglycerol (TNG, nitroglycerine).**

*Preparation.* Glycerol is added **slowly** to a cooled mixture of concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$ and concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($1:1$ or $1:2$) at **$10$-$15^\circ$C** (the nitration is exothermic, so the temperature is controlled). The oily TNG separates on top, is washed with water and sodium carbonate and dried:
$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3+3\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ <15^\circ\text{C}}\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{ONO}_2)_3+3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
*Uses.* Manufacture of **dynamite** (absorbed in kieselguhr, Nobel, 1867), blasting gelatin and gelignite for mining, quarrying and tunnelling, a component of cordite and smokeless powder, and in tiny doses as a **vasodilator** for angina pectoris.

**Trinitrotoluene (TNT).**

*Preparation.* Toluene is nitrated in three stages with a mixture of concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$ and concentrated $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, raising the temperature and the acid strength each time (about $30^\circ$C for mono-, $60^\circ$C for di-, and $100$-$115^\circ$C for tri-):
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_3\xrightarrow{\text{HNO}_3/\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4}\text{CH}_3\text{C}_6\text{H}_2(\text{NO}_2)_3+3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The crude product is washed and recrystallised from alcohol (pale yellow solid, m.p. $81^\circ$C).

*Uses.* Military high explosive (shells, bombs, mines, grenades, as *amatol* with ammonium nitrate); blasting in mines, quarrying and demolition; underwater explosions; and as a standard for measuring the power of other explosives (the "ton of TNT").

**i) Essential requirements of a good paint.**

1. It should **spread easily and uniformly** (suitable viscosity) and form a smooth, thin, uniform film.
2. It should **dry quickly** to a hard, tough film with **good adhesion** to the surface and good covering power (opacity).
3. The film should resist **weather, water, heat, chemicals and corrosion**, and should not crack, peel, blister or chalk.
4. It should have a **lasting, bright colour** and good gloss.
5. It should be **non-toxic, inexpensive**, stable during storage (no settling or skinning), and give good protection to the surface.

**ii) Lubricating oil.** A lubricating oil is a **liquid lubricant** (mineral oil from petroleum, vegetable or animal oil, or synthetic oil) put between two moving surfaces to form a thin film that **reduces friction and wear, removes heat and prevents corrosion**. Its important properties are viscosity, viscosity index, flash and fire points, cloud and pour point, oiliness and acid value.

**Conditions at which solid lubricants are used.** Solid lubricants (graphite, molybdenum disulphide $\text{MoS}_2$, talc, mica, soapstone) are used

1. at **very high temperature** where oils or greases would burn, oxidise or decompose (furnace and kiln parts, hot rolling);
2. under **extremely heavy loads and low speeds**, where an oil film would be squeezed out;
3. in a **vacuum or in space** where oils evaporate, and in the presence of **corrosive chemicals or radiation**;
4. where **oil or grease contamination must be avoided**, e.g. in food, textile, paper and pharmaceutical machines, and where lubrication is difficult or impossible and a dry film is wanted;
5. for **rough surfaces and for running-in** of new engines, or when the surfaces move only intermittently.

@s 14
**i) Conditions for geometrical isomerism.**

1. There must be **restricted rotation** about a bond: a $\text{C=C}$ (or $\text{C=N}$, $\text{N=N}$) double bond, a ring, or a rigid structure.
2. **Each of the two doubly bonded atoms must carry two different groups** ($a\ne b$ on one atom and $c\ne d$ on the other). Otherwise (e.g. $\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CH}_2$, $\text{R}_2\text{C=CHR}'$) there are no geometrical isomers.

**Why is the cis isomer less stable than the trans isomer?** In the cis isomer the two similar (larger) groups lie on the **same side** of the double bond, so there is **steric (van der Waals) repulsion** between them, which raises the energy. In the trans isomer these groups are far apart on opposite sides, so there is no strain and the energy is lower. For example, cis-but-2-ene has a heat of hydrogenation $\approx 120$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ against $\approx 116$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ for the trans isomer, so the trans is about $4$ kJ mol$^{-1}$ more stable. (The cis isomers have higher dipole moments and lower melting points; exceptions occur when intramolecular hydrogen bonding stabilises the cis form, as in maleic acid.)

**ii) Stereoisomers of 3-bromo-2-butanol**, $\text{CH}_3\text{–C}^*\text{H(OH)–C}^*\text{HBr–CH}_3$. **b) S$_\text{N}$1: retention and inversion.** The S$_\text{N}$1 reaction of a chiral halide (e.g. optically active $\text{R}_1\text{R}_2\text{R}_3\text{C–Br}$) takes place in **two steps**: the slow ionisation to a **planar ($sp^2$) carbocation** (the C+ with its three groups in one plane) and the fast attack of the nucleophile. The carbocation is **flat**, so the nucleophile can approach from **either face with almost equal probability**:

- attack from the side **opposite to the leaving group** gives the product with **inversion** of configuration;
- attack from the **same side as the departed Br$^-$** gives the product with **retention**.

The two products form in nearly equal amounts (**racemisation**, usually with a slight excess of inversion because the leaving group still shields its own side as an ion pair), so an optically active halide gives an (almost) optically inactive racemic alcohol.
