@s 1
**Salt bridge.** A salt bridge is a U-tube (or a strip of filter paper) filled with a saturated solution of an inert electrolyte such as KCl, KNO₃ or NH₄NO₃ in agar-agar gel, that joins the two half-cells of a galvanic cell. The ions chosen have nearly equal ionic mobilities (K⁺ and Cl⁻).

**Role.**
1. It completes the internal circuit by allowing ions to migrate between the half-cells.
2. It keeps each half-cell electrically neutral: anions move to the anode compartment to balance the Cd²⁺ formed, cations move to the cathode to replace the ions discharged.
3. It eliminates (reduces to a few mV) the liquid-junction potential between the two solutions, and prevents their mixing.

**(i) Anode and cathode.** The data are oxidation potentials: Cd, $E^\circ_{ox} = 0.40$ V, Ni, $E^\circ_{ox} = 0.24$ V. The metal with the larger oxidation potential (equivalently, the lower reduction potential) loses electrons more readily. Hence:
- **Cd is the anode** (oxidation): $\text{Cd}\to\text{Cd}^{2+} + 2e^-$ (electrons are released here, negative terminal).
- **Ni is the cathode** (reduction): $\text{Ni}^{2+} + 2e^-\to\text{Ni}$ (positive terminal). Its reduction potential is $-0.24$ V and that of Cd is $-0.40$ V.

**(ii) Cell representation:**
$$\text{Cd(s)}\,|\,\text{Cd}^{2+}(0.01\ \text{M})\,\|\,\text{Ni}^{2+}(0.1\ \text{M})\,|\,\text{Ni(s)}$$
Cell reaction: $\text{Cd} + \text{Ni}^{2+}\to\text{Cd}^{2+} + \text{Ni}$, $n = 2$.

**(iii) EMF.** $E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{ox}(\text{anode}) + E^\circ_{red}(\text{cathode}) = 0.40 + (-0.24) = 0.16$ V. By the Nernst equation at 25°C:
$$E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell} - \frac{0.0591}{n}\log\frac{[\text{Cd}^{2+}]}{[\text{Ni}^{2+}]} = 0.16 - \frac{0.0591}{2}\log\frac{0.01}{0.1}$$
$$= 0.16 - 0.02955(-1) = 0.16 + 0.02955$$
$$\boxed{E_{cell} = 0.19\ \text{V}}$$

**Or. Polarisation** is the deviation of an electrode potential from its equilibrium (reversible) value when a net current flows through the electrode. **Overpotential** ($\eta$) is the extra potential (beyond the thermodynamic value) that must be applied to drive an electrode reaction at a given rate:
$$\eta = E_{applied} - E_{eq}$$
(Activation overpotential arises from slow electron transfer, concentration overpotential from slow mass transport, and resistance overpotential from the ohmic drop. Examples: the hydrogen overpotential is about 1 V on Pb or Hg but almost zero on Pt.)

**Tafel equation.** For large $\eta$ (activation control), the Butler–Volmer equation reduces to
$$\boxed{\eta = a + b\log j,\qquad b = \frac{2.303RT}{\alpha nF},\qquad a = -\frac{2.303RT}{\alpha nF}\log j_0}$$
where $j$ is the current density, $j_0$ the exchange current density and $\alpha$ the transfer coefficient. A **Tafel plot** of $\eta$ against $\log j$ is a straight line; the slope is $b$ (the Tafel slope) and the intercept gives $j_0$.

*Applications:* (i) determination of the exchange current density and transfer coefficient, i.e. the kinetics of electrode reactions; (ii) estimation of corrosion rates ($i_{corr}$) and testing of corrosion inhibitors; (iii) comparison of electrocatalysts for fuel cells and water electrolysis (a lower Tafel slope is better); (iv) battery and electroplating design and the choice of electrode materials.

@s 2
**Acidic buffer** is a solution of a weak acid and its salt with a strong base, for example $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$. The acid is only slightly ionised, and the salt is fully ionised, so the solution contains a large reserve of both $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ (acid reserve) and $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$ (base reserve).
- **On adding a little strong acid** ($\text{H}^+$): the added $\text{H}^+$ is removed by the acetate ions, $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+\to\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$. The weak acid formed ionises very little, so $[\text{H}^+]$ and the pH hardly change.
- **On adding a little strong base** ($\text{OH}^-$): the $\text{OH}^-$ is removed by the acid, $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{OH}^-\to\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}$, so again the pH is almost unchanged.

Quantitatively, from $K_a = \dfrac{[\text{H}^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]} \\ (\text{Henderson})\end{gathered}$$
The ratio salt/acid changes only slightly when a small amount of $\text{H}^+$ or $\text{OH}^-$ is added.

**Numerical.** Moles of HCl $= 0.100\ \text{L}\times0.2\ \text{M} = 20$ mmol. Moles of $\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$ $= 0.050\times0.5 = 25$ mmol. They react: $\text{NH}_4\text{OH} + \text{HCl}\to\text{NH}_4\text{Cl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.
$$\begin{gathered}\text{NH}_4\text{OH left} = 25 - 20 = 5\ \text{mmol} \\ \text{NH}_4\text{Cl formed} = 20\ \text{mmol}\end{gathered}$$
The mixture is a **basic buffer** (weak base + its salt). The volume is 150 mL, which cancels in the ratio:
$$\text{p}K_b = -\log(1.8\times10^{-5}) = 4.745$$
$$\text{pOH} = \text{p}K_b + \log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{base}]} = 4.745 + \log\frac{20}{5} = 4.745 + 0.602 = 5.347$$
$$\boxed{\text{pH} = 14 - 5.347 = 8.65}$$

@s 3
**Homogeneous catalysis.** The catalyst and the reactants are in the **same phase**. Examples: (i) $\text{NO}$ catalysing $2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2\to2\text{SO}_3$ (lead chamber process, all gases); (ii) $\text{H}^+$ catalysing the hydrolysis of an ester, $\text{CH}_3\text{COOCH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{CH}_3\text{OH}$ (all liquid). Advantages: high selectivity and activity at mild conditions; disadvantage: difficult separation and recycling.

**Heterogeneous catalysis.** The catalyst is in a **different phase** from the reactants (usually a solid catalyst with gaseous or liquid reactants). Examples: (i) Fe in the Haber process, $\text{N}_2(g) + 3\text{H}_2(g)\to2\text{NH}_3(g)$; (ii) $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ in the contact process; (iii) Ni in hydrogenation of oils; (iv) Pt/Rh in catalytic converters. The reaction occurs on the surface (adsorption, surface reaction, desorption); the catalyst is easily separated and reused.

**Selection criteria of a catalyst.**
1. **Activity:** it must increase the rate sufficiently (large turnover frequency) at the working temperature and pressure.
2. **Selectivity:** it should give mainly the desired product with few side reactions.
3. **Stability and lifetime:** it must resist sintering and deactivation, i.e. remain active over many cycles, and be regenerable.
4. **Resistance to poisoning** by impurities (e.g. S, CO, Pb).
5. **Mechanical strength and surface area:** a porous support with high surface area and strength to survive the reactor conditions.
6. **Cost, availability and environmental safety**, and ease of separation from the product.

@s 4
**Chromatography.** It is a physical technique for separating the components of a mixture by distributing them between two phases: a **stationary phase** (a solid or a liquid held on a solid) and a **mobile phase** (a liquid or a gas) which moves through or over the stationary phase. Components are separated because they differ in their affinity for the two phases.

**Thin layer chromatography (TLC).**
*Principle:* adsorption chromatography. The stationary phase is a thin layer (0.2–0.5 mm) of adsorbent (silica gel or alumina) spread on a glass, plastic or aluminium plate. A drop of the sample is spotted near the bottom (the base line) and the plate is placed upright in a closed chamber with a shallow pool of the solvent (mobile phase). By capillary action the solvent rises up the plate and carries the components with it; components that are weakly adsorbed (more soluble in the solvent) travel faster than the strongly adsorbed ones, which gives separate spots. The plate is dried and the spots are located (UV lamp, iodine vapour or spray reagents). The characteristic parameter is
$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the spot}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front}}\ (<1)$$
*Applications:* (i) checking the purity of a compound and following the progress of a reaction; (ii) identifying compounds by comparing $R_f$ with standards; (iii) analysis of amino acids, drugs, dyes, pesticides, plant pigments and food additives; (iv) choosing the solvent for column chromatography, and small-scale preparative separation.

**Or. IR spectroscopy: principle.** Molecules absorb infrared radiation ($4000$–$400\ \text{cm}^{-1}$) when the frequency of the radiation equals a vibrational frequency of the bonds **and** the vibration changes the dipole moment of the molecule (a selection rule). The absorption excites the bonds from the ground to the first excited vibrational level (stretching or bending). The bond vibration frequency is
$$\begin{gathered}\bar\nu = \frac{1}{2\pi c}\sqrt{\frac k\mu} \\ \mu = \frac{m_1m_2}{m_1 + m_2}\end{gathered}$$
so a stronger bond ($k$ larger) or lighter atoms absorb at a higher wavenumber. A plot of percentage transmittance against wavenumber is the IR spectrum.

**Functional-group region ($4000$–$1500\ \text{cm}^{-1}$).** The bands here arise from stretching of specific bonds and are characteristic of the functional group, so it is used to identify the groups present: O–H (3600–3200, broad), N–H (3500–3300), C–H (3300–2850), C≡C and C≡N (2260–2100), C=O (1800–1650, strong), C=C (1680–1600).

**Fingerprint region ($1500$–$400\ \text{cm}^{-1}$).** It has a complex pattern of many overlapping bands from C–C, C–O, C–N stretching and bending of the whole molecular skeleton. The pattern is unique for each compound (as a fingerprint is for a person), so it is used for the final identification: two samples are the same only if their fingerprint regions match exactly.

@s 5
**Chelate.** When a ligand with two or more donor atoms (a polydentate ligand such as ethylenediamine (en), oxalate or EDTA) binds to the same central metal ion through two or more donor sites, it forms a ring structure, and the complex is called a **chelate** (the ligand is a chelating agent). Chelates (five- and six-membered rings) are much more stable than complexes of monodentate ligands (the chelate effect). Example: $[\text{Co(en)}_3]^{3+}$, $[\text{Fe(C}_2\text{O}_4)_3]^{3-}$.

**VBT for $[\text{CoF}_6]^{3-}$.** Co is in the +3 state: $\text{Co}^{3+}$: $[\text{Ar}]3d^6$. $\text{F}^-$ is a weak-field ligand and does not force pairing, so the $3d$ electrons stay as they are (4 unpaired electrons: $t_{2g}^4e_g^2$). The six ligand pairs occupy one $4s$, three $4p$ and two **outer** $4d$ orbitals:
$$\begin{array}{l}\text{Co}^{3+}\ (3d^6):\ \ [\uparrow\downarrow][\uparrow][\uparrow][\uparrow][\uparrow]\ \ \underline{4s}\ \ \underline{4p\,4p\,4p}\ \ \underline{4d\,4d}\\ \text{hybridisation: }sp^3d^2\ \text{(outer orbital)}\end{array}$$
- **Geometry: octahedral** ($sp^3d^2$).
- **Magnetism: paramagnetic** with 4 unpaired electrons, $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = \sqrt{24} = 4.9$ BM. It is a high-spin, outer-orbital complex.

*If $[\text{CoF}_4]^-$ is meant:* $\text{Co}^{3+}\ (3d^6)$ with four $\text{F}^-$ uses one $4s$ and three $4p$ orbitals, $sp^3$ hybridisation, so the shape is **tetrahedral**, and the $3d^6$ electrons (4 unpaired) make it **paramagnetic**.

**Formulae.**
(i) Triamminetriaquachromium(III) chloride: $\boxed{[\text{Cr(NH}_3)_3(\text{H}_2\text{O})_3]\text{Cl}_3}$.
(ii) Potassium trioxalatoaluminate(III): $\boxed{\text{K}_3[\text{Al(C}_2\text{O}_4)_3]}$.

@s 6
**(a) Postulates of crystal field theory (CFT).**
1. The metal–ligand bond is purely **electrostatic**: ligands are treated as negative point charges (or dipoles) and the metal ion as a positive point charge.
2. The five $d$ orbitals of the free ion are degenerate. When the ligands approach, the orbitals pointing towards them are repelled more strongly than those pointing between them, so the degeneracy is **lifted** (splitting).
3. The splitting pattern depends on the geometry (octahedral, tetrahedral, square planar) and its size ($\Delta$) on the ligand strength (spectrochemical series), the metal and the oxidation state.
4. The $d$ electrons occupy the split levels by Hund's rule and pairing energy, giving high-spin or low-spin complexes; there is no overlap or covalent bond between metal and ligand orbitals.

**Splitting in an octahedral field.** Six ligands lie along $\pm x$, $\pm y$, $\pm z$. The $e_g$ orbitals ($d_{x^2-y^2}$, $d_{z^2}$) point directly at the ligands and are **raised**; the $t_{2g}$ orbitals ($d_{xy}$, $d_{yz}$, $d_{xz}$) point between the ligands and are **lowered**, relative to the barycentre (the average energy of the five orbitals, preserved):
$$\begin{gathered}E(e_g) = +0.6\Delta_o \\ E(t_{2g}) = -0.4\Delta_o\end{gathered}$$
$\Delta_o$ is the crystal field splitting energy. $\Delta_o<P$ (weak field, e.g. $\text{F}^-$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$): high-spin; $\Delta_o>P$ (strong field, e.g. $\text{CN}^-$, CO): low-spin. The d–d transitions $t_{2g}\to e_g$ give the colour of the complex.

@s 7
**(a) BOD and COD.**
- **BOD (biochemical oxygen demand)** is the amount of dissolved oxygen needed by aerobic micro-organisms to decompose the biodegradable organic matter in a sample of water, usually in 5 days at 20°C (BOD$_5$), in mg O₂/L.
- **COD (chemical oxygen demand)** is the amount of oxygen needed to oxidise all the organic (and oxidisable inorganic) matter in the water chemically, using a strong oxidant such as acidified $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$, in mg O₂/L. COD $\ge$ BOD; it takes only 2–3 hours.

*Consequences with reference to water pollution:* a high BOD or COD means a large load of organic pollutant. Micro-organisms use up the dissolved oxygen, so the dissolved oxygen (DO) falls; fish and other aquatic life die (below about 4 mg/L), anaerobic decomposition begins producing foul gases ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{NH}_3$), the water gets turbid and smelly, and it becomes unfit for drinking, irrigation and recreation. Drinking water has BOD < 1 mg/L; treated sewage should have BOD < 30 mg/L before discharge.

**Steps in the industrial treatment of waste water.**
1. **Preliminary treatment:** screens remove large solids; grit chambers settle sand; oil and grease are skimmed off; equalisation tanks and neutralisation adjust the flow and pH.
2. **Primary treatment:** sedimentation (with coagulants such as alum) settles suspended solids as sludge in clarifiers, removing about 60% of the suspended solids and 30% of the BOD.
3. **Secondary (biological) treatment:** aerobic micro-organisms oxidise dissolved organic matter by the activated-sludge process, trickling filter or oxidation pond; BOD is reduced by 85–95%.
4. **Tertiary (advanced) treatment:** removal of nutrients (N, P), heavy metals and traces of organics by chemical precipitation, adsorption on activated carbon, ion exchange, membrane filtration (reverse osmosis), and disinfection (chlorination, ozone or UV).
5. **Sludge treatment and disposal:** thickening, anaerobic digestion (biogas), drying and safe disposal or reuse.

**(b) Solid waste segregation and management.** *Segregation* means separating waste at the source into (i) **biodegradable** (kitchen and garden waste), (ii) **recyclable** (paper, glass, metal, plastic), (iii) **hazardous** (batteries, chemicals, medical, e-waste) and (iv) inert/residual waste. It makes recycling and composting easier and cheaper and keeps hazardous waste out of landfill.

*Management follows the waste hierarchy:* **reduce → reuse → recycle → recover (compost, biogas, energy) → safe landfill.** Methods are composting and vermicomposting of organic waste, biogas production by anaerobic digestion, recycling of paper/plastic/metal/glass, incineration with heat recovery, and sanitary landfill with leachate and gas collection for the residue.

*Challenges for waste to energy (WtE):*
- mixed waste with high moisture and low calorific value (as in Nepal, ~60–70% organic) needs pre-sorting and drying;
- high capital and operating costs, and a need for skilled operation and a stable tipping fee or tariff;
- toxic emissions (dioxins, furans, $\text{NO}_x$, $\text{SO}_2$, heavy metals, fly ash) need expensive gas cleaning and ash disposal;
- lack of segregation at source, weak collection, and public opposition ("not in my backyard");
- lack of policy and regulation; competition with recycling and composting.

**Classification of nanomaterials by dimension.** Nanomaterials have at least one dimension in 1–100 nm. By the number of dimensions that are **not** confined to the nanoscale:

| Class | Nano-dimensions | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| 0-D | all three (quantum dots, nanoparticles) | gold nanoparticles, quantum dots (CdSe), fullerene $C_{60}$ |
| 1-D | two (wires, rods, tubes) | carbon nanotubes, nanowires (ZnO, Si), nanorods, nanofibres |
| 2-D | one (sheets, films) | graphene, MXenes, $\text{MoS}_2$ sheets, thin films |
| 3-D | none confined, but nano-structured bulk | nanocrystalline solids, nanocomposites, aerogels, dendrimers |

**Chemical vapour deposition (CVD).** CVD makes thin films, nanotubes, nanowires and nanoparticles by chemical reaction of **volatile precursor gases at a heated substrate**, which deposit a solid product.

*Steps:*
1. Precursors (e.g. $\text{SiH}_4$, $\text{CH}_4$/$\text{C}_2\text{H}_2$ with a carrier gas such as Ar or $\text{H}_2$) are fed to the reaction chamber (a quartz tube in a furnace) at controlled flow.
2. The gases diffuse to the heated substrate (300–1200°C, often with a metal catalyst particle such as Fe, Ni or Cu for nanotubes or graphene).
3. They are adsorbed on the surface, where they decompose or react (thermal, plasma or laser activation).
4. Atoms nucleate and grow as film, nanotube or nanowire; volatile by-products desorb and are carried away by the gas stream and pumped out.

*Examples:* $\text{SiH}_4\xrightarrow{600^\circ\text{C}}\text{Si(s)} + 2\text{H}_2$; $\text{CH}_4\xrightarrow{1000^\circ\text{C, Cu}}\text{C(graphene)} + 2\text{H}_2$.

*Types:* thermal CVD, plasma-enhanced CVD (PECVD, lower temperature), low-pressure (LPCVD), metal-organic CVD (MOCVD).
*Advantages:* uniform, pure, dense and conformal films with controlled thickness, over large areas, at good growth rates. *Limitations:* high temperature, toxic and hazardous precursors and by-products, and expensive equipment.

@s 9
**(a) Addition vs condensation polymers.**

| Addition polymer | Condensation polymer |
|---|---|
| Formed by repeated addition of unsaturated monomers (C=C), no loss of atoms | Formed by repeated reaction between two functional groups with loss of a small molecule ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$, HCl, $\text{NH}_3$) |
| Monomer needs a double/triple bond | Monomer needs two or more functional groups (–OH, –COOH, –NH₂) |
| Repeat unit has the same composition as the monomer, so $M_{polymer} = nM_{monomer}$ | Repeat unit differs from monomers; $M$ is less than the sum of the monomers |
| Chain reaction (initiation, propagation, termination) | Step-wise reaction, with the molar mass rising slowly |
| Examples: polyethylene, PVC, PMMA, polystyrene | Examples: nylon-6,6, polyester (PET), Bakelite, polyurethane |

**Cationic polymerisation.** It is a chain-growth addition polymerisation started by an electrophile (a cation), and it works for monomers with electron-donating groups that stabilise the carbocation (isobutylene, vinyl ethers, styrene). *Example:* polymerisation of isobutylene (2-methylpropene) with $\text{BF}_3$ + trace $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (co-catalyst) in a non-polar solvent at low temperature to form polyisobutylene.
1. **Initiation:** $\text{BF}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\to\text{H}^+[\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-$. The proton adds to the monomer to form a tertiary carbocation:
$$\text{H}^+ + \text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)_2\to\text{(CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+\ [\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-$$
2. **Propagation:** the carbocation attacks the next monomer, regenerating a carbocation at the chain end:
$$\text{(CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+ + n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)_2\to\text{(CH}_3)_3\text{C}-[\text{CH}_2\text{C(CH}_3)_2]_n^{\ }\!-\text{CH}_2\text{C}^+(\text{CH}_3)_2$$
3. **Termination/chain transfer:** by loss of a proton from the chain end to the counter-ion (forming a terminal double bond and regenerating the catalyst), or by combination with the counter-ion, $\text{...C}^+ + [\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-\to\text{...C–OH} + \text{BF}_3$.
The product, polyisobutylene (butyl rubber, with ~2% isoprene) is used for inner tubes, sealants and adhesives.

**(b) Carbon fibre.** Carbon fibre is a long thin fibre (5–10 µm diameter) made up of more than 90% carbon atoms in aligned graphite-like microcrystals along the fibre axis. It is very strong and stiff and light (high strength-to-weight ratio), with low thermal expansion, and made by heating polyacrylonitrile (PAN) fibres: stabilisation in air at 200–300°C, carbonisation in an inert gas at 1000–1500°C, and optionally graphitisation at 2000–3000°C. It is used in aircraft, sports goods, wind turbine blades, racing cars and as reinforcement of plastics (CFRP).

**Kevlar (aramid).** *Preparation:* a condensation polymer of terephthaloyl chloride and *p*-phenylenediamine in a polar solvent (N-methylpyrrolidone with $\text{CaCl}_2$) at low temperature, with the loss of HCl:
$$n\,\text{H}_2\text{N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–NH}_2 + n\,\text{ClOC–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–COCl}\to[\text{–NH–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–NH–CO–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–CO–}]_n + 2n\,\text{HCl}$$
The rigid chains are strongly hydrogen-bonded sheets. *Uses:* bullet-proof vests and helmets, tyre cord, ropes and cables, fire-protective clothing, brake linings and composite aircraft parts (five times stronger than steel by weight).

**Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA, Perspex, acrylic glass).** *Preparation:* free-radical addition polymerisation of methyl methacrylate (using peroxide/AIBN, bulk or suspension):
$$n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)\text{COOCH}_3\xrightarrow{\text{peroxide, heat}}[\text{–CH}_2\text{–C(CH}_3)(\text{COOCH}_3)\text{–}]_n$$
*Uses:* transparent glass substitute (aircraft windows, aquaria, skylights), lenses, light covers and signboards, bone cement and dental plates, and car tail-light covers.

**Or. (a) Chitin.** A natural polysaccharide, a linear polymer of N-acetyl-D-glucosamine units joined by $\beta$-(1→4) glycosidic linkages (similar to cellulose with –OH at C-2 replaced by –NHCOCH₃). It is the structural material in the exoskeletons of crabs, shrimps and insects and in fungal cell walls. Its deacetylation with hot NaOH gives **chitosan**. It is biodegradable, non-toxic and biocompatible and is used in wound dressings and sutures, drug delivery, water treatment (adsorbs heavy metals and dyes), cosmetics and as a food additive and in agriculture.

**(b) Polythiazyl, $(\text{SN})_x$.** An inorganic polymer of alternating sulfur and nitrogen atoms, $[\text{–S≡N–}]_x$. It is made by solid-state polymerisation of disulfur dinitride ($\text{S}_2\text{N}_2$), itself obtained by heating $\text{S}_4\text{N}_4$ over silver wool at about 300°C: $\text{S}_4\text{N}_4\xrightarrow{\text{Ag}}2\text{S}_2\text{N}_2\to(\text{SN})_x$. It is a golden lustrous fibrous solid, and is the first known **polymeric conductor** (metallic, with conductivity $\sim10^3\ \Omega^{-1}\text{cm}^{-1}$) and becomes a superconductor below 0.3 K. It is used in research on conducting polymers and electronic devices.

**(a) Plastic explosive.** A plastic explosive is a malleable, putty-like high explosive that can be moulded by hand into any shape, made of a high explosive (RDX, PETN) mixed with a plasticiser (oil, wax, polyisobutylene). Example: C-4 and Semtex.

**Nitroglycerine (glyceryl trinitrate, GTN).** *Preparation:* nitration of glycerol with a mixture of conc. nitric acid and conc. sulfuric acid (the latter absorbs the water formed), at a low temperature below 10–15°C (the reaction is highly exothermic):
$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3 + 3\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ <15^\circ\text{C}}\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{ONO}_2)_3 + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The oily product is washed with water and sodium carbonate and separated. It is shock-sensitive, so it is made safe by absorbing it on kieselguhr (dynamite, Nobel 1867).

*Decomposition:* $4\,\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{ONO}_2)_3\to12\,\text{CO}_2 + 10\,\text{H}_2\text{O} + 6\,\text{N}_2 + \text{O}_2$ (a large volume of hot gas).
*Applications:* dynamite and blasting gelatin in mining, quarrying and tunnelling, a component of smokeless propellants (cordite, double-base powder), and in medicine as a vasodilator for angina.

**(b) Lubricants.** A lubricant is a substance placed between moving surfaces to reduce friction and wear.
*Types:*
1. **Liquid (oils):** mineral oils (petroleum), vegetable and animal oils (castor, palm), synthetic oils (silicones, polyglycols).
2. **Semi-solid (greases):** lubricating oil thickened with metal soap (Ca, Li or Na soaps).
3. **Solid lubricants:** graphite, $\text{MoS}_2$, talc, PTFE, for high temperature or loads.
4. **Gaseous lubricants:** air, nitrogen, used for very high speed.
*Functions:* reduce friction and wear between surfaces; cool the machine by removing heat; seal the gaps (piston rings); prevent corrosion and rust; remove dirt and wear debris; reduce noise and energy loss and increase the machine life.
