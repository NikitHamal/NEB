@s 1
**(a) Factors affecting the strength of structural timber.**

1. **Species and density.** Strength and stiffness rise with density (heavy hardwoods such as Sal are 2 to 3 times stronger than softwoods such as pine). IS 883 groups species into A, B, C by strength.
2. **Moisture content.** Below the fibre saturation point (about 25 to 30 %) strength rises as the wood dries (about 4 to 6 % per 1 % drop in moisture); above it, strength is constant. Seasoned timber (12 to 15 %) is used for design.
3. **Defects:** knots (the most important; reduce tension and bending strength), slope of grain (cross grain), shakes, splits, checks, decay, insect attack, pitch pockets, warping and wane. The grade (Select, Grade 1, Grade 2) is set by the permissible limit of defects.
4. **Direction of grain.** Timber is orthotropic: strength is highest parallel to the grain, and far lower perpendicular to it (tension perpendicular to grain is only about 1/30 of that along the grain).
5. **Duration of load.** Strength under long-term load is about 60 % of that under a short-term test (creep and fatigue); IS 883 gives the factor for permanent, medium and short-term loads.
6. **Size and shape of the member**, rate of growth (annual ring width), temperature (strength falls at high temperature), preservative treatments, fire exposure, and the position of the piece in the tree (heartwood is stronger and more durable than sapwood).

**(b) Cross laminated timber (CLT) and glued laminated timber (glulam).**

| Basis | Glued laminated timber (glulam) | Cross laminated timber (CLT) |
|---|---|---|
| Layer arrangement | Many boards (laminations, 20 to 45 mm) glued with the grain of **all** layers parallel to the member's length | Layers (3, 5, 7, ...) of boards glued with the grain of **adjacent layers at 90°** (crosswise) |
| Behaviour | One-way (beam-like), strong along the length, like a large sawn section | Two-way (plate-like), strong in both directions in the plane of the panel |
| Form | Straight, curved or tapered beams, columns, arches, portals | Large flat panels (walls, floors, roofs), up to 3 m by 16 m |
| Shrinkage and swelling | As solid timber along the grain | Cross layers restrain each other, so the dimensional change in the plane of the panel is very small |
| Typical use | Long-span beams, arches, halls, bridges | Multi-storey timber buildings, diaphragms, shear walls |
| Odd number of layers | Not required | Required for symmetry |

Both allow large sections to be made from small pieces, with defects spread out and dispersed, and kiln-dried timber used, so the strength is more reliable and higher than for sawn timber.

@s 2
**(a) Behaviour of bolted joints in timber.** In a bolted timber joint the load is passed from one member to another by bearing of the bolt on the wood and bending of the bolt. The load-slip behaviour is:

- At small load, the wood bears on the bolt shank and the slip is small and nearly elastic. The bolt hole is made 1.5 mm larger (clearance), so some initial slip occurs before the bolts bear. The bolts do not all take the load equally (the end bolts carry more).
- As load increases, the wood under the bolt crushes (embedment) and the bolt bends (hinging), making the joint more flexible. Failure modes of the dowel-type joint are: (i) crushing of timber in the bearing zone, (ii) bending yield of the bolt with one or two plastic hinges, (iii) splitting of the timber along the grain from the bolt (row shear, block shear, end tear-out when the end or edge distance is small), and (iv) tension in the member net section.
- **Strength** depends on bolt diameter $d$, the thickness of the members $t$, the **angle of load to the grain** (parallel-to-grain bearing is the strongest; perpendicular is about 1/3, given by the Hankinson formula $N=\dfrac{PQ}{P\sin^2\theta+Q\cos^2\theta}$), the species group and the number of shear planes.
- **Geometry rules** (IS 883) to avoid splitting: spacing along the grain $\ge7d$ (parallel), end distance $\ge7d$ (tension member) or $5d$ (compression), edge distance $\ge1.5d$, spacing between rows across the grain $\ge4d$.
- Bolts are used with washers (to avoid crushing of wood under the nut), tightened when the wood is seasoned, and re-tightened after shrinkage. The slip of a bolted joint is larger than that of a nailed or glued joint, and design strength is reduced for the number of bolts in a row.

**(b) Types of structural timber joints.**

1. **Splice joints** (to extend a member in length): *lap (halved) joint*, *scarf joint* (with the ends cut at a slope of 1:4 to 1:6 and held by bolts or straps), and *fish-plate (butt) joint* with timber or steel side plates, bolted.
2. **Tension joints**: tabled scarf and bolted fish-plated joints, with the net area checked.
3. **Corner and angle joints**: *mortise and tenon joint* (the tenon, about 1/3 of the member thickness, fits into the mortise, secured with a peg or glue), *halving* (cross-halved lap), *dovetail joint*, *bridle joint*.
4. **Joints in trusses**: the *notched (birdsmouth) joint* at the heel (rafter on tie beam), *bolted joints with steel gusset plates*, *strap and stirrup joints*, and *king-post/queen-post* connections.
5. **Joints by fasteners**: *nailed*, *screwed*, *bolted*, *dowelled* joints, and joints with *connectors*: toothed-plate, shear-plate and split-ring connectors which carry the load by bearing on a large area of timber.
6. **Glued joints** (finger joint, glued scarf, plywood gussets): high stiffness, used in glulam and factory fabrication.

@s 4
**Masonry bonds.** A *bond* is the arrangement of bricks or stones in courses so that vertical joints in successive courses do not coincide ("break joints") and the wall acts as one unit. Terms: *stretcher* (brick laid with its length along the face), *header* (laid with its end showing, to tie the wall across its thickness), *closer* (a brick cut lengthwise or a quarter bat, placed next to the quoin header so that the vertical joints break), *queen closer*, *bat*, *quoin*.

1. **Stretcher bond.** All bricks are stretchers: used only for half-brick (115 mm) partitions and the outer leaf of cavity walls. Each course is shifted half a brick.
2. **Header bond.** All bricks are headers: used for curved walls and one-brick (230 mm) thick circular work.
3. **English bond.** Alternate courses of stretchers and headers, with a *queen closer* after the quoin header in each header course. Strongest bond: it is used for load-bearing walls of 230 mm and more.
4. **Flemish bond.** Each course has alternate headers and stretchers, a header being centred on the stretcher below. Better appearance, a little weaker than English bond, and uses fewer full bricks (a closer is placed beside the quoin header).
5. **Others:** garden-wall bond (English garden wall: 3 stretchers to 1 header per course; Flemish garden wall), *raking bond*, *dutch bond* and *facing bond*, *zigzag (herring-bone) bond* for floors and paving.

**(b) Procedure to design a masonry wall with eccentricity (IS 1905).** When the load is applied at an eccentricity $e$ from the wall centre line (a slab or beam bearing on part of the wall, a corbel, lateral loads, an eccentrically connected floor), the wall is designed for the axial load plus the bending moment $Pe$.

1. **Loads.** Find the vertical loads on the wall at the section (slab reactions, upper walls, self-weight) and the eccentricities. Load from a slab (or beam) bearing on the wall is taken to act at **one-third of the bearing depth from the face of the wall**, and axial loads from the wall above on its centre line. For a lateral wind or earthquake load, the moment is added.
2. **Resultant eccentricity.** $e=\dfrac{\sum P_ie_i}{\sum P_i}$ and the eccentricity ratio $e/t$ (code limit: $e\le t/3$ in the compression zone: so that no tension develops on the section, $e\le t/6$ if tensile stress is not permitted).
3. **Slenderness ratio** $SR=h_{ef}/t_{ef}$ with $h_{ef}$ and $t_{ef}$ from the end restraints and stiffening (maximum 27 for load-bearing walls; $\le 20$ with hollow units).
4. **Basic compressive stress $f_b$** from the unit strength and mortar grade (Table), and the multipliers: *stress reduction factor $k_s$* from the table with both $SR$ and $e/t$ (for $e/t=1/6$, $k_s$ is much smaller than for the axial case), the *area factor* $k_a=0.7+1.5A$ for $A<0.2$ m$^2$, and the *shape factor* $k_p$.
5. **Permissible stress** $f_{perm}=f_bk_sk_ak_p$ (with a 25 % increase when wind or seismic loads act with the vertical loads).
6. **Check:** $f_{act}=P/A\le f_{perm}$. When the table $k_s$ includes the eccentricity (the code's method) no further moment check is needed. As an alternative, the combined stress is checked using the interaction form: $\dfrac{P/A}{f_{perm,axial}}+\dfrac{M/Z}{f_{b,perm}}\le1$, and tension $\dfrac{P}{A}-\dfrac{M}{Z}\ge-f_t$ (flexural tension $f_t\le0.07$ N/mm$^2$ in 1:6 mortar).
7. **Revise the section** (thickness, bond, stronger unit or mortar, stiffening by piers or pilasters, or reinforcement) if the check fails. Verify the *bearing stress under the concentrated load* ($\le1.25$ to $1.5f_b$) and provide a *bearing plate* or pad stone if needed.

@s 6
**(a) In-plane and out-of-plane behaviour of masonry walls under lateral loads.** Masonry is strong in compression but has low tensile and shear strength. A wall resists lateral load in two ways, depending on the direction of the load relative to the plane of the wall.

*In-plane behaviour* (load along the length of the wall, as a shear wall). The wall acts as a vertical cantilever deep beam. Failure modes: (1) **sliding** along a bed-joint when the lateral force exceeds the friction plus cohesion; (2) **diagonal tension (shear) cracking:** typical X-cracks along the diagonals, which are stepped through the mortar joints or go through the units when the principal tensile stress exceeds the diagonal tensile strength (the main failure in earthquakes); (3) **rocking (flexural)** with toe crushing and horizontal cracks at the base for slender walls with low axial load; (4) crushing of the toe. The strength depends on the wall length (the longer walls are stiffer and attract more force), the axial load (it raises both friction and strength), the aspect ratio, the openings (which weaken the piers) and the mortar.

*Out-of-plane behaviour* (load perpendicular to the wall). The wall acts as a **slab or plate in bending** (one-way vertical, or two-way when supported on cross walls), or as a cantilever if unsupported at the top. Tension appears on the face away from the load, where the flexural tensile strength is very small, and the wall cracks horizontally (bed joint) at mid-height or at the base and **overturns**, falling as a rigid block. The resistance depends on the **thickness and the height-to-thickness ratio**, the support at the top and the sides (floor diaphragms, cross walls, bands), and the vertical compression. Out-of-plane failure is the main cause of collapse in unreinforced masonry buildings (the walls fall outward, then the roof falls), so these walls need ties to the floors and roof and to the cross walls, and limits on the height-thickness ratio and unsupported length.

**(b) Lateral load resisting elements in masonry structures.**

1. **Shear walls** (the load-bearing masonry walls, in both directions).
2. **Cross (transverse) walls and buttresses/pilasters** (support out-of-plane walls).
3. **Horizontal bands** (plinth, lintel, roof and gable bands) and **vertical reinforcement** at corners and openings.
4. **Floor and roof diaphragms** (RCC slab or a braced timber floor) that distribute the lateral load to the walls.
5. **Reinforced or confined masonry elements** (tie-columns and tie-beams), frame infill, and bracing.

**Horizontal bands (seismic bands).** A horizontal band is a continuous reinforced concrete (or timber) beam, running round all the walls at a particular level, tying them together so that the building acts as a box and the long walls are supported against out-of-plane bending by the cross walls. Types: **plinth band** (at the plinth level, where the foundation may settle unequally, essential on soft soil), **lintel band** (at the lintel level of the doors and windows, also acting as the lintel, the most important one), **roof band** (below the roof or floor slab, for the pitched roofs) and the **gable band**. Details: the band depth is at least 75 mm (RCC 1:2:4 concrete, M20) with two to four longitudinal bars of 8 to 12 mm (tied by 6 mm stirrups at 150 mm), the reinforcement is continuous round corners and splice lengths of 60 times the bar diameter; for a span or a wall of 230 mm thickness the band is 230 mm wide, with reinforcement of $2\phi10$ (for a span up to 4 m) and increased for a longer span. A band is needed in every storey; in a one-storey building the lintel and roof bands can be combined.

@s 7
**(a) Design principles of earthquake-resistant masonry construction** (NBC 201/202, IS 4326, IS 13828).

1. **Regular, simple, symmetrical plan and elevation.** Rectangular plans with the centre of mass near the centre of stiffness (to avoid torsion). Avoid L, T and U shapes, or divide them with seismic gaps. Keep the length-to-width ratio $\le3$ and no abrupt changes in stiffness or mass between storeys.
2. **Box action.** The walls, floors and the roof are tied together with bands, so that the building acts as a rigid box. Walls in two orthogonal directions are connected by **bonded corners** (toothing, or with dowel bars at 500 mm) and cross walls.
3. **Limits of the building:** height (maximum 3 storeys for a masonry building in cement mortar in zone V, 2 storeys for mud mortar), storey height $\le3$ m, wall height to thickness $h/t\le12$ to 16 and wall length between cross walls $\le$ 6 m (or limited by $l/t\le$ 40).
4. **Openings.** Small and centrally located: the total width of the openings $\le$ 50 % of the wall length in a single storey building (45 % and 42 % in 2 and 3 storeys); the distance from a corner $\ge600$ mm; the pier width $\ge$ 600 mm (1/2 of the opening height).
5. **Materials and quality.** Strong bricks (class 7.5 or more) and stone, cement mortar (1:4 to 1:6, never mud mortar in higher storeys), full filled joints and wetting, with wall thickness $\ge230$ mm, and lap for toothing.
6. **Seismic bands and vertical reinforcement** at the plinth, lintel, roof and gable levels and at the corners, the T-junctions and the sides of the openings (steel bars anchored in the foundation and the roof band) to resist the horizontal and vertical bending.
7. **Foundation:** uniform and firm foundation on the same type of soil and at one level, a plinth band on soft soil, with the avoidance of masonry on loose or liquefiable soils.
8. **Light roof and floors**, tied with the walls; a rigid diaphragm (RCC slab or a braced one) that delivers the lateral load to the walls in proportion to their stiffness; avoid heavy parapets, cantilevers and projections.
9. **Strength and ductility.** Providing ductile reinforced elements; the structure is designed for the seismic coefficient $\alpha_h=ZIS_a/2Rg$ ($Z$ zone factor) and the base shear in each direction is shared by the shear walls in proportion to their stiffness.

**(b) Strengthening (retrofitting) measures to sustain earthquake effects.** For existing buildings, the measures aim to restore or add strength, stiffness, ductility and box action:

1. **Repair of cracks:** grouting with cement slurry or epoxy for fine cracks, stitching or replacing the bricks over wide cracks, and filling of the voids with grout (pressure injection) to restore the monolithic action.
2. **Jacketing (ferrocement or reinforced plaster overlay):** a wire mesh or a layer of weld mesh on one or both faces of the wall, tied across with 6 mm steel ties (at 450 mm), and covered with 25 to 50 mm of cement mortar or micro-concrete (shotcrete). It improves the shear and flexural strength, ductility and the resistance to out-of-plane failure. *Steel strips or bars (vertical and horizontal) in grooves of the mortar joints* give similar results (**mesh and strip reinforcement**).
3. **Adding bands and ties:** inserting RCC (or steel) *horizontal bands* at the lintel and roof levels when they are missing, *vertical ties (steel rods) at the corners*, and *through-bolts* connecting the walls (cross-ties and tie rods with plates) to tie the roof or floor to the walls.
4. **Strengthening of the openings:** surrounding the large doors and windows with RC or steel frames, reducing the size of the openings or infilling them.
5. **Improving the floors and roofs** (adding diaphragm action by braces, a concrete topping and anchoring the beams to the walls).
6. **Adding buttresses, shear walls or new RC frames**, or new reinforced walls, to increase the stiffness and the lateral resistance, with enlargement of the foundation (and underpinning) when it is weak.
7. **Fibre-reinforced polymer (FRP) wraps and strips, and base isolation** for important buildings, and **reducing the weight** (removing the heavy parapets and roof), **chimneys** and the **parapet stays** (anchoring).

@s 8
**(a) Importance of testing masonry elements.** Masonry strength depends on the unit, the mortar, the workmanship and the bond, not only on the unit; there is a large scatter. Testing is needed to:

1. **Know the actual strength** (compressive, shear, flexural bond) of the units, mortar, prisms and wall panels, as a basis for the *design values* (permissible stress by IS 1905 and the characteristic strength).
2. **Quality control** of the materials supplied (the crushing strength, water absorption (should be $<20$ %), efflorescence, dimension and the shape of bricks and blocks, according to IS 3495) and of the construction workmanship (mortar cube tests: IS 2250).
3. **Verify the assumptions of the analysis**, calibrate numerical models and compare the structure with the code requirements (seismic design needs the shear strength).
4. **Assess existing and damaged buildings** (in-situ tests: flat-jack tests, shove test, and the rebound hammer), to decide the retrofitting; to **select the economical materials** (compare the products) and do research on new types of masonry (CSEB, fly-ash bricks, AAC blocks).

**(b) Diagonal shear (diagonal compression) test.** The test finds the **diagonal tensile (shear) strength** $f_{dt}$ of masonry (ASTM E519, RILEM LUMB6). It is the key parameter for the in-plane (seismic) shear strength.

*Specimen:* a square wall panel (wallette) of at least $1.2\times1.2$ m (or 600 mm minimum for small specimens), built with the same units, mortar and workmanship (age 28 days) as the structure, and of the full thickness.

*Setup:* the panel is placed in a loading frame with one diagonal **vertical**, with steel shoes (loading plates with a curved or angled seat, bedded in gypsum or high-strength mortar) on two opposite corners. A compression load $P$ is applied through a hydraulic jack on the top shoe and reacts on the bottom shoe. Under this load the other (horizontal) diagonal is in tension and the panel behaves as if under pure shear along the loaded diagonal. Dial gauges or LVDTs across both diagonals record the shortening and the extension (to get the shear strain $\gamma$ and the shear modulus $G$).

*Procedure:* load is increased at a constant rate until the panel splits along the loaded diagonal, with a stepped crack along the mortar joints or through the units. The peak load $P_{max}$ is recorded.

*Results:*

$$\begin{gathered}f_{dt}=\frac{0.707\,P_{max}}{A_n} \\ (\text{ASTM E519}) \\ A_n=\frac{w+h}{2}\,t\,n\end{gathered}$$

where $w,h,t$ are the panel width, height and thickness, and $n$ the percentage of the net area (1 for solid masonry). Alternatively, the shear stress is $\tau=P/(\sqrt2A)$ and, with the pure-shear assumption, the principal tensile stress is $\sigma_t=\tau$ (for the RILEM method, $f_t=0.5\,P_{max}/A_n$). The shear modulus is $G=\tau/\gamma$ with $\gamma=(\Delta_v+\Delta_h)/g$ ($g$ is the gauge length). The test also gives the failure mode and the cracking pattern. The *shear strength with precompression* (the Mohr-Coulomb parameters, cohesion and friction coefficient) is found by combining it with the triplet shear test.
