@s 1
**(a) Degree of freedom.** The number of independent coordinates (independent ways of moving or storing energy) needed to specify completely the position and configuration of a system. A monoatomic gas atom can only translate along $x$, $y$ and $z$, so its translational degrees of freedom are $\boxed{3}$. (By equipartition each carries $\tfrac12kT$ per molecule, giving $\tfrac32kT$.)

**(b) Role of helium.** The lasing atoms are neon, but a discharge excites neon inefficiently. The helium atoms (He : Ne $\approx$ 10 : 1) are raised by electron impact to the long-lived metastable levels $2^3S$ (19.8 eV) and $2^1S$ (20.6 eV). These almost coincide with the neon $2s$ and $3s$ levels, so on collision the excitation energy is transferred to neon. Helium is thus the **pumping agent** that produces the **population inversion** in neon.

**(c) Electric dipole and dipole moment.** An electric dipole is a pair of equal and opposite charges $+q$ and $-q$ separated by a small distance $2a$. The **dipole moment** is the vector
$$\vec p = q\,(2\vec a)$$
of magnitude $p = q\times$ (separation), directed from $-q$ to $+q$; unit C m.

**(d) Eddy currents.** When the magnetic flux through a bulk piece of conductor changes (the conductor moves in a field, or the field varies), Faraday's law induces emfs in the conductor. Since the metal provides closed paths, these emfs drive circulating currents (**eddy currents**) in planes perpendicular to the field. By Lenz's law, they flow so as to oppose the change of flux that produces them. They cause heating ($I^2R$ loss), and are used in induction furnaces, magnetic braking and damping.

@s 2
**(a)** A uniform rod of length $L = 1$ m pivoted at one end swings as a physical pendulum. Moment of inertia about the pivot: $I = \tfrac13ML^2$; distance from pivot to centre of mass: $h = L/2$.
$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{I}{Mgh}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{ML^2/3}{Mg\,L/2}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2L}{3g}}$$
$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2(1)}{3(9.8)}} = \boxed{1.64\ \text{s}}$$
(It swings like a simple pendulum of length $2L/3 = 0.667$ m.)

**(b)** Temperature $T = 273 + 273 = 546$ K. A diatomic molecule has 3 translational and 2 rotational degrees of freedom at this temperature (vibration is not excited), each with $\tfrac12RT$ per mole.
- Translational part: $\dfrac32RT = \dfrac32(8.31)(546) = 6.81\times10^3$ J.
- Rotational part: $RT = 4.54\times10^3$ J.
$$\boxed{E_{total} = \frac52RT = \frac52(8.31)(546) = 1.13\times10^4\ \text{J}\approx11.3\ \text{kJ}}$$
(The translational kinetic energy alone is 6.81 kJ.)

**(c)** For a non-relativistic particle $p = h/\lambda$ and $K = \dfrac{p^2}{2m} = \dfrac{h^2}{2m\lambda^2}$. With $m_n = 1.675\times10^{-27}$ kg and $\lambda = 0.1\ \text{nm} = 10^{-10}$ m:
$$K = \frac{(6.626\times10^{-34})^2}{2(1.675\times10^{-27})(10^{-10})^2} = \boxed{1.31\times10^{-20}\ \text{J}\approx0.082\ \text{eV}}$$
(Such a "thermal" neutron has about the energy of $kT$ at room temperature, $0.025$ eV, which is why thermal neutrons diffract from crystals.)

**(d) Maxwell's equations** (free space, $\rho$ and $\vec J$ the free charge and current densities):

| Law | Differential form | Integral form |
|---|---|---|
| Gauss (electric) | $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho$ | $\oint\vec D\cdot d\vec S = q$ |
| Gauss (magnetic) | $\nabla\cdot\vec B = 0$ | $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec S = 0$ |
| Faraday | $\nabla\times\vec E = -\dfrac{\partial\vec B}{\partial t}$ | $\oint\vec E\cdot d\vec l = -\dfrac{d\Phi_B}{dt}$ |
| Ampère–Maxwell | $\nabla\times\vec H = \vec J + \dfrac{\partial\vec D}{\partial t}$ | $\oint\vec H\cdot d\vec l = I + \dfrac{d\Phi_D}{dt}$ |

with $\vec D = \varepsilon\vec E$ and $\vec B = \mu\vec H$. The integral forms follow from the differential ones by the divergence and Stokes theorems.

@s 3
**Bar pendulum.** A bar of mass $M$ suspended at a point $S$ at distance $h$ from its centre of gravity $G$ has radius of gyration $k$ about $G$. About $S$, $I = M(k^2 + h^2)$, and the period is
$$\begin{gathered}T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{k^2 + h^2}{gh}} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac Lg} \\ L = h + \frac{k^2}{h}\end{gathered}$$
The centre of oscillation $O$ lies on the line $SG$ beyond $G$, at distance $SO = L$ from $S$, i.e. at distance $\dfrac{k^2}{h}$ from $G$.

**Minimum time period.** $T$ is least when $L$ is least ($g$ and $k$ are constants):
$$\frac{dL}{dh} = 1 - \frac{k^2}{h^2} = 0\ \Rightarrow\ h = k$$
$$\frac{d^2L}{dh^2} = \frac{2k^2}{h^3}>0\ \text{at }h = k\quad(\text{a minimum})$$
So the period is minimum when the distance of the point of suspension from the centre of gravity equals the radius of gyration, $h = k$. Then
$$\begin{gathered}L_{\min} = k + \frac{k^2}{k} = 2k \\ T_{\min} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2k}{g}}\end{gathered}$$
The distance between the point of suspension and the point of oscillation is $SO = L_{\min} = 2k$, so
$$\boxed{k = \frac{SO}{2}}$$
the radius of gyration is **half the distance between the point of suspension and the point of oscillation**.

**Or. Equal distances from the C.G.** With suspension at distance $l_1 = h$ from $G$, the centre of oscillation is at distance
$$l_2 = L - l_1 = \frac{k^2}{l_1}\ \Rightarrow\ l_1l_2 = k^2$$
The period is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{(l_1 + l_2)/g}$. For minimum $T$ we need minimum $l_1 + l_2$ at fixed product $l_1l_2 = k^2$. Using $\dfrac{dT}{dl_1} = 0$:
$$\frac{d}{dl_1}\left(l_1 + \frac{k^2}{l_1}\right) = 1 - \frac{k^2}{l_1^2} = 0\ \Rightarrow\ l_1 = k\ \Rightarrow\ l_2 = \frac{k^2}{l_1} = k$$
$$\boxed{l_1 = l_2 = k}$$
(Equivalently, by the AM–GM inequality $l_1 + l_2\ge2\sqrt{l_1l_2} = 2k$, with equality only if $l_1 = l_2$.) Hence at minimum time period the points of suspension and oscillation lie at **equal distances** $k$ on opposite sides of the centre of gravity, and $T_{\min} = 2\pi\sqrt{2k/g}$.

@s 5
**Assumptions of Einstein's theory.**
1. Each atom is an independent 3-D harmonic oscillator (equivalent to three linear oscillators); a mole has $3N_A$ oscillators.
2. All oscillators vibrate with the **same frequency** $\nu_E$.
3. Their energy is **quantised**, $\varepsilon_n = nh\nu_E$, $n = 0,1,2,\ldots$ (zero-point energy omitted).
4. The oscillators are in thermal equilibrium at $T$ and obey Boltzmann statistics, $P_n\propto e^{-nh\nu/kT}$.

**Derivation.** The mean energy of one oscillator is, with $x = h\nu_E/kT$,
$$\bar\varepsilon = \frac{\sum_nnh\nu e^{-nx}}{\sum_ne^{-nx}} = -h\nu\frac{d}{dx}\ln\left(\frac1{1 - e^{-x}}\right) = \frac{h\nu_E}{e^{h\nu_E/kT} - 1}$$
The internal energy of one mole is $U = 3N_A\bar\varepsilon$, so the molar heat capacity is
$$C_V = \frac{dU}{dT} = 3N_Ak\left(\frac{h\nu_E}{kT}\right)^2\frac{e^{h\nu_E/kT}}{\left(e^{h\nu_E/kT} - 1\right)^2}$$
$$\boxed{C_V = 3R\left(\frac{\theta_E}{T}\right)^2\frac{e^{\theta_E/T}}{\left(e^{\theta_E/T} - 1\right)^2},\qquad\theta_E = \frac{h\nu_E}{k}}$$
**High temperature** ($T\gg\theta_E$): $e^{\theta_E/T}\approx1 + \theta_E/T$ so $C_V\to3R\approx25$ J mol⁻¹K⁻¹ (5.97 cal mol⁻¹K⁻¹): the **Dulong–Petit law**.

**Low temperature** ($T\ll\theta_E$): $e^{\theta_E/T}\gg1$ and
$$C_V\approx3R\left(\frac{\theta_E}{T}\right)^2e^{-\theta_E/T}\to0\quad(T\to0)$$
The heat capacity vanishes at absolute zero, as observed, which classical theory could not explain. (Experimentally $C_V\propto T^3$, slower than Einstein's exponential fall; Debye's refinement with a frequency spectrum fixes this.)

**Or. Van der Waals equation.** The ideal gas law $PV = RT$ neglects the size of molecules and the forces between them.

*Correction for volume.* Molecules are not points; each excludes the others from a part of the space. The free volume available for the molecular motion is $(V - b)$, where $b$ is the **co-volume**, four times the actual volume of the molecules in one mole.

*Correction for pressure.* A molecule in the interior is attracted equally from all sides. A molecule about to strike the wall is pulled **inward** by the molecules behind it, so it hits the wall with less momentum and the measured pressure $P$ is lower than the ideal pressure $P_i$. The inward pull is proportional to the density of molecules near the wall ($\propto1/V$) and to the density of those pulling ($\propto1/V$), so
$$P_i = P + \frac{a}{V^2}$$
($a$ measures the attraction; $a/V^2$ is the *internal pressure*.) Applying $P_i(V - b) = RT$ for one mole gives
$$\boxed{\left(P + \frac{a}{V^2}\right)(V - b) = RT}$$
and for $\mu$ moles, $\left(P + \dfrac{\mu^2a}{V^2}\right)(V - \mu b) = \mu RT$. At the critical point, comparing the cubic form $V^3 - \left(b + \dfrac{RT}{P}\right)V^2 + \dfrac aPV - \dfrac{ab}P = 0$ with $(V - V_c)^3 = 0$ gives $V_c = 3b$, $P_c = \dfrac{a}{27b^2}$ and $T_c = \dfrac{8a}{27Rb}$.

@s 6
**(a) Circle of least confusion.** A lens forms images of different colours at different points on the axis (chromatic aberration). The violet focus is nearest the lens and the red focus is farthest. Between them, the bundle of rays has a cross-section of smallest size: the smallest circular patch, where the image of a point is least blurred. This is the **circle of least confusion**.

**Proof (independence of focal length).** Let a lens of aperture $D$ receive a parallel beam (object at a large distance). Let $f_v$ and $f_r$ be the focal lengths for violet and red and $f$ the mean (yellow) one. The dispersive power is
$$\omega = \frac{\mu_v - \mu_r}{\mu_y - 1}\ \Rightarrow\ f_r - f_v = \omega f$$
since $f\propto\dfrac1{\mu - 1}$ gives $\dfrac{f_r - f_v}{f} = \dfrac{\mu_v - \mu_r}{\mu_y - 1}$.

The marginal rays are at height $D/2$ at the lens. The violet marginal ray, after crossing the axis at $F_V$, and the red marginal ray, still converging towards $F_R$, are at equal and opposite heights at a plane distance $x$ from the lens where
$$\frac D2\left(1 - \frac x{f_r}\right) = -\frac D2\left(1 - \frac x{f_v}\right)\ \Rightarrow\ x = \frac{2f_rf_v}{f_r + f_v}$$
The radius of the circle there is
$$\frac d2 = \frac D2\left(1 - \frac{x}{f_r}\right) = \frac D2\left(1 - \frac{2f_v}{f_r + f_v}\right) = \frac D2\cdot\frac{f_r - f_v}{f_r + f_v}$$
$$\begin{gathered}d = D\,\frac{f_r - f_v}{f_r + f_v} = D\,\frac{\omega f}{2f} \\ (f_r + f_v\approx2f)\end{gathered}$$
$$\boxed{d = \frac{\omega D}{2}}$$
The diameter of the circle of least confusion depends only on the dispersive power $\omega$ of the glass and the aperture $D$; the focal length has cancelled. It is therefore **independent of the focal length of the lens**.

**(b) Newton's rings, two wavelengths.** $\lambda_1 = 6.0\times10^{-5}$ cm, $\lambda_2 = 5.9\times10^{-5}$ cm, $R = 90$ cm. For dark rings, $D_n^2 = 4n\lambda R$. The $n$th dark ring of $\lambda_1$ coincides with the $(n + 1)$th of $\lambda_2$:

$$D_{59}^2 = 4(59)(6.0\times10^{-5})(90) = 1.274\ \text{cm}^2$$

**(d) Double refraction.** A beam of unpolarised light entering an anisotropic crystal (calcite, quartz) is split into two plane-polarised rays with mutually perpendicular vibrations: the **ordinary ray** ($o$, obeys Snell's law, speed independent of direction) and the **extraordinary ray** ($e$, does not obey Snell's law, speed depends on direction). Along the optic axis both travel with the same speed.

**Production of polarised light.** Plane-polarised light of amplitude $A$ falls normally on a crystal plate of thickness $t$ cut parallel to the optic axis. Let the vibration make an angle $\phi$ with the optic axis. Inside the plate the two components travel with different speeds and with the same frequency:
$$\begin{gathered}E_e = A\cos\phi\ \cos\omega t\ (=x) \\ E_o = A\sin\phi\ \cos(\omega t + \delta)\ (=y)\end{gathered}$$
The phase difference after the plate is
$$\delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mu_o - \mu_e)t$$
Eliminating $t$: with $a = A\cos\phi$ and $b = A\sin\phi$,
$$\boxed{\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{2xy}{ab}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta}$$
- **Linear (plane) polarisation:** $\delta = 0$ or $\pi$ (e.g. a half-wave plate, $(\mu_o - \mu_e)t = \lambda/2$, or $t = 0$). The equation reduces to $\left(\dfrac xa\mp\dfrac yb\right)^2 = 0$, i.e. $y = \pm\dfrac bax$: a straight line.
- **Circular polarisation:** $\delta = \dfrac\pi2$ and $a = b$ ($\phi = 45^\circ$). It gives $x^2 + y^2 = A^2/2$: a circle. Use a **quarter-wave plate**, $t = \dfrac{\lambda}{4(\mu_o - \mu_e)}$.
- **Elliptical polarisation:** $\delta = \dfrac\pi2$ with $a\ne b$ ($\phi\ne45^\circ$) gives $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$, an ellipse with axes along $x$ and $y$; for any other $\delta$ the ellipse is tilted. This is the general case.

**(a) Axial field of a short quadrupole.** Take the linear quadrupole $+q$ at $z = -d$, $-2q$ at the origin, and $+q$ at $z = +d$. At the axial point $P$ at distance $r$ from the centre ($r>d$) the three fields point along the axis:
$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac1{(r - d)^2} - \frac2{r^2} + \frac1{(r + d)^2}\right]$$
Combine the first and third terms: $\dfrac1{(r - d)^2} + \dfrac1{(r + d)^2} = \dfrac{2(r^2 + d^2)}{(r^2 - d^2)^2}$. Then
$$E = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2\left[r^2(r^2 + d^2) - (r^2 - d^2)^2\right]}{r^2(r^2 - d^2)^2} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2d^2(3r^2 - d^2)}{r^2(r^2 - d^2)^2}$$
For a short quadrupole $r\gg d$: $3r^2 - d^2\approx3r^2$, $(r^2 - d^2)^2\approx r^4$:
$$\begin{gathered}\boxed{E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{6qd^2}{r^4} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{3Q}{r^4}} \\ Q = 2qd^2\end{gathered}$$
The field is directed along the axis (away from the quadrupole for $q>0$) and decreases as $1/r^4$. It agrees with $E = -dV/dr$ for $V = \dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}$.

**Or. Field of a charged ring.** A ring of radius $R$ carries charge $Q$ uniformly. At the axial point at distance $y$ from the centre, an element $dq$ is at distance $s = \sqrt{R^2 + y^2}$, and its field is $\dfrac{dq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0s^2}$. The components perpendicular to the axis cancel by symmetry; the axial component is $dE\cos\phi$ with $\cos\phi = y/s$:
$$E = \int\frac{dq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0s^2}\frac ys = \frac{y}{4\pi\varepsilon_0s^3}\int dq$$
$$\boxed{E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Qy}{(R^2 + y^2)^{3/2}}}$$
**Position of maximum field.** $E = 0$ at $y = 0$ and as $y\to\infty$, so it has a maximum in between:
$$\frac{dE}{dy} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac{1}{(R^2 + y^2)^{3/2}} - \frac{3y^2}{(R^2 + y^2)^{5/2}}\right] = 0\ \Rightarrow\ R^2 + y^2 = 3y^2$$
$$\boxed{y = \pm\frac{R}{\sqrt2}}$$
and the maximum field is
$$E_{\max} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{R/\sqrt2}{(3R^2/2)^{3/2}} = \frac{Q}{6\sqrt3\,\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}$$
(The second derivative is negative there, so it is a maximum.)

$$B = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(13.3)(0.069)}{2} = \boxed{5.8\times10^{-7}\ \text{T}}$$
This is about 0.58 µT, the largest value of the field, at the edge of the plates.

@s 9
In vacuum the electric and magnetic fields of a plane wave are related by $E_0 = cB_0$.

**(a) Amplitude of the magnetic field:**
$$B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{3}{3\times10^8} = \boxed{1.0\times10^{-8}\ \text{T}}$$
**(b) Intensity** (average power per unit area, the magnitude of the average Poynting vector):
$$I = \frac12\varepsilon_0cE_0^2 = \frac12(8.854\times10^{-12})(3\times10^8)(3)^2 = \boxed{1.2\times10^{-2}\ \text{W m}^{-2}}$$
Check with $I = \dfrac{E_0B_0}{2\mu_0} = \dfrac{3\times10^{-8}}{2(4\pi\times10^{-7})} = 1.19\times10^{-2}$ W/m² ✓. The frequency ($5\times10^{14}$ Hz, wavelength 600 nm, visible light) is not needed.

@s 10
**Energy of a particle in a 1-D infinite well.** Let the potential be $V = 0$ for $0<x<L$ and $V = \infty$ for $x\le0$ and $x\ge L$. The particle cannot be outside, so $\psi = 0$ there, and $\psi(0) = \psi(L) = 0$ by continuity.

Inside the well the time-independent Schrödinger equation is
$$\begin{gathered}-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\psi\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + k^2\psi = 0 \\ k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\end{gathered}$$
$$\psi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx$$
$\psi(0) = 0$ gives $B = 0$. $\psi(L) = 0$ gives $A\sin kL = 0\Rightarrow kL = n\pi$, i.e. $k = \dfrac{n\pi}{L}$, $n = 1, 2, 3,\ldots$ ($n = 0$ would give $\psi\equiv0$.) Hence
$$\boxed{E_n = \frac{\hbar^2k^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2} = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2}}$$
Energies are quantised, and $E_n = n^2E_1$: $E_1$, $4E_1$, $9E_1,\ldots$ with the **zero-point energy** $E_1 = \dfrac{h^2}{8mL^2}>0$.

**Normalisation:** $\displaystyle\int_0^LA^2\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}dx = A^2\frac L2 = 1\Rightarrow A = \sqrt{\frac2L}$:
$$\boxed{\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac2L}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L}}$$
**Graphs.** $\psi_1$ has no node inside the well and a single maximum at $L/2$. $\psi_2$ has one node at $L/2$. $\psi_3$ has two nodes at $L/3$ and $2L/3$. $\psi_n$ has $n - 1$ nodes. The probability density $|\psi_n|^2$ is not uniform; for $n = 1$ the particle is most likely found at the centre.
