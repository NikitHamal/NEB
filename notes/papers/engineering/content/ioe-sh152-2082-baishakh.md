@s 1
**(a) Critical coefficient.** The critical coefficient is the dimensionless ratio
$$K_c = \frac{RT_c}{P_cV_c}$$
built from the critical temperature, pressure and molar volume. For a van der Waals gas ($V_c = 3b$, $P_c = a/27b^2$, $T_c = 8a/27Rb$) it equals $\boxed{8/3 = 2.67}$, the same for every gas. Experimental values for real gases are larger, about 3.4–4.6 (e.g. $\approx3.6$ for CO₂).

**(b) Angle of acceptance.** It is the maximum angle $\theta_0$ (to the fibre axis) at which light can enter the end of an optical fibre and still be guided by total internal reflection at the core–cladding boundary:
$$\theta_0 = \sin^{-1}\left(\frac{\sqrt{n_1^2 - n_2^2}}{n_0}\right)$$
where $n_1$ is the core index, $n_2$ the cladding index and $n_0$ the index of the outside medium ($n_0 = 1$ for air).

**(c)** The electric field is the negative gradient of the potential:
$$\vec E = -\nabla V = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\hat i - \frac{\partial V}{\partial y}\hat j - \frac{\partial V}{\partial z}\hat k$$
In one dimension $E = -\dfrac{dV}{dr}$; the field points in the direction of the fastest decrease of potential.

**(d)** A cyclotron accelerates **charged particles that are heavy enough to stay non-relativistic**: protons, deuterons, $\alpha$-particles and positive ions. Neutral particles (neutrons) cannot be accelerated, since there is no force from $E$ or $B$; electrons are unsuitable, since their small mass makes them relativistic at low energy and they drop out of resonance (the resonance frequency $qB/2\pi m$ depends on the mass).

@s 2
**(a)** For a uniform stick of length $l = 1$ m pivoted at a point $S$ at distance $h$ from the centre of gravity $G$, the radius of gyration about $G$ is
$$k^2 = \frac{l^2}{12}\ \Rightarrow\ k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{12}} = 0.289\ \text{m}$$
The period is $T = 2\pi\sqrt{L/g}$ with equivalent length $L = h + \dfrac{k^2}{h}$. $T$ is least when $L$ is least:
$$\begin{gathered}\frac{dL}{dh} = 1 - \frac{k^2}{h^2} = 0\ \Rightarrow\ h = k \\ L_{\min} = k + k = 2k\end{gathered}$$
$$\boxed{L_{\min} = 2k = \frac{l}{\sqrt3} = 0.577\ \text{m}}$$
So the stick behaves as a simple pendulum of length 0.577 m with the least period $T_{\min} = 2\pi\sqrt{2k/g} = 1.52$ s, when pivoted 0.289 m from its centre (about 21 cm from an end).

**(b) Clapeyron's equation:** $\dfrac{dP}{dT} = \dfrac{L}{T(V_2 - V_1)}$, so for a small change $dT = \dfrac{T(V_2 - V_1)}{L}dP$. Per gram of water:
- $T = 100^\circ\text{C} = 373$ K,
- $V_2 - V_1 = 1677 - 1 = 1676\ \text{cm}^3 = 1.676\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$,
- $L = 540\ \text{cal/g} = 540\times4.186 = 2260\ \text{J/g}$,
- $dP = 1\ \text{atm} = 1.013\times10^5\ \text{Pa}$.
$$dT = \frac{(373)(1.676\times10^{-3})(1.013\times10^5)}{2260} = \boxed{28\ \text{K}}$$
The boiling point rises by about $28^\circ$C, to roughly $128^\circ$C. (The linear estimate is approximate over this large pressure change; the true boiling point of water at 2 atm is about 120°C.)

**(c) Normalisation.** The wave function $\psi(\vec r, t)$ has no direct meaning, but $|\psi|^2 = \psi^*\psi$ is the probability density: $|\psi|^2dV$ is the probability of finding the particle in the volume element $dV$. Since the particle must be somewhere in space, the total probability is 1:
$$\boxed{\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\psi|^2\,dV = 1}$$
A wave function satisfying this is **normalised**. If a solution $\phi$ of the Schrödinger equation gives $\int|\phi|^2dV = N\ne1$ (the equation is linear, so $\phi$ can be multiplied by any constant), then $\psi = \phi/\sqrt N$ is normalised, and $A = 1/\sqrt N$ is the **normalisation constant**. A wave function must be finite, single-valued and continuous, and for normalisation $\psi\to0$ as $x\to\pm\infty$.
*Example:* for $\psi = A\sin\dfrac{n\pi x}{a}$ in $0<x<a$, $A^2\dfrac a2 = 1$, so $A = \sqrt{2/a}$.

**(d) Charge conservation from Maxwell's equations.** Ampère–Maxwell law: $\nabla\times\vec H = \vec J + \dfrac{\partial\vec D}{\partial t}$. Take the divergence; since $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec H) = 0$,
$$0 = \nabla\cdot\vec J + \frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\nabla\cdot\vec D)$$
Using Gauss' law $\nabla\cdot\vec D = \rho$:
$$\boxed{\nabla\cdot\vec J + \frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = 0}$$
This is the equation of continuity (charge conservation): the net outflow of current from a region equals the decrease of the charge inside it, so the total charge of an isolated system is constant.

@s 3
**Torsional pendulum is SHM.** A body (moment of inertia $I$ about the wire) is hung from a wire of length $l$ and radius $r$. If the body is twisted through a small angle $\theta$, the wire is twisted and produces a restoring couple proportional to $\theta$ (within the elastic limit):
$$\begin{gathered}\tau = -C\theta \\ C = \frac{\pi\eta r^4}{2l}\end{gathered}$$
where $C$ is the torsional constant (couple per unit twist) and $\eta$ the modulus of rigidity. By the rotational form of Newton's law, $I\ddot\theta = -C\theta$:
$$\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} + \frac CI\theta = 0$$
This has the form $\ddot\theta + \omega^2\theta = 0$: the angular acceleration is proportional to, and opposite to, the angular displacement. Hence the motion is **simple harmonic** (angular SHM) with
$$\boxed{\omega = \sqrt{\frac CI},\qquad T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac IC} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2lI}{\pi\eta r^4}}}$$

**Determination of the modulus of rigidity.** From the period, $\eta = \dfrac{8\pi lI}{r^4T^2}$.
1. Clamp the upper end of the wire, attach a disc (or a bar) at its lower end, and measure $l$ with a metre scale and the radius $r$ with a screw gauge (average of several readings).
2. Twist the disc through a small angle, release it, and find the time of 20 oscillations: $T_0$ for the disc alone.
3. Put a ring (or two identical cylinders) of known moment of inertia $I_r$ on the disc and find the new period $T_1$.
4. $T_0^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2I_0}{C}$ and $T_1^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2(I_0 + I_r)}{C}$. Subtracting eliminates the unknown $I_0$:
$$C = \frac{4\pi^2I_r}{T_1^2 - T_0^2}\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\eta = \frac{2lC}{\pi r^4} = \frac{8\pi lI_r}{r^4\left(T_1^2 - T_0^2\right)}}$$
Repeating with different wire lengths and plotting $T^2$ against $l$ (a straight line through the origin) gives a better average.

**Or. Time period and an unknown moment of inertia.** The period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{I/C}$ was derived above. Because the same wire (same $C$) is used, $T^2\propto I$.
1. Suspend a body of known moment of inertia $I_1$ (a regular disc, $I_1 = \tfrac12MR^2$, or a ring $I_1 = MR^2$) and measure its period $T_1$.
2. Remove it and suspend the unknown body with its axis along the wire. Measure its period $T_2$ (same amplitude, same wire).
3. Since $C = \dfrac{4\pi^2I_1}{T_1^2} = \dfrac{4\pi^2I_2}{T_2^2}$,
$$\boxed{I_2 = I_1\frac{T_2^2}{T_1^2}}$$
This needs neither $\eta$ nor the dimensions of the wire. Alternatively, if $C$ is known from $\eta$, $l$, $r$, then $I = \dfrac{CT^2}{4\pi^2}$ directly.

@s 4
By Sabine's formula the reverberation time is $T = \dfrac{0.161V}{A}$ where $A$ is the total absorption. So $A = \dfrac{0.161V}{T}$ and the absorption of the hall is inversely proportional to $T$. Let $a$ be the absorption of one person.

- Empty hall: $A_0 = \dfrac{0.161V}{1.5}$
- With 500 persons: $A_{500} = \dfrac{0.161V}{1.4} = A_0 + 500a$
- With $N$ persons: $A_N = \dfrac{0.161V}{1.312} = A_0 + Na$

Subtracting $A_0$:
$$\begin{gathered}500a = 0.161V\left(\frac1{1.4} - \frac1{1.5}\right) \\ Na = 0.161V\left(\frac1{1.312} - \frac1{1.5}\right)\end{gathered}$$
Dividing (the unknown volume and $a$ cancel):
$$\frac N{500} = \frac{\dfrac1{1.312} - \dfrac1{1.5}}{\dfrac1{1.4} - \dfrac1{1.5}} = \frac{0.76220 - 0.66667}{0.71429 - 0.66667} = \frac{0.09553}{0.04762} = 2.006$$
$$\boxed{N\approx1003\ \approx 1000\ \text{persons}}$$

@s 5
**Einstein's quantum theory of specific heat.** Classical (Dulong–Petit) theory gives $C_V = 3R$ for all temperatures, but experiments show $C_V\to0$ as $T\to0$. Einstein (1907) applied Planck's quantum idea to the vibrations of atoms.

**Assumptions.**
1. Each atom of the solid is an independent three-dimensional harmonic oscillator, equivalent to three independent linear oscillators; $N$ atoms give $3N$ oscillators.
2. All oscillators vibrate with the **same frequency** $\nu_E$.
3. The energy of an oscillator is quantised: $\varepsilon_n = nh\nu_E$ ($n = 0, 1, 2,\ldots$, zero-point energy neglected).
4. The oscillators obey the Boltzmann distribution (thermal equilibrium at $T$).

**Mean energy.** The probability of the level $n$ is proportional to $e^{-nh\nu/kT}$. With $x = h\nu/kT$:
$$\bar\varepsilon = \frac{\sum_nnh\nu\,e^{-nx}}{\sum_ne^{-nx}} = -h\nu\frac{d}{dx}\ln\left(\sum_ne^{-nx}\right) = -h\nu\frac{d}{dx}\ln\frac{1}{1 - e^{-x}} = \frac{h\nu}{e^{h\nu/kT} - 1}$$
**Heat capacity.** The internal energy of one mole is $U = 3N_A\bar\varepsilon = \dfrac{3N_Ah\nu_E}{e^{h\nu_E/kT} - 1}$ and
$$C_V = \frac{dU}{dT} = 3N_Ak\left(\frac{h\nu_E}{kT}\right)^2\frac{e^{h\nu_E/kT}}{\left(e^{h\nu_E/kT} - 1\right)^2}$$
With the Einstein temperature $\theta_E = h\nu_E/k$:
$$\boxed{C_V = 3R\left(\frac{\theta_E}{T}\right)^2\frac{e^{\theta_E/T}}{\left(e^{\theta_E/T} - 1\right)^2}}$$
- **High temperature** ($T\gg\theta_E$): $e^{\theta_E/T}\approx1 + \theta_E/T$, so $C_V\to3R$, the Dulong–Petit value ✓.
- **Low temperature** ($T\ll\theta_E$): $C_V\approx3R\left(\dfrac{\theta_E}{T}\right)^2e^{-\theta_E/T}\to0$ as $T\to0$.

**Agreement at low temperature.** The theory correctly shows that $C_V$ **falls to zero** as $T\to0$ (the classical theory cannot), explaining why the specific heat of diamond and other solids is low at low temperatures. But the fall predicted is exponential, whereas experiment shows a slower $C_V\propto T^3$; the theory therefore agrees only *qualitatively* at low $T$. The discrepancy comes from assuming a single frequency; Debye's theory, with a spectrum of frequencies, gives the $T^3$ law.

**Or. Van der Waals equation.** The ideal-gas equation $PV = RT$ assumes point molecules with no mutual attraction. Van der Waals corrected for both:

*Volume correction.* The molecules have finite size, so each molecule is not free to move in the whole volume $V$. The available volume is $V - b$, where $b$ is the *co-volume* (four times the actual volume of the molecules in a mole).

*Pressure correction.* A molecule inside the gas is pulled equally in all directions, but a molecule near the wall feels a net inward pull from the molecules behind it. This reduces the momentum with which it strikes the wall, hence the observed pressure $P$ is **less** than the pressure $P_i$ the gas would exert without attraction. The inward pull is proportional to the number of molecules near the wall (density $\propto1/V$) and to the number pulling on each (also $\propto1/V$):
$$P_i = P + \frac{a}{V^2}$$
where $a$ is a constant measuring the attraction. Using the ideal-gas form with the corrected quantities for one mole:
$$\boxed{\left(P + \frac a{V^2}\right)(V - b) = RT}$$

**Critical constants.** Expand in powers of $V$:
$$V^3 - \left(b + \frac{RT}{P}\right)V^2 + \frac aPV - \frac{ab}{P} = 0$$
At the critical point the three roots coincide, $V = V_c$, so the cubic equals $(V - V_c)^3 = V^3 - 3V_cV^2 + 3V_c^2V - V_c^3$. Comparing coefficients,
$$\begin{gathered}3V_c = b + \frac{RT_c}{P_c} \\ 3V_c^2 = \frac a{P_c} \\ V_c^3 = \frac{ab}{P_c}\end{gathered}$$
Dividing the third by the second gives $V_c = 3b$; then $P_c = \dfrac{a}{3V_c^2}$ and $T_c$ follow:
$$\boxed{V_c = 3b,\qquad P_c = \frac{a}{27b^2},\qquad T_c = \frac{8a}{27Rb}}$$
Inverting, $a = 3P_cV_c^2 = \dfrac{27R^2T_c^2}{64P_c}$ and $b = \dfrac{V_c}{3} = \dfrac{RT_c}{8P_c}$: the constants $a$ and $b$ of a gas can be determined from its critical constants.

@s 6
**(a) Achromatism.** The refractive index depends on the colour, so a single lens has a different focal length for each colour: **chromatic aberration** (coloured fringes round the image). A lens combination is **achromatic** if it forms an image free from chromatic aberration, i.e. if the focal length (and so the position and size of the image) is the same for two chosen colours (red and violet).

**Proof.** Two thin lenses of the same material, focal lengths $f_1$ and $f_2$, separated by $d$, give a combined focal length
$$\frac1F = \frac1{f_1} + \frac1{f_2} - \frac d{f_1f_2}$$
For a lens $\dfrac1f = (\mu - 1)K$ ($K$ depends on the radii only). A small change $\delta\mu$ in the index (from red to violet) changes it by $\delta\left(\dfrac1f\right) = K\delta\mu = \dfrac1f\cdot\dfrac{\delta\mu}{\mu - 1}$. With the same $\mu$ for both lenses, put $\kappa = \dfrac{\delta\mu}{\mu - 1}$ and differentiate $\dfrac1F$ (the distance $d$ is fixed):
$$\delta\left(\frac1F\right) = \kappa\left[\frac1{f_1} + \frac1{f_2} - \frac{2d}{f_1f_2}\right] = \kappa\,\frac{f_1 + f_2 - 2d}{f_1f_2}$$
For no chromatic change, $\delta(1/F) = 0$:
$$f_1 + f_2 - 2d = 0\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{d = \frac{f_1 + f_2}{2}}$$
The combination is achromatic when the separation equals the **arithmetic mean of the focal lengths**. (This is the arrangement of the Huygens and Ramsden eyepieces.)

**(b) Newton's rings.** For an equi-convex lens, $\dfrac1f = (\mu - 1)\left(\dfrac1R + \dfrac1R\right) = \dfrac{2(\mu - 1)}{R}$:
$$R = 2(\mu - 1)f = 2(0.5)(100) = 100\ \text{cm} = 1\ \text{m}$$
In reflected light the bright rings have $D_n^2 = 2(2n - 1)\lambda R$ (derived from $2t + \lambda/2 = n\lambda$ with $t = r^2/2R$). For $n = 5$, $\lambda = 5.89\times10^{-7}$ m:
$$D_5^2 = 2(2\times5 - 1)(5.89\times10^{-7})(1) = 1.060\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2$$
$$\boxed{D_5 = 3.26\times10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 3.26\ \text{mm}}$$

**(c) Sodium doublet in the first order.** Grating element
$$d = \frac{0.5\ \text{cm}}{2500} = 2\times10^{-4}\ \text{cm} = 2.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}$$
$d\sin\theta = n\lambda$ with $n = 1$:
$$\sin\theta_1 = \frac{5890\times10^{-10}}{2.0\times10^{-6}} = 0.2945\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\theta_1 = 17.13^\circ}$$
$$\sin\theta_2 = \frac{5896\times10^{-10}}{2.0\times10^{-6}} = 0.2948\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\theta_2 = 17.15^\circ}$$
The two lines are separated by only $\Delta\theta = 0.018^\circ\approx1.1'$ in the first order. This agrees with the dispersion $\dfrac{d\theta}{d\lambda} = \dfrac{n}{d\cos\theta} = 5.2\times10^{-5}\ \text{rad/Å}$ times $\Delta\lambda = 6$ Å, which is $3.1\times10^{-4}$ rad. So the doublet is only just resolved in this first order.

**(d) Stimulated emission.** An excited atom can drop to a lower level by emitting a photon. In **stimulated emission** an incoming photon of energy $h\nu = E_2 - E_1$ makes the excited atom emit a second photon of the **same frequency, phase, direction and polarisation**. This coherent amplification is the basis of the laser. It dominates over absorption only if there is a **population inversion** ($N_2>N_1$).

**Construction of the He–Ne laser.**
- A glass discharge tube about 30 cm long and 2–8 mm bore, filled with helium (about 1 torr) and neon (about 0.1 torr), i.e. He : Ne $\approx$ 10 : 1.
- Electrodes at the ends connect to a high-voltage source (1–2 kV, a few mA) to cause an electric discharge.
- An optical resonator: two parallel mirrors at the ends, one totally reflecting and the other about 99 % reflecting (partially transmitting, from which the beam emerges). The end windows are inclined at Brewster's angle, which gives a plane-polarised output.

**Working (energy level diagram).**
1. The discharge accelerates electrons, which collide with He atoms and excite them to the metastable levels $2^3S$ (19.8 eV) and $2^1S$ (20.6 eV). These metastable atoms live long, so the He level populations build up.
2. Colliding with ground-state Ne atoms, the He atoms transfer their energy resonantly (the levels nearly coincide, 20.66 and 19.78 eV in neon) and return to the ground state. Neon atoms are pumped to the $3s$ and $2s$ levels, giving a **population inversion** with respect to the lower $3p$ and $2p$ levels.
3. Spontaneous photons stimulate transitions between these levels. The main laser lines are $3s\to2p$ at **632.8 nm** (red, the usual output), $3s\to3p$ at 3.39 µm and $2s\to2p$ at 1.15 µm.
4. Neon then decays $2p\to1s$ (fast) and $1s\to$ ground by collisions with the tube wall (so the bore is narrow), which empties the lower level and maintains the inversion.
5. Photons bounce between the mirrors, the intensity grows, and a continuous coherent beam (1–50 mW) leaves through the partial mirror.

@s 7
**(a) Field of a short dipole.** Let the dipole have moment $p$ ($\pm q$ separated by $2a$, $p = 2qa$). The potential at a point $(r,\theta)$ with $r\gg a$ is
$$V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}$$
The field components follow from $\vec E = -\nabla V$ in polar coordinates:
$$\begin{gathered}E_r = -\frac{\partial V}{\partial r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2p\cos\theta}{r^3} \\ E_\theta = -\frac1r\frac{\partial V}{\partial\theta} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\sin\theta}{r^3}\end{gathered}$$
$$E = \sqrt{E_r^2 + E_\theta^2} = \frac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}\sqrt{1 + 3\cos^2\theta}$$
- **Axial line** ($\theta = 0$): $E_\theta = 0$, $E_r = \dfrac{2p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}$, so $\boxed{E_{axial} = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{2p}{r^3}}$, along $\vec p$.
- **Equatorial line** ($\theta = 90^\circ$): $E_r = 0$, $E_\theta = \dfrac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}$, so $\boxed{E_{eq} = \dfrac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{p}{r^3}}$, opposite to $\vec p$.
$$\frac{E_{axial}}{E_{eq}} = \frac{2p/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}{p/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3} = 2$$
so the field at a point on the axial line is **twice** that at the same distance on the equatorial line. *Direct check (axial):* $E = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\dfrac{1}{(r - a)^2} - \dfrac{1}{(r + a)^2}\right] = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{4ar}{(r^2 - a^2)^2}\approx\dfrac{2p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}$ ✓.

**Or. Electric quadrupole.** A linear electric quadrupole consists of charges $+q$, $-2q$, $+q$ on a line with spacing $d$ (two opposite dipoles end to end). Net charge and dipole moment are zero. The **quadrupole moment** is $Q = 2qd^2$.

*Potential on the axis* at distance $r$ from the centre ($r>d$):
$$V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left[\frac1{r - d} - \frac2r + \frac1{r + d}\right] = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2d^2}{r(r^2 - d^2)}$$
For $r\gg d$:
$$\boxed{V = \frac{2qd^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^3}}$$
The potential falls off as $r^{-3}$.

**(b) Steps to find a capacitance.**
1. Put charges $+Q$ and $-Q$ on the two conductors.
2. Find the electric field $\vec E$ between them (Gauss' law, using the symmetry).
3. Find the potential difference $V = -\int_-^+\vec E\cdot d\vec l$.
4. Then $C = Q/V$ (independent of $Q$, a function of geometry and dielectric).

**Cylindrical capacitor.** Coaxial cylinders of radii $a$ (inner) and $b$ (outer), length $L\gg b$, with charge $\pm Q$. Choose a coaxial Gaussian cylinder of radius $r$ ($a<r<b$) and length $l$: $E(2\pi rl) = \dfrac{(Q/L)l}{\varepsilon_0}$:
$$E = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0Lr}$$
$$V = -\int_b^aE\,dr = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0L}\int_a^b\frac{dr}{r} = \frac{Q}{2\pi\varepsilon_0L}\ln\frac ba$$
$$\boxed{C = \frac QV = \frac{2\pi\varepsilon_0L}{\ln(b/a)}}$$
Capacitance per unit length $= \dfrac{2\pi\varepsilon_0}{\ln(b/a)}$; with a dielectric, $\varepsilon_0\to\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r$. (For $b - a = s\ll a$ it reduces to the parallel-plate result $C\approx\varepsilon_0A/s$.)

**Displacement current density.** Maxwell's displacement current density is $J_d = \varepsilon_0\dfrac{dE}{dt}$:
$$J_d = (8.854\times10^{-12})(2\times10^{12}) = \boxed{17.7\ \text{A m}^{-2}}$$
(The unit of $dE/dt$ is V m⁻¹ s⁻¹.) The displacement current through the plates is
$$I_d = J_dA = (17.7)(2.3\times10^{-2}) = 0.41\ \text{A}$$
**Induced magnetic field.** Take circular plates of area $A = \pi R^2 = 2.3\times10^{-2}$ m², so $R = 0.0856$ m. By the Ampère–Maxwell law with the field uniform over the plates, for a circular loop of radius $r\le R$ in the gap:
$$B(2\pi r) = \mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{dE}{dt}\pi r^2\ \Rightarrow\ B = \frac{\mu_0\varepsilon_0r}{2}\frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{\mu_0J_dr}{2}$$
$B$ is zero on the axis and largest at the rim ($r = R$):
$$B_{\max} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(17.7)(0.0856)}{2} = \boxed{9.5\times10^{-7}\ \text{T}\approx0.95\ \mu\text{T}}$$
(Outside the plates, $r>R$, $B = \dfrac{\mu_0J_dR^2}{2r}$.)

@s 9
Relative permittivity $\varepsilon_r = 4$ and relative permeability $\mu_r = 1$.

**(i)** Speed of the wave in the medium:
$$v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu\varepsilon}} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r}} = \frac{3\times10^8}{\sqrt{1\times4}} = \boxed{1.5\times10^{8}\ \text{m s}^{-1}}$$
(refractive index $n = \sqrt{\mu_r\varepsilon_r} = 2$).

**(ii)** The frequency is $f = v/\lambda$ with $\lambda = 400$ nm in the medium:
$$f = \frac{1.5\times10^8}{400\times10^{-9}} = \boxed{3.75\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}}$$
The frequency does not change on entering a medium; the wavelength in vacuum would be $c/f = 800$ nm.

@s 10
**Barrier tunnelling.** A particle of mass $m$ and energy $E$ moves in the $+x$ direction toward a rectangular potential barrier of height $V_0>E$ and width $L$:
$$V(x) = \begin{cases}0 & x<0\ \text{(region I)}\\ V_0 & 0<x<L\ \text{(region II)}\\ 0 & x>L\ \text{(region III)}\end{cases}$$
Classically the particle is reflected. Quantum mechanically the wave function does not vanish inside the barrier, so there is a finite probability of finding the particle beyond it: this is **tunnelling**.

**Schrödinger equation in each region**, $\dfrac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + \dfrac{2m}{\hbar^2}(E - V)\psi = 0$:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{I, III: }\ \psi'' + k^2\psi = 0 \\ k = \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}\end{gathered}$$
$$\begin{gathered}\text{II: }\ \psi'' - \kappa^2\psi = 0 \\ \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}}{\hbar}\end{gathered}$$
Solutions: 
$$\begin{gathered}\psi_I = Ae^{ikx} + Be^{-ikx} \\ \psi_{II} = Ce^{\kappa x} + De^{-\kappa x} \\ \psi_{III} = Fe^{ikx}\end{gathered}$$
In region III there is only a transmitted (outgoing) wave. $A$ is the incident, $B$ the reflected amplitude. Since the potential is finite, $\psi$ and $\psi'$ are continuous at $x = 0$ and $x = L$:
$$\begin{gathered}A + B = C + D \\ ik(A - B) = \kappa(C - D) \\ Ce^{\kappa L} + De^{-\kappa L} = Fe^{ikL} \\ \kappa\left(Ce^{\kappa L} - De^{-\kappa L}\right) = ikFe^{ikL}\end{gathered}$$
Solving the last two for $C$ and $D$ and substituting into the first two gives
$$\frac{A}{F}e^{ikL} = \cosh\kappa L - \frac i2\left(\frac k\kappa - \frac\kappa k\right)\sinh\kappa L$$
Taking the modulus squared and using $\cosh^2 = 1 + \sinh^2$ together with $k^2 + \kappa^2 = 2mV_0/\hbar^2$ and $k^2\kappa^2 = 4m^2E(V_0 - E)/\hbar^4$:
$$\left|\frac AF\right|^2 = 1 + \frac{(k^2 + \kappa^2)^2}{4k^2\kappa^2}\sinh^2\kappa L = 1 + \frac{V_0^2\sinh^2\kappa L}{4E(V_0 - E)}$$
**Transmission coefficient** $T$ is the ratio of the transmitted to the incident probability current (flux); since the speed is the same on both sides, $T = \dfrac{|F|^2}{|A|^2}$:
$$\boxed{T = \left[1 + \frac{V_0^2\sinh^2\kappa L}{4E(V_0 - E)}\right]^{-1}}$$
**Reflection coefficient** $R = \dfrac{|B|^2}{|A|^2}$ is the fraction of the incident flux that is reflected. By conservation of probability,
$$\boxed{R + T = 1}$$
**Thick barrier** ($\kappa L\gg1$, $\sinh\kappa L\approx\tfrac12e^{\kappa L}$):
$$\begin{gathered}T\approx16\frac{E}{V_0}\left(1 - \frac E{V_0}\right)e^{-2\kappa L} \\ \kappa = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0 - E)}}{\hbar}\end{gathered}$$
So tunnelling decreases exponentially with barrier width and with $\sqrt{m(V_0 - E)}$: electrons tunnel readily through barriers of about 1 nm, heavier particles hardly at all. *Applications:* $\alpha$-decay, the tunnel diode, the scanning tunnelling microscope, and flash memory.
