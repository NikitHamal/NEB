**Bayes' theorem.** If $A_1, \dots, A_n$ are mutually exclusive and exhaustive events with $P(A_i) > 0$ and $B$ is any event with $P(B) > 0$, then

$$P(A_i|B) = \frac{P(A_i)\,P(B|A_i)}{\sum_{j=1}^{n}P(A_j)\,P(B|A_j)}$$

It updates the prior probability $P(A_i)$ to the posterior $P(A_i|B)$ once $B$ is known. The denominator is the total probability $P(B)$.

Let $D$ = "touch screen is defective": $P(A) = 0.30$, $P(B) = 0.40$, $P(C) = 0.30$, $P(D|A) = 0.08$, $P(D|B) = 0.06$, $P(D|C) = 0.12$.

| Machine | Prior $P(M)$ | $P(D\|M)$ | $P(M)P(D\|M)$ | Posterior $P(M\|D)$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | 0.30 | 0.08 | 0.024 | 0.024/0.084 = 0.2857 |
| B | 0.40 | 0.06 | 0.024 | 0.024/0.084 = 0.2857 |
| C | 0.30 | 0.12 | 0.036 | 0.036/0.084 = 0.4286 |
| **Total** | 1 | | **0.084** | 1 |

$$P(D) = 0.024 + 0.024 + 0.036 = 0.084$$

**(a)** $P(A|D) = \dfrac{0.024}{0.084} = \boxed{\dfrac27 = 0.2857}$

**(b)** $P(B|D) = \dfrac{0.024}{0.084} = \boxed{\dfrac27 = 0.2857}$

**(c)** $P(C|D) = \dfrac{0.036}{0.084} = \boxed{\dfrac37 = 0.4286}$

The posteriors add up to $1$ ✓. Machine C, with the highest defect rate, is the most likely source of a defective screen although it makes only 30% of the output.

**Conditions for a Poisson distribution.** A Poisson process counts the number of occurrences of an event in a fixed interval of time or space when: (1) the events occur **independently** of each other; (2) the **average rate** $\lambda$ per interval is constant; (3) two events cannot occur at exactly the same instant (the probability of an occurrence in a very short interval is proportional to its length, and of two or more is negligible); (4) the number of trials is large and the probability of success per trial is small ($n\to\infty$, $p\to0$, $np = \lambda$). Then

$$\begin{gathered}P(X = x) = \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{x!},\quad x = 0, 1, 2, \dots \\ \text{mean} = \text{variance} = \lambda.\end{gathered}$$

Here $\lambda = 2.5$ accidents per month, $e^{-2.5} = 0.082085$.

| $x$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $P(x)$ | 0.08208 | 0.20521 | 0.25652 | 0.21376 |

**(a)** $P(X = 2) = \dfrac{e^{-2.5}(2.5)^2}{2!} = \dfrac{0.082085\times6.25}{2} = \boxed{0.2565}$

**Normal distribution.** A continuous random variable $X$ is normally distributed, $X\sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, if its pdf is

$$\begin{gathered}f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-\frac{(x - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}} \\ -\infty < x < \infty.\end{gathered}$$

It is a symmetric bell-shaped curve with mean = median = mode $= \mu$, points of inflection at $\mu\pm\sigma$, total area $1$, and about $68.3\%$, $95.4\%$, $99.7\%$ of the area within $\pm1\sigma$, $\pm2\sigma$, $\pm3\sigma$.

$\mu = 1000$ h, $\sigma = 200$ h, $N = 2000$ lamps.

$$\boxed{P(X = 900) = 0}$$

**Importance of the central limit theorem.** The CLT states that the distribution of the sample mean $\bar X$ tends to normal, with mean $\mu$ and standard error $\sigma/\sqrt n$, for large $n$ whatever the shape of the population. Its importance:
1. It lets us use the normal table for probabilities about $\bar X$ even when the population is not normal.
2. It is the basis of confidence intervals and of the $z$ and $t$ tests for means.
3. It justifies approximating binomial and Poisson distributions by the normal one.
4. It shows that $\bar X$ becomes more precise as $n$ increases (the standard error falls as $1/\sqrt n$), which guides the choice of sample size, and it underlies control charts.

@s 7
**Chi-square test for independence of two qualitative (categorical) variables.** It tests whether two attributes, e.g. A with $r$ categories and B with $c$ categories, are associated.

**Step 1. Arrange the data** in an $r\times c$ **contingency table** of observed frequencies $O_{ij}$, with row totals $R_i$, column totals $C_j$, and grand total $N$.

**Step 2. State the hypotheses.**
$H_0$: the two attributes are independent (no association).
$H_1$: the attributes are not independent (associated).

**Step 3. Choose the level of significance** $\alpha$ (e.g. $0.05$).

**Step 4. Compute the expected frequencies** under independence:

$$E_{ij} = \frac{R_i\times C_j}{N}$$

Check the condition that every $E_{ij}\ge5$ (otherwise combine adjacent categories).

**Step 5. Compute the test statistic**

$$\chi^2_{cal} = \sum_{i}\sum_{j}\frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}$$

For a $2\times2$ table with small counts, apply Yates' correction $\dfrac{(|O - E| - 0.5)^2}{E}$.

**Step 6. Degrees of freedom and critical value.** $df = (r - 1)(c - 1)$; read $\chi^2_{\alpha,df}$ from the table.

**Step 7. Decision.** If $\chi^2_{cal} > \chi^2_{\alpha, df}$ (or $p < \alpha$), reject $H_0$: the attributes are associated. Otherwise accept $H_0$: no evidence of association.

**Step 8. State the conclusion** in the language of the problem. (If $H_0$ is rejected, strength of association can be measured by the contingency coefficient $C = \sqrt{\chi^2/(\chi^2 + N)}$ or Cramér's $V$.)

@s 10
**Properties of Karl Pearson's coefficient of correlation $r$.**
1. $-1 \le r \le +1$.
2. $r = +1$: perfect positive linear relation; $r = -1$: perfect negative; $r = 0$: no *linear* relation.
3. $r$ is a pure number (no units), symmetric: $r_{xy} = r_{yx}$.
4. $r$ is independent of change of origin and scale: for $u = (x - a)/h$, $v = (y - b)/k$ with $h, k > 0$, $r_{uv} = r_{xy}$.
5. $r$ is the geometric mean of the regression coefficients, $r = \pm\sqrt{b_{yx}b_{xy}}$, with the same sign as both.
6. Independent variables have $r = 0$, but $r = 0$ does not imply independence (non-linear relations).
7. $r^2$, the coefficient of determination, gives the fraction of variation explained.

**Calculation.** $x$ = curing time, $y$ = strength, $n = 7$.

| $x$ | $y$ | $x^2$ | $y^2$ | $xy$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 18 | 9 | 324 | 54 |
| 7 | 24 | 49 | 576 | 168 |
| 14 | 29 | 196 | 841 | 406 |
| 21 | 32 | 441 | 1024 | 672 |
| 28 | 35 | 784 | 1225 | 980 |
| 35 | 37 | 1225 | 1369 | 1295 |
| 42 | 39 | 1764 | 1521 | 1638 |
| **150** | **214** | **4468** | **6880** | **5213** |

$$r = \frac{n\sum xy - \sum x\sum y}{\sqrt{[n\sum x^2 - (\sum x)^2][n\sum y^2 - (\sum y)^2]}} = \frac{7(5213) - 150(214)}{\sqrt{[7(4468) - 150^2][7(6880) - 214^2]}}$$

$$= \frac{36491 - 32100}{\sqrt{(8776)(2364)}} = \frac{4391}{4554.8}$$

$$\boxed{r = 0.964}$$

**Coefficient of determination**

$$\boxed{r^2 = 0.929}$$

**Interpretation.** $r = 0.964$ shows a very strong positive correlation: concrete strength increases with curing time. $r^2 = 0.929$ means that about $92.9\%$ of the variation in compressive strength is explained by curing time; the remaining $7.1\%$ is due to other factors (mix, temperature, etc.). (The relation is actually curved, rising fast in the first weeks and levelling off, so a straight line is slightly less accurate than $r$ suggests.)
