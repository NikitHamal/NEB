@s 1
**(a) Cell notation** is the shorthand way of writing a galvanic cell. The anode (oxidation half-cell) is written on the left and the cathode (reduction half-cell) on the right; a single bar $|$ (or /) shows a phase boundary, the double bar $\|$ (or //) shows the salt bridge, and concentrations are written in brackets. Here Zn is the anode and Ag the cathode.

Cell reaction: $\text{Zn} + 2\text{Ag}^+\to\text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{Ag}$, with $n = 2$.
$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cathode}} - E^\circ_{\text{anode}} = 0.80 - (-0.76) = 1.56\ \text{V}$$
By the Nernst equation at 25°C,
$$E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ_{\text{cell}} - \frac{0.0591}{2}\log\frac{[\text{Zn}^{2+}]}{[\text{Ag}^+]^2}$$
$$E_{\text{cell}} = 1.56 - 0.02955\log\frac{0.1}{(0.2)^2} = 1.56 - 0.02955\log2.5 = 1.56 - 0.02955(0.398)$$
$$\boxed{E_{\text{cell}} = 1.56 - 0.0118 = 1.548\ \text{V}}$$

**Or. Polarisation.** Polarisation is the deviation of the potential of an electrode from its equilibrium (reversible) value when a current is drawn from or passed through it. The cell voltage of a working galvanic cell falls (and the voltage needed for electrolysis rises) because of it. Types: *activation* (slow electron-transfer step), *concentration* (the ion concentration near the electrode differs from the bulk), and *ohmic* (film or solution resistance).

*Example.* In the simple Volta cell (Zn | dil. H₂SO₄ | Cu), the H⁺ ions are discharged at the copper plate: $2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-\to\text{H}_2$. The H₂ bubbles collect on the copper, so the plate behaves like a hydrogen electrode instead of copper, and the emf falls from about 1.1 V to about 0.4 V within a short time. This is hydrogen (gas) polarisation. It is removed by a **depolariser** (e.g. MnO₂ in the Leclanché cell, which oxidises H₂ to H₂O).

@s 2
**Henderson's equation for a basic buffer.** A basic buffer is a weak base B and its salt BH⁺ (e.g. NH₄OH + NH₄Cl).
$$\text{B} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{BH}^+ + \text{OH}^-,\qquad K_b = \frac{[\text{BH}^+][\text{OH}^-]}{[\text{B}]}$$
$$[\text{OH}^-] = K_b\frac{[\text{B}]}{[\text{BH}^+]}$$
The base is weak and its ionisation is suppressed by the salt, so $[\text{B}]\approx$ initial concentration of base and $[\text{BH}^+]\approx$ concentration of salt. Taking $-\log$ on both sides:
$$\text{pOH} = \text{p}K_b + \log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{base}]},\qquad\text{pH} = 14 - \text{pOH}$$
$$\boxed{\text{pH} = 14 - \text{p}K_b - \log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{base}]}}$$

**Numerical.** The sodium acetate–acetic acid mixture is an *acidic* buffer, for which $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\dfrac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]}$.
Moles of CH₃COONa $= \dfrac{1.64}{82} = 0.02$ mol, in 0.2 L (the volume change is neglected), so $[\text{salt}] = 0.1$ M. The acid is $[\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}] = 0.2$ M. $\text{p}K_a = -\log(1.8\times10^{-5}) = 4.745$.
$$\text{pH} = 4.745 + \log\frac{0.1}{0.2} = 4.745 - 0.301 = \boxed{4.44}$$

@s 3
**(a)** *Homogeneous catalysts* are in the **same phase** as the reactants. Examples: NO(g) in the lead-chamber process, $2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2\xrightarrow{\text{NO}}2\text{SO}_3$ (all gases); H⁺(aq) in the hydrolysis of an ester.

*Heterogeneous catalysts* are in a **different phase** from the reactants (usually a solid catalyst with gaseous or liquid reactants). Examples: Fe in the Haber process, $\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\xrightarrow{\text{Fe}}2\text{NH}_3$; V₂O₅ in the Contact process; Ni in hydrogenation of oils. They are easily separated and reused, and the reaction occurs on the surface (adsorption, reaction, desorption).

**(b) Photocatalytic water splitting.** A semiconductor photocatalyst (TiO₂, ZnO, CdS, SrTiO₃) absorbs a photon of energy $h\nu\ge E_g$ (3.2 eV for TiO₂, so UV light). An electron is promoted from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), leaving a hole $h^+$ in the VB:
$$\text{TiO}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu\ \ge E_g}e^-_{CB} + h^+_{VB}$$
The charge carriers migrate to the surface, where
- at the hole (oxidation): $\text{H}_2\text{O} + 2h^+\to\tfrac12\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}^+$ ($E^\circ = 1.23$ V vs NHE),
- at the electron (reduction): $2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-\to\text{H}_2$ ($E^\circ = 0$ V).

The overall reaction is $\text{H}_2\text{O}\xrightarrow{h\nu}\text{H}_2 + \tfrac12\text{O}_2$ ($\Delta G = +237$ kJ/mol). For it to work, the CB edge must lie **above** (more negative than) the H⁺/H₂ level and the VB edge **below** the O₂/H₂O level, so $E_g>1.23$ eV. Co-catalysts such as Pt (for H₂) and RuO₂ (for O₂) and sacrificial agents improve the yield. A major problem is the recombination of $e^-$ and $h^+$.

@s 4
**Chromatography** is a technique for separating the components of a mixture by distributing them between two phases: a **stationary phase** (a solid, or a liquid held on a solid) and a **mobile phase** (a liquid or gas) that moves through it. Components that interact differently with the two phases travel at different rates and separate.

**Paper chromatography.**
*Principle:* **partition**. The stationary phase is the water held in the pores of the cellulose of filter paper, and the mobile phase is an organic solvent (e.g. butanol–acetic acid–water). Each solute partitions between the bound water and the moving solvent according to its partition coefficient. The more soluble in the mobile phase, the farther it moves. A spot is characterised by
$$R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the solute}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent front}}\ (<1)$$
*Procedure:* a spot of the mixture is placed near the bottom of the paper strip, which is hung in a closed jar with the lower edge dipping in the solvent. The solvent rises by capillarity, and the paper is removed, dried and developed (ninhydrin for amino acids).

*Applications:* (i) separation and identification of amino acids, sugars and organic acids; (ii) analysis of pigments (plant dyes, inks); (iii) detection of drugs and food additives; (iv) checking the purity of a compound; (v) separating metal ions (inorganic analysis).

**Or. (a) IR spectroscopy.**
*Principle:* a molecule absorbs infrared radiation (4000–400 cm⁻¹) when the frequency matches a **vibrational** (stretching or bending) frequency of a bond **and** the vibration causes a **change in the dipole moment**. Homonuclear diatomics (H₂, O₂, N₂) are therefore IR-inactive. The stretching frequency is
$$\bar\nu = \frac{1}{2\pi c}\sqrt{\frac k\mu}$$
where $k$ is the force constant and $\mu$ the reduced mass. The spectrum is a plot of % transmittance against wavenumber.

*Applications:* (i) identification of functional groups (O–H ≈ 3300 cm⁻¹ broad, C=O ≈ 1700 cm⁻¹, C≡N ≈ 2250 cm⁻¹, N–H ≈ 3400 cm⁻¹); (ii) identification of an unknown compound by its fingerprint region (1500–600 cm⁻¹); (iii) checking purity; (iv) following the progress of a reaction; (v) studying hydrogen bonding; (vi) quantitative analysis.

**(b) Spin–spin splitting in NMR.** The signal of a proton is split into a **multiplet** by the magnetic fields of non-equivalent protons on **adjacent** carbons. By the **$n+1$ rule**, a proton with $n$ equivalent neighbouring protons gives $n+1$ lines, with intensities given by Pascal's triangle (singlet 1; doublet 1:1; triplet 1:2:1; quartet 1:3:3:1). The spacing between the lines is the coupling constant $J$ (in Hz), the same for the coupled partners.

*Example: ethyl bromide, CH₃CH₂Br.* The CH₃ protons have two neighbouring CH₂ protons, so they appear as a **triplet** (1:2:1) at about $\delta$ 1.7. The CH₂ protons have three neighbouring CH₃ protons, so they appear as a **quartet** (1:3:3:1) at about $\delta$ 3.4. Equivalent protons do not split each other, and the O–H proton usually appears as a singlet because of exchange.

@s 5
**Chelate.** A chelate is a complex in which a **polydentate ligand** (one that has two or more donor atoms) binds to the central metal ion through two or more donor atoms, forming a **ring** (usually 5- or 6-membered). Chelates are more stable than complexes of monodentate ligands (the chelate effect). *Example:* $[\text{Co(en)}_3]^{3+}$ (en = ethane-1,2-diamine, bidentate), or the Ca²⁺–EDTA complex.

**VBT for $[\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{3+}$.**
- Fe³⁺ has configuration $[\text{Ar}]3d^5$, with 5 unpaired electrons.
- H₂O is a **weak-field** ligand, so it cannot pair the 3d electrons. The 3d orbitals stay singly occupied.
- Six water molecules donate lone pairs into the vacant $4s$, three $4p$ and two $4d$ orbitals: **$sp^3d^2$** hybridisation (outer-orbital complex).

| | 3d | 4s | 4p | 4d |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fe³⁺ | ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ | ○ | ○ ○ ○ | ○ ○ |
| In complex | ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ ↑ | ⇅ (from H₂O) | ⇅ ⇅ ⇅ | ⇅ ⇅ |

**Geometry: octahedral.** **Magnetic property:** five unpaired electrons, so it is **paramagnetic (high spin)**, with $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)} = \sqrt{35} = 5.92$ BM.

**IUPAC names.**
- (i) $\text{K}_3[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]$: **potassium hexacyanidoferrate(III)**.
- (ii) $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{3+}$: **hexaamminecobalt(III) ion**.
- (iii) $[\text{Cr(en)}_3]\text{Cl}_3$ (written $[\text{Cr(en)}_3\text{Cl}_3]$ in the paper; Cr(III) is six-coordinate with three en, and the three Cl⁻ are counter-ions): **tris(ethane-1,2-diamine)chromium(III) chloride**.

@s 6
**Postulates of crystal field theory (CFT).**
1. The bonding between the metal ion and the ligands is **purely electrostatic**: ligands are treated as point negative charges (or dipoles), and the metal ion as a positive point charge.
2. In the free ion the five $d$ orbitals are degenerate. In the field of the ligands they are **repelled** by the ligand electrons, and the degeneracy is lifted.
3. In an **octahedral** field the $d$ orbitals split into two sets: the lower $t_{2g}$ ($d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{zx}$, which point between the ligands) and the higher $e_g$ ($d_{x^2-y^2}, d_{z^2}$, which point at the ligands), separated by $\Delta_o$. The $t_{2g}$ set lies $0.4\Delta_o$ below and the $e_g$ set $0.6\Delta_o$ above the barycentre. In a **tetrahedral** field the splitting is inverted and smaller, $\Delta_t = \tfrac49\Delta_o$.
4. The size of $\Delta$ depends on the ligand (spectrochemical series: $\text{I}^-<\text{Br}^-<\text{Cl}^-<\text{F}^-<\text{H}_2\text{O}<\text{NH}_3<\text{en}<\text{CN}^-<\text{CO}$), on the metal and its oxidation state, and on the geometry.
5. The electrons fill the orbitals according to the competition between $\Delta_o$ and the pairing energy $P$: **weak field** ($\Delta_o<P$) gives *high spin*; **strong field** ($\Delta_o>P$) gives *low spin*.
6. The colour, magnetism and stability of complexes arise from the $d$–$d$ transitions and from the crystal field stabilisation energy (CFSE).

**Applications of cerium.**
- *Catalysis*: CeO₂ in automobile catalytic converters (oxygen storage/release), and in cracking catalysts.
- *Glass*: polishing powder for lenses and mirrors; ceria glass absorbs UV light; decolourises glass by oxidising Fe²⁺.
- *Metallurgy*: in mischmetal alloys (lighter flints, ~50% Ce) and as an additive in steels and Mg alloys.
- *Analysis*: Ce(IV) is a strong oxidant in volumetric analysis (cerimetry).
- *Others*: phosphors in lamps and displays, self-cleaning oven walls, diesel fuel additive to lower soot emission, fuel-cell electrolytes.

@s 7
**(a) E-waste.** Electronic waste (old computers, phones, TVs, batteries) is a growing concern because of its volume (the fastest-growing waste stream, from short product life) and because it contains **toxic substances**, such as lead, mercury, cadmium, arsenic, chromium(VI) and brominated flame retardants. Dumping or burning it contaminates soil, water and air, harming health, and informal recycling exposes workers. At the same time it contains valuable metals (Au, Ag, Cu, Pd, rare earths) that are lost if not recycled.

**Four principles of green chemistry** (Anastas and Warner):
1. **Prevention of waste**: it is better to prevent waste than to treat or clean it up afterwards.
2. **Atom economy**: design syntheses so that most of the atoms of the reactants end up in the product. Atom economy = $\dfrac{\text{M of desired product}}{\text{M of all reactants}}\times100$.
3. **Less hazardous chemical syntheses**: use and generate substances with little or no toxicity to people and the environment.
4. **Safer solvents and auxiliaries**: avoid auxiliary substances or use harmless ones (water, supercritical CO₂ in place of chlorinated solvents).

(Others: design of safer chemicals, energy efficiency, renewable feedstocks, catalysis, real-time analysis, accident prevention.)

**(b) Water pollutants** and their control:

| Pollutant | Source and effect | Control |
|---|---|---|
| Pathogens (bacteria, viruses) | sewage; cholera, typhoid | chlorination, ozonation, UV, boiling |
| Organic waste (high BOD) | sewage, food industry; oxygen depletion | activated-sludge treatment, trickling filters, oxidation ponds |
| Nutrients (nitrate, phosphate) | fertilisers, detergents; eutrophication | tertiary treatment, controlled fertiliser use, phosphate-free detergents |

@s 8
**Nanomaterials** are materials in which at least one dimension lies in the nanometre range, about **1–100 nm** (1 nm = $10^{-9}$ m). At this size the large surface-to-volume ratio and quantum confinement give properties (optical, electrical, magnetic, catalytic, mechanical) that differ from those of the bulk material.

**Classification on the basis of dimensions.** The classification depends on how many dimensions are **outside** the nano range:
- **0-D (zero-dimensional)**: all three dimensions in the nano range. Examples: nanoparticles, quantum dots, fullerene C₆₀, nanoclusters.
- **1-D**: two dimensions in the nano range, one large. Examples: nanowires, nanorods, nanotubes (CNT), nanofibres.
- **2-D**: one dimension in the nano range. Examples: graphene, nanosheets, thin films, coatings, nanoplates.
- **3-D**: no dimension is confined to the nano scale, but the material is built from nanoscale units (e.g. nanocrystalline bulk materials, nanocomposites, aggregates of nanowires or nanotubes, dendrimers).

@s 9
**(a)(i) Free-radical (chain-growth) addition polymerisation**, with ethene/styrene as the example, takes place in three steps. Let I–I be an initiator such as benzoyl peroxide or AIBN and M the monomer, CH₂=CHX.
1. **Initiation**: the initiator decomposes by heat or light to free radicals, which add to the monomer.
$$\text{I–I}\xrightarrow{\Delta}2\,\text{I}\cdot,\qquad \text{I}\cdot + \text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHX}\to\text{I–CH}_2\text{–C}\cdot\text{HX}$$
2. **Propagation**: the new radical adds to further monomer molecules, one at a time, each step regenerating a radical at the chain end.
$$\text{I–CH}_2\text{–C}\cdot\text{HX} + n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHX}\to\text{I–(CH}_2\text{CHX)}_n\text{–CH}_2\text{–C}\cdot\text{HX}$$
3. **Termination**: two growing radicals meet and end the chain by *combination* (coupling) to give one long chain, or by *disproportionation* (one chain takes a hydrogen atom from the other, giving one saturated and one unsaturated chain).

*Example:* styrene $\to$ polystyrene; ethene $\to$ polythene; vinyl chloride $\to$ PVC.

**(ii) Polyphosphazene.**
*Preparation:* (1) Phosphorus pentachloride reacts with ammonium chloride (in chlorobenzene or tetrachloroethane) to give hexachlorocyclotriphosphazene, $\text{PCl}_5 + \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}\to\tfrac13(\text{NPCl}_2)_3 + 4\text{HCl}$. (2) **Ring-opening polymerisation** of the trimer at about 250°C gives poly(dichlorophosphazene), $[\text{–N=PCl}_2\text{–}]_n$ (an inorganic rubber). (3) The reactive P–Cl bonds are then replaced by reacting with sodium alkoxides, aryloxides or amines, e.g. $[\text{NPCl}_2]_n + 2n\,\text{NaOR}\to[\text{NP(OR)}_2]_n + 2n\,\text{NaCl}$, giving stable poly(organophosphazenes).

*Uses:* fire-resistant and flexible materials, low-temperature elastomers, fuel hoses, O-rings and seals (aircraft, automotive), biomedical materials (drug delivery, tissue scaffolds), solid polymer electrolytes, insulation.

**(b) Kevlar** (poly(p-phenylene terephthalamide), an aramid).
*Preparation:* condensation (step-growth) polymerisation of **terephthaloyl chloride** with **p-phenylenediamine** in a solvent such as N-methylpyrrolidone with CaCl₂, at low temperature, with loss of HCl:
$$n\,\text{ClOC–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–COCl} + n\,\text{H}_2\text{N–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–NH}_2\to[\text{–OC–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–CO–NH–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–NH–}]_n + 2n\,\text{HCl}$$
The rigid rod-like chains, held together by hydrogen bonds, are spun from concentrated H₂SO₄.
*Applications:* bulletproof vests and helmets, tyre cords, ropes and cables, aircraft and spacecraft parts, protective clothing, brake linings (replacing asbestos).

**Epoxy resin.**
*Preparation:* bisphenol-A reacts with excess epichlorohydrin in the presence of NaOH to give the diglycidyl ether (DGEBA) prepolymer; on mixing with a **hardener** (a polyamine such as triethylenetetramine, or an anhydride) the epoxide rings open and cross-link into a rigid thermoset network.
$$\text{HO–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–C(CH}_3)_2\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–OH} + 2\,\text{ClCH}_2\text{–CH–CH}_2\text{(O)}\xrightarrow{\text{NaOH}}\text{DGEBA} + 2\,\text{HCl}$$
*Applications:* strong adhesives (Araldite), protective coatings and paints, fibre-reinforced composites, circuit boards, encapsulation of electronic parts, flooring.

**Or. (i) Conducting polypyrrole.** It is an intrinsically conducting polymer made by oxidative polymerisation of pyrrole, chemically (FeCl₃ or (NH₄)₂S₂O₈) or electrochemically on an electrode. The monomers couple at the 2 and 5 positions, and the chain is oxidised (**p-doped**) with anions, giving a conjugated backbone with mobile charge carriers (conductivity up to about $10^3$ S/cm). *Uses:* sensors, rechargeable batteries and supercapacitors, antistatic coatings, EMI shielding, corrosion protection, artificial muscles and biomedical electrodes.

**(ii) Carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer (CFRP).** It is a composite with carbon fibres as the reinforcement and a polymer (usually epoxy) as the matrix. *Preparation:* the fibres are made from polyacrylonitrile by stabilisation in air (200–300°C), carbonisation in an inert gas (1000–1500°C), then optional graphitisation (2000–3000°C). The fibres (woven or unidirectional) are impregnated with epoxy resin, laid up and cured under heat and pressure. *Uses:* aircraft and spacecraft structures, racing cars, bicycles and sports goods (rackets, golf clubs), wind turbine blades, prosthetics. They are light, very strong and stiff, and resistant to corrosion.

**(iii) Chitin.** Chitin is a natural biopolymer, poly($\beta$-(1→4)-N-acetyl-D-glucosamine), the second most abundant polysaccharide, found in the shells of crabs and shrimps, in insect exoskeletons and in fungal cell walls. It is extracted by demineralisation (HCl) and deproteinisation (NaOH). Deacetylation with strong NaOH gives **chitosan**. *Uses:* wound dressings and surgical sutures, drug delivery, water purification (adsorbs heavy metals and dyes), biodegradable packaging films, cosmetics, agriculture (seed coatings).

@s 10
**(i) Lubricant.** A lubricant is a substance (oil, grease, solid or gas) introduced between two moving surfaces to reduce friction and wear between them.

**Functions of a lubricant:**
1. It reduces **friction** and wear by forming a film between the surfaces, and so saves energy.
2. It acts as a **coolant**, carrying away the heat generated.
3. It prevents **corrosion** and rust of the surfaces.
4. It acts as a **seal** (e.g. between piston rings and cylinder) and prevents the entry of dust and water.
5. It carries away dirt and wear debris, and in some cases transmits power (hydraulic oil).
6. It avoids seizing and noise, and increases the life of the machine.

**(ii) TNT (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene).**
*Preparation:* by the **stepwise nitration of toluene** with a mixture of concentrated HNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄ (the acid supplies the nitronium ion $\text{NO}_2^+$ and absorbs the water formed). Three stages with increasing severity:
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_3\xrightarrow[\ \text{mixed acid, 30–50°C}\ ]{\text{HNO}_3/\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4}\text{mono-nitrotoluene}\xrightarrow[\ 60–80^\circ\text{C}\ ]{\text{HNO}_3/\text{oleum}}\text{dinitrotoluene}\xrightarrow[\ 100–115^\circ\text{C}\ ]{\text{HNO}_3/\text{oleum}}\text{2,4,6-trinitrotoluene}$$
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_3 + 3\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4}\text{C}_6\text{H}_2(\text{NO}_2)_3\text{CH}_3 + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The crude product is washed with water, and the unsymmetrical isomers are removed with aqueous sodium sulphite ("sellite") to give a pale-yellow crystalline solid (m.p. 81°C).

*Applications:* a high explosive, insensitive to shock and stable, so it is widely used in shells, bombs, grenades and torpedoes, usually mixed (amatol, with ammonium nitrate); in mining, quarrying and demolition; and as the standard for expressing explosive energy (1 kg of TNT = 4.2 MJ).
