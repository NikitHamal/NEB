@s 1
WASH = **Water, Sanitation and Hygiene**. Water is the common base of all three:

- **Water:** safe drinking water prevents the water-borne diseases (diarrhoea, typhoid, cholera, hepatitis A) and gives the **quantity** for drinking, cooking and cleaning (a minimum of 45 lpcd in rural Nepal, 20 lpcd at the basic level);
- **Sanitation:** water carries the wastes (the flush toilet, the sewer) and is needed for the cleaning and the operation of the sanitation facilities; safe water supply must be protected from the faecal contamination from the latrines;
- **Hygiene:** hand-washing with soap, bathing, washing clothes, food hygiene and menstrual hygiene all need an adequate and accessible water;
- it also reduces the **time burden of water collection** (mainly of women and girls), improves the school attendance and the health-care facilities, and supports the livelihoods, nutrition and the SDG 6 (clean water and sanitation).

@s 2
**Technical considerations:** the **quantity** (the dependable yield $\ge$ the design demand), the **quality** (the turbidity, the chemicals, the pathogens and the need of treatment), the **location and elevation** (the gravity flow is preferred, the distance and the head), the reliability (the perennial source), the ease of the intake, the **cost** of the development and the operation, and the safety against the flood, the landslide and the pollution. Example: a perennial spring on the hill above the village (gravity, good quality) is preferred to a polluted river that needs pumping and treatment.

**Legal considerations:** the **water rights and the ownership** (the Water Resources Act 2049 and its Regulation; the priority of the drinking water), the permission or the licence for the use of the source, the **downstream users' rights and the environmental (minimum) flow**, the land acquisition and the right of way for the intake and the pipes, the **Drinking Water Quality Standard 2062**, the protected area and the forest rules and the local government's consent. Example: a spring used by a neighbouring village already requires an agreement with the users and a share of the discharge.

@s 3
- **Base period:** the period of the **past** records (the census data of the previous decades) used to project the trend of the population and the demand: usually the last 3 to 5 censuses (the basis of the forecast). *Purpose:* to determine the **growth rate** and the **per-capita demand trend**.
- **Design period:** the number of **future years** for which the system is designed, i.e. the time from the commissioning to the time when the capacity is fully used: intake, dams and the treatment plant 15 to 30 years; the pipes, the reservoirs and the buildings 20 to 30 (50 for the dam); the pump and the mechanical parts 10 to 15 years; in Nepal, 15 to 20 years for the rural schemes, and 20 to 25 years for the urban. *Purpose:* to determine the **design population and the design flow**, the **economic** selection of the capacity (the interest rate, the life of the components, and the ease of the staging and expansion).

@s 4
**Health:** the temperature affects the **palatability** (the best at 10 to 15 °C; warm water is flat and the cold water is chilling), the growth of the **pathogens and the algae** (the optimum 20 to 40 °C; the Legionella and the naegleria in warm water) and the **dissolved oxygen** (gas solubility falls); the hot water causes scalding.

**Environment:** the higher temperature **reduces the dissolved oxygen** and increases the **metabolic rate of aquatic life, the algal bloom, the eutrophication, the BOD decay rate** (the thermal pollution), and the stratification of lakes and the reservoirs.

**Engineering:** it changes the **viscosity and density of water**, which controls the **settling velocity** ($v_s\propto1/\nu$; colder water settles slowly, the tank must be larger), the **filtration**, the flocculation and the coagulation efficiency (slow in the cold), the **chlorine and the other disinfection rate** (the rate $k$ rises $2$ to $3$ times for 10 °C), the chemical dissolution and the scaling (the hardness precipitates), the corrosion rate, the pipe expansion and the head loss ($C$ and $\nu$), and the **water demand** (higher in the hot weather).

@s 5
**Advanced oxidation process (AOP).** A group of treatment processes that generate highly reactive, non-selective **hydroxyl radicals ($\cdot$OH, E° = 2.8 V)** *in situ*, which oxidise the organic and the micro-pollutants that resist the ordinary oxidation, e.g. $\text{O}_3/\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$, $\text{UV}/\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$, $\text{UV}/\text{O}_3$, the Fenton ($\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$), the photocatalysis ($\text{TiO}_2$/UV). They destroy the pesticides, the pharmaceuticals, the dyes, the taste and the odour compounds, and the emerging pollutants, ideally to $\text{CO}_2$ and water.

**Disinfection kinetics.** The rate at which the microorganisms are inactivated by a disinfectant. **Chick's law** (first order): $\dfrac{dN}{dt}=-kN$, so $\ln\dfrac{N}{N_0}=-kt$; **Chick-Watson law**: $\ln\dfrac{N}{N_0}=-kC^nt$ ($C$ the disinfectant concentration, $n$ the coefficient of dilution; $n=1$ gives the $Ct$ concept). The rate rises with the temperature (Arrhenius or the van't Hoff), and falls with the turbidity, the pH (for the chlorine) and the organism's resistance; used to find the **contact time** for the required log kill.

@s 6
A **smart water distribution system** combines the conventional water network with **sensors, smart (AMR/AMI) meters, remote-controlled valves and pumps, communication networks (IoT, SCADA) and data analytics (GIS, hydraulic models, AI)** to monitor and to control the supply in real time.

*Main features:* (1) **real-time monitoring** of the flow, pressure, level and the water quality (the chlorine, turbidity, pH); (2) **smart metering** with automatic reading and the billing and the consumption analytics; (3) **leak and burst detection** and the **non-revenue water** reduction, with the **DMAs**; (4) **pressure management** with the PRVs and the variable-speed pumps (the energy saving); (5) **remote control and automation** of the pumps, valves and the reservoirs; (6) **hydraulic modelling and the digital twin** for the planning and the forecast of the demand; (7) the **alarms, the early warning and the customer interface** (the mobile applications), the data-based asset management.

@s 7
**Flushing arrangements** are the **washout (blow-off) valves** on the pipelines, normally at the low points and the dead ends, and the **hydrants** in the distribution network. They are **practical and necessary** because:

- the **sediment, silt, sand and the corrosion products settle** in the low points and the dead ends of the network, and the stagnant water loses the chlorine residual and gets the taste and the bacterial growth; the periodic flushing (at velocity 1 to 1.5 m/s, the monthly) removes them;
- the **new or repaired pipes** must be flushed and disinfected before commissioning;
- the pipe can be **emptied** through the washout for repairs, and the contaminated water can be removed after an incident;
- practical points: the valve (gate, size 50 to 100 mm) in a chamber with a drain, the discharge into a drain or a soak pit (not into the surrounding to avoid the pit erosion and the contamination), in the hilly Nepal at each low point of the transmission main (together with the air valves at the high points), and the **community operation** as a routine of the caretaker (the village maintenance worker). The disadvantages are the loss of water (to be limited) and the need for a good drainage.

@s 8
**Water (hydrologic) cycle.** The continuous circulation of water between the oceans, the atmosphere and the land: **evaporation** (from the oceans, lakes and the soil), **transpiration** (plants), **condensation** (clouds), **precipitation** (rain, snow), **interception, infiltration, percolation, surface runoff and the groundwater flow**, and the return to the sea by the rivers. It is driven by the solar energy and gravity, and fixes the renewable water available to a region.

**Climate change threats to the water availability in the Terai.**

1. **Altered monsoon:** the intense and erratic rainfall, with the floods in the monsoon and the long dry periods in the pre-monsoon and the winter, so the **recharge efficiency** is reduced and the surface water is irregular;
2. **Changing flows of the snow- and glacier-fed Himalayan rivers** (the early peak because of the melting, the later decline) that supply the Terai rivers and the recharge;
3. **Falling groundwater table** by the higher **evapotranspiration and the heavy pumping** (the shallow tube wells go dry in the dry season, the deep aquifer is mined) with the higher temperature;
4. **Flood, sedimentation and the contamination:** the floods cause **the inundation of the tube wells and the latrines, the contamination by pathogens**, the damaged intakes, and a high turbidity; the **arsenic** and the salinity (the salinity intrusion is less in Nepal, arsenic is present in the shallow groundwater of many Terai districts);
5. increased demand (temperature, population, irrigation) and the competition among the users, and the drought and the heat wave.

*Adaptation:* the groundwater recharge and conservation, the rainwater harvesting, the protected deep wells, the flood-proof platforms and the intakes, and the integrated water-resources management.

@s 9
Population forecasting estimates the design population for the design period. The methods and their suitability:

| Method | Formula | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|
| **Arithmetic increase** | $P_n=P_0+n\bar x$ | **old, large cities** that have reached near saturation, with a low and constant growth; gives the lowest value |
| **Geometric increase** | $P_n=P_0(1+r)^n$ | **growing towns and the new cities** with the unlimited area and resources; the most commonly used in Nepal; gives a high value |
| **Incremental increase** | $P_n=P_0+n\bar x+\dfrac{n(n+1)}2\bar y$ | **average and medium cities** with a changing increment (both the rise and the fall of the growth rate are included) |
| **Decreasing rate of growth** | $r$ falls linearly | cities near the **saturation** (the growth rate declines) |
| **Logistic (S-curve)** | $P=\dfrac{P_s}{1+e^{a+bt}}$ | a **large city** growing in the three phases (the early, the log and the saturation), the best for the long period with the data |
| **Graphical comparison / ratio and correlation** | the curve of similar cities, or the ratio with the national or the district population | the cities with the **limited data**, or the **new development** that follows other cities |
| **Master plan / zoning (the land-use) method** | the density $\times$ the area | the **planned** cities (the future land use is known) |
| **Component (the cohort) method** | the birth, the death, the migration | where the **migration** is important and the data of the age structure are available |

The most suitable method is chosen by comparing the results of several methods and the judgment on the local conditions (the Census 2078, the growth, the migration, the planning of the new industry). In Nepal the geometric method with the census growth rate is common for the rural schemes, and the incremental and the logistic methods are used for the urban projects.

@s 10
Total hardness $TH=410$ mg/L as CaCO$_3$, carbonate hardness $CH=105$ mg/L as CaCO$_3$.

**Calcium and magnesium (as CaCO$_3$).** Hardness is caused by calcium and magnesium: $TH=\text{Ca}+\text{Mg}$, and $\text{Ca}=1.5\,\text{Mg}$:

$$\begin{gathered}2.5\,\text{Mg}=410\ \Rightarrow\ \text{Mg}=\mathbf{164\ mg/L}\ \text{as CaCO}_3 \\ \text{Ca}=1.5\times164=\mathbf{246\ mg/L}\ \text{as CaCO}_3\end{gathered}$$

In terms of the ions: $\text{Ca}^{2+}=246\times\dfrac{40.08}{100.09}=98.5$ mg/L and $\text{Mg}^{2+}=164\times\dfrac{24.31}{100.09}=39.8$ mg/L.

**Total alkalinity.** Carbonate hardness is the part of the hardness that is balanced by the alkalinity: $CH=\min(TH,\ \text{alkalinity})$. Here $CH=105<TH=410$, so the whole alkalinity is balanced by the hardness and the carbonate hardness equals the alkalinity:

$$\text{Alkalinity}=\mathbf{105\ mg/L\ as\ CaCO_3}$$

The **non-carbonate hardness** $=410-105=305$ mg/L as CaCO$_3$ (the calcium and the magnesium combined with the chloride and the sulphate, which is not removed by boiling and which needs the lime-soda softening: the soda ash is needed for 305 mg/L). The water is **very hard** (above 300 mg/L).

@s 11
**Design considerations for a river intake.** The intake is to give a safe, adequate and good-quality water throughout the year, so I would select:

1. **Location:** at a **stable, straight reach (or the concave outer bank of a bend, where the water is deep and the scour keeps the intake clear)** and **upstream** of the town and of the discharge of the sewage, the industry and the settlements; away from the sand bars, the confluences and the unstable banks; a place with a **good depth even in the dry season**, and a natural rock foundation; on the **concave bank** where the sediment is less (not at the convex side, where the silt is deposited).
2. **Level (the intake opening)** at a **level 0.5 to 1 m above the river bed** and below the minimum water level (the multi-level ports at the different depths, to give a choice of the best water quality, the floating matter and the turbidity), to keep out the bed load and the floating debris; with the **coarse screen (the bar rack), the fine screen**, a trash rack and a sluice gate.
3. **Hydraulic and structural safety:** the intake structure above the **highest flood level (HFL)** with the freeboard; protected by the **guide walls, the spurs and the training works** against the flood, the scour and the debris; the foundation below the scour depth; the velocity of the entry 0.15 to 0.3 m/s (to avoid the entry of the sediment, and the fish).
4. **Quantity and quality:** the dependable yield in the **lowest flow** is more than the demand and the environmental flow is kept; the **sedimentation (the settling basin or the sand trap)** at the intake and the arrangement for the flushing; the **protection against the pollution** (the fence, the buffer).
5. **Operation and maintenance, the economy and the environment:** easy access in the flood, the power supply (for the pump), the cost, the effect on the fish, the ecology and the downstream users, and the permission.

*Reason:* to ensure the **reliability** (the supply in the dry season and the safety in the flood), the **quality** (the less treatment cost), the **durability** (the stability of the structure) and the least **environmental** damage.

@s 12
**Coagulation** is the **destabilisation of the colloidal particles** (turbidity, colour, the bacteria, 1 nm to 1 $\mu$m) by the addition of the chemical (the coagulant) with the **rapid mixing** (the velocity gradient $G=300$ to $1000\ \text{s}^{-1}$ for 30 to 60 s), so that they can aggregate.

*Mechanisms:* (i) **double-layer compression** (the counter-ions of the high charge reduce the repulsion); (ii) **adsorption and charge neutralisation** by the hydrolysed metal species ($\text{Al(OH)}_2^+$, $\text{Fe(OH)}_2^+$) on the negatively charged colloid; (iii) **sweep coagulation (enmeshment)** in the precipitate of $\text{Al(OH)}_3$ or $\text{Fe(OH)}_3$ at the higher dose and the pH 6.5 to 7.5; (iv) **inter-particle bridging** by the polymers.

*Example (alum):*

$$\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3\cdot14\text{H}_2\text{O}+3\,\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2\ \to\ 2\,\text{Al(OH)}_3\downarrow+3\,\text{CaSO}_4+6\,\text{CO}_2+14\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

(1 mg/L of alum consumes about 0.5 mg/L alkalinity as CaCO$_3$).

**Flocculation** is the **gentle (the slow) mixing** ($G=20$ to $75\ \text{s}^{-1}$ for 20 to 40 min, the paddle or the baffled flocculator), which brings the destabilised particles into the contact (the **perikinetic** collision by the Brownian motion of the very small particles, and the **orthokinetic** collision by the velocity gradient) so that **flocs** (0.1 to 3 mm) grow and become big enough to settle; the flocculant aids (the polyelectrolyte) can give stronger flocs. The mixing must be gentle to avoid the shear that breaks up the flocs. The coagulation-flocculation is followed by the sedimentation and the filtration; the dose is found by the **jar test**.

@s 13
| | Granular **sand filter** (rapid or slow) | **Membrane filter** (MF, UF, NF, RO) |
|---|---|---|
| Medium | a bed of sand (0.45 to 1.0 mm) and gravel, 0.6 to 1 m deep | a thin **semi-permeable polymer or ceramic membrane** (pores 0.001 to 10 $\mu$m) |
| Driving force | gravity (the head 1 to 2 m) | the **pressure or the vacuum** (0.1 to 50 bar) |
| Removal mechanism | **depth filtration**: straining, sedimentation in the pores, interception, impaction, diffusion, adsorption and the attachment (the biological action in the slow sand filter, the schmutzdecke); the **coagulated** water is needed for the rapid filter | **surface sieving (size exclusion)** by the pore size; for NF and RO also the **charge repulsion, the solution-diffusion** of the dissolved ions |
| Pollutants removed | the suspended particles and turbidity (5 to 10 $\mu$m and above), the floc, a part of the bacteria (the slow sand 90 to 99 %), the iron and manganese (with oxidation) | the particles, the **bacteria, protozoa** (MF), the **viruses** (UF), the hardness and the organics (NF), the **dissolved salts, arsenic, fluoride, nitrate** (RO) |
| Rate | 5 to 6 m/h (rapid), 0.1 to 0.4 m/h (slow) | 0.02 to 0.1 m/h (flux $20$ to $100\ \text{L/m}^2\text{h}$) |
| Cleaning | the backwash (the rapid) or the scraping of the top layer (slow) | the backflushing, the chemical cleaning (CIP), the fouling control |
| Cost and energy | low, simple, the local material | high capital and energy, a skilled operation, the pre-treatment and the waste brine (RO) |

The sand filter is the cheap, robust choice for the turbidity and the bulk pathogen removal, and the membrane filter gives the absolute barrier and the removal of the dissolved pollutants; they are often used in series.

@s 14
**Break pressure tank (BPT).** A small open tank (the storage 1 to 3 m³ or the tank with the float valve) placed in a **gravity pipeline when the static head exceeds the pressure rating of the pipe** (about 60 m for the PE or the 6 kgf/cm² pipe, 100 m for the 10 kgf/cm² pipe), or in the pipeline running down a steep hill. The flow enters through the float (ball) valve and leaves from the outlet to the next pipe section at the **atmospheric pressure**, so that the **static head is reduced to zero** and the pipe is designed only for the head of the next section. **Locations:** along the transmission main at 40 to 60 m (and so on) below the previous BPT, before the reservoir or the tap stand in the steep hill, at the change of the pipe class; the **tank** gives also a point for the inspection, the flushing and the removal of air. For the large flow, the valve may be a **pressure-reducing valve** in place of the BPT.

**Interruption chamber (IC).** A small chamber of the same type, but **without the storage function**: it **interrupts the pipeline** to reduce the pressure, and gives the free-flow to the section downstream. It is used (a) **at the end of the tap-stand branch line, before the individual tap-stands** (the IC serves a **single branch, where the BPT is not needed**), (b) **where the pressure is too high at the tap**, (c) **before the sedimentation tank or the reservoir** on the hillside. The IC typically is small (the plan about 0.6 m by 0.6 m with the float valve or the free outlet); it is cheaper than the BPT, and it **cannot store water** (the BPT can).

*Difference:* the BPT has a **storage and the float valve** (the water level control, and it gives a buffer), while the IC has a free outflow with a small volume. **Both are not used where the pipe is pressurised** (the pumped main) and the pressure is needed to climb the next section. They are common in the **hilly rural gravity water supply** of Nepal, together with the air valve and the washout.

@s 15
**Settling velocity (Stokes' law, 20 °C: $\nu=1.003\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$, $S_s=2.65$).**

$$v_s=\frac{g(S_s-1)d^2}{18\nu}=\frac{9.81(1.65)(0.04\times10^{-3})^2}{18(1.003\times10^{-6})}=1.434\times10^{-3}\ \text{m/s}=\mathbf{123.9\ m/day}$$

Check: $Re=v_sd/\nu=0.057<1$, so Stokes' law holds.

**Ideal tank design.** To trap all the particles of diameter $\ge0.04$ mm, the overflow rate must be $v_0=Q/A\le v_s$. Take $v_0=v_s$:

$$Q=\frac{12\times10^6\ \text{L/d}}{1000}=12\,000\ \text{m}^3/\text{day}=0.1389\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$$

$$A=\frac{Q}{v_s}=\frac{12\,000}{123.9}=\mathbf{96.8\ m^2}$$

Take a depth of $H=3$ m (the usual 2.5 to 4 m) and **$L:B=4:1$** (the range 3 to 5):

$$\begin{gathered}B=\sqrt{96.8/4}=4.92\ \text{m}\to5.0\ \text{m} \\ L=\frac{96.8}{5.0}=19.4\ \text{m}\to20\ \text{m}\end{gathered}$$

Provided area $20\times5=100$ m² ($v_0=120$ m/day $<v_s$, so the efficiency for the 0.04 mm particles is 100 %); volume $=100\times3=300$ m³.

**Detention time.**

$$t_d=\frac VQ=\frac{300}{12\,000}\ \text{day}=0.025\ \text{day}=\mathbf{0.60\ h\ (36\ min)}$$

(the theoretical time $H/v_s=3/1.434\times10^{-3}=2091\ \text{s}=34.9$ min). With the allowance for the inlet and outlet zones (the sludge zone 0.5 m, freeboard 0.3 m and the short-circuiting), the actual tank is built about **20 m × 5 m × 3.8 m total depth**; the horizontal velocity $Q/(BH)=0.1389/(5\times3)=9.3$ mm/s is far below the scour velocity.

**Answer:** surface area 96.8 m² (**20 m × 5 m, depth 3 m**, $L:B=4:1$), detention time about **0.6 h (35 to 36 min)**, overflow rate 120 to 124 m³/m²/day.

@s 16
**Chick-Watson law:** $\ln\dfrac{N}{N_0}=-k\,C^n\,t$, with $n=1$: $\ln\dfrac{N_0}{N}=k\,C\,t$.

**Constant $k$ from the experiment.** 99 % reduction: $N/N_0=0.01$, $\ln(N_0/N)=\ln100=4.605$, $C=0.2$ g/m³, $t=10$ min:

$$k=\frac{4.605}{0.2\times10}=\mathbf{2.303\ m^3/(g\cdot min)}$$

**Time for 99.9 % at $C=0.04$ g/m³.** $\ln(N_0/N)=\ln1000=6.908$:

$$t=\frac{6.908}{k\,C}=\frac{6.908}{2.303\times0.04}=\mathbf{75\ min}$$

Check with $Ct$: $Ct$ for 99 % is $0.2\times10=2.0$ g·min/m³; for 99.9 % it is $\ln1000/\ln100\times2.0=3.0$ g·min/m³; so $t=3.0/0.04=75$ min. (A lower chlorine concentration of one fifth needs a 7.5 times longer contact time, a larger contact tank.)

@s 17
**Pumping (inflow to the reservoir).** The pumping hours: 3 to 9 (6 h) and 16 to 20 (4 h), total 10 h per day, so the pumping rate (the supply to the reservoir) is

$$Q_p=\frac{4000}{10}=400\ \text{m}^3/\text{h}$$

**Consumption (outflow from the reservoir).** The supply hours: 4 to 7 (3 h) and 16 to 18 (2 h), total 5 h, so the outflow rate is

$$Q_d=\frac{4000}{5}=800\ \text{m}^3/\text{h}$$

**Mass curve (hourly balance).** Cumulative inflow minus cumulative outflow:

| Time (h) | Inflow | Outflow | Net (m³) | Cumulative (m³) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to 3 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 3 to 4 | 400 | 0 | +400 | **+400** (max surplus) |
| 4 to 7 | 3 × 400 | 3 × 800 | 3 × (-400) | -800 (at 7 h) |
| 7 to 9 | 2 × 400 | 0 | +800 | 0 |
| 9 to 16 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| 16 to 18 | 2 × 400 | 2 × 800 | 2 × (-400) | -800 (at 18 h) |
| 18 to 20 | 2 × 400 | 0 | +800 | 0 |
| 20 to 24 | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 |
| **Total** | **4000** | **4000** | 0 | |

The largest cumulative surplus is $+400$ m³ (at 4 h, after the first hour of pumping when the supply starts) and the largest cumulative deficit is $-800$ m³ (at 7 h and at 18 h). The reservoir must hold the difference between the maximum surplus and the maximum deficit:

$$\text{Capacity}=400+800=\mathbf{1200\ m^3}\ (=30\ \%\ \text{of the daily demand})$$

Stored volume: if the reservoir holds $V_0=800$ m³ at midnight (the carry-over from the previous day, which is needed to meet the first draw-off), the volume is $800+400=1200$ m³ at 4 h (full), falls to $800-800=0$ at 7 h (empty) and is back to 800 m³ at 9 h; the same cycle occurs between 16 h and 20 h. The reservoir therefore just meets the demand with the capacity above. A fire or an emergency reserve (about one third of the total or the fire demand) would be added to this balancing volume.
