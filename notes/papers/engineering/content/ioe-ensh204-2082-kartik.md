@s 1
Any one is required; both are answered.

**(a) Technical communication.** Technical communication is the process of conveying **technical, scientific or specialised information** (about a product, process, system or research) **clearly, accurately and efficiently to a specific audience** so that they can use it to act, decide or learn. It is audience-centred, purpose-driven, fact-based and objective, and uses a precise language with visuals (tables, graphs, drawings) and a fixed format (reports, proposals, manuals, e-mails, memos, instructions).

*Scope for engineers.* An engineer spends a large part of the working day on communication, and the scope is wide:

- **Written:** technical reports (feasibility, progress, final), proposals, tender documents, specifications, project documentation, user manuals, data sheets, standards, letters, memos, e-mails, minutes, journal and conference papers, patents, résumés and job applications.
- **Oral:** meetings, presentations, project defences, briefings to clients and the public, telephone calls, interviews, group discussions.
- **Visual and electronic:** drawings, charts, dashboards, CAD models, slides, websites, social media and videos for demonstrations.
- **Directions of flow:** upward (to managers), downward (to technicians and workers), horizontal (between teams) and external (to clients, suppliers, government, community).
- **Importance:** it prevents costly errors and accidents, wins funding and contracts, shows professionalism, supports teamwork across disciplines and cultures, and is a key employability skill.

**(b) Plagiarism.** Plagiarism is using another person's words, ideas, data, images or work **without giving proper credit** (citation), and presenting them as your own. It is academic dishonesty and an ethical and often legal offence (copyright). Forms: copying directly, copy-paste, self-plagiarism (re-using one's own earlier work without acknowledgement), mosaic work, buying papers, false citation.

| Basis | Paraphrasing plagiarism | Patchwork (mosaic) plagiarism |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | restating someone's idea in your own words or with a few words changed, but **not crediting** the source | stitching together phrases, sentences or ideas **copied from several sources**, with small changes, into one text |
| Source | usually one passage / one source | two or more sources mixed together |
| Wording | sentence structure is changed or synonyms replace words; the *idea* is the original author's | pieces of the original wording are kept and joined with your own connecting words |
| What is wrong | the idea is not acknowledged | neither the wording nor the ideas are acknowledged; the result looks original |
| Example | "The use of renewable energy reduces pollution" $\to$ "Employing green power lowers contamination" (no citation) | a paragraph made of one sentence from a journal, one from a website and one from a book, lightly reworded, with no references |
| Remedy | cite the source even when paraphrasing (author, year) | write in your own structure, use quotation marks for exact words, cite every source |

| Biased term | Bias-free alternative |
|---|---|

| mailman | mail carrier / postal worker / letter carrier |
| freshman | first-year student / fresher |
| front man | spokesperson / representative / figurehead |
| working woman | working person / employee / professional |
| brotherhood | fellowship / solidarity / community / kinship |
| manhood | adulthood / maturity / humanity (for "mankind": humankind) |
| masterplan | master plan / comprehensive plan / grand plan (avoid "mastermind": planner, architect) |

| workmanship | quality of work / skill / craftsmanship (or "artisanship") |

*Rule:* replace *-man / -woman / -ess* job names by neutral titles (chairperson, firefighter), avoid generic "he" (use "they", "he or she", or the plural), and avoid stereotypes.

**(b) Coherent and concise writing.**

*Coherence* means that the ideas **hang together logically and flow smoothly**. Tips:

1. Plan and outline first; one paragraph = one main idea with a clear topic sentence.
2. Arrange ideas in a logical order (chronological, general to specific, cause to effect, problem to solution).
3. Use **transitions** (however, therefore, in addition, for example, finally) and pronouns / repeated key words to link sentences.
4. Keep a consistent point of view, tense and tone; use parallel structure for parallel ideas.
5. Use headings, lists and numbering in technical documents; revise and read aloud.

*Conciseness* means saying what is needed in the **fewest words without losing meaning**. Tips:

1. Cut redundant words and doubled expressions: *"return back"* $\to$ *return*; *"past history"* $\to$ *history*; *"in my own opinion I think"* $\to$ *I think*.
2. Replace wordy phrases by single words: *"due to the fact that"* $\to$ *because*; *"at this point in time"* $\to$ *now*; *"in order to"* $\to$ *to*.
3. Prefer the active voice and strong verbs to noun forms (*"make a decision"* $\to$ *decide*).
4. Avoid empty openers (*there is, it is*), unnecessary adverbs and jargon, and delete sentences that repeat earlier ones.
5. Use lists or tables for data; combine short related sentences.

**Does avoiding redundancy lead to concise writing?** Yes. Redundancy (repeating the same idea in different words, e.g. *"free gift"*, *"end result"*, *"each and every"*) adds words but no information, so removing it shortens the text and sharpens it. Note, however, that conciseness is not the same as brevity: planned repetition for emphasis, a summary or a definition may be kept, and cutting too far can make the text unclear. The aim is *no wasted words*, not *fewest words*.

**(c) Corrected sentences.**

| No. | Corrected sentence | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| i | He is here, **which** is fortunate. | relative pronoun *which* refers to the whole clause (non-restrictive) |
| ii | I played with the same bat **as** you did. | *same ... as* is the correct pair for a comparison of kind; (*that* is used when it is identical) |
| iii | More than one player **is** playing outside at present. | *more than one* takes a singular verb |
| iv | He is one of the best leaders who **have** served the nation perfectly. | *who* refers to the plural *leaders*, so the verb is plural |
| v | They were petrified, so **they could not speak** on hearing this news. | a bare infinitive *speak* follows *could not* (result clause with *so*) |
| vi | I had rather **take** this than that. | *had rather* is followed by a bare infinitive |
| vii | He asked **the reason for my coming**. | reduced to a simple sentence (one finite clause) |
| viii | There were many wars **while Queen Victoria reigned** (or *when Queen Victoria was the ruler*). | the phrase *during ... reign* becomes a subordinate clause, giving a complex sentence |
| ix | She is working hard with a view **to winning** this competition. | *with a view to* is followed by the *-ing* form (*to* is a preposition) |
| x | This is the same woman **that** attended the party last week. | *same* takes *that* (or *as*) as relative, not *who* |

**(c) Case-study report: cybersecurity breach (abstract, findings, discussion).**

> **ABSTRACT**<br>
> This report studies the ransomware attack of 2023 on a mid-sized regional hospital group (a typical case study). Attackers entered through a phishing e-mail, moved across the network, encrypted the patient-record servers and demanded a ransom in cryptocurrency. The hospitals worked on paper for eleven days, 1.2 million patient records were exposed and the direct and indirect cost was about USD 8 million. The report presents the main findings and discusses the lessons.

> **FINDINGS**<br>
> 1. The entry point was a phishing e-mail opened by an employee; there was no multi-factor authentication (MFA) on the remote access.<br>
> 2. The network was flat: once inside, the attackers moved freely and reached the servers within two days; the intrusion was not detected for 19 days.<br>
> 3. Software patches, including a known server vulnerability, were more than six months old.<br>
> 4. The backups were stored on the same network and were also encrypted, so recovery took 11 days.<br>
> 5. The consequences were: disruption of treatment, cancelled operations, leakage of personal and medical data, legal action, regulator fines, loss of public trust and a fall in revenue.

> **DISCUSSION**<br>
> The breach shows that technology and human behaviour are both weak links. Regular staff training in phishing awareness, MFA, timely patching, network segmentation and monitoring would have stopped or limited the attack. Offline, tested backups and an incident response plan shorten the recovery. Paying the ransom does not guarantee the return of data and encourages further crime. For engineers it means that security must be designed in from the beginning (security by design), with the principle of least privilege and a culture of reporting incidents. A similar attack can happen to any organisation, including banks and government offices in Nepal, which is why the Electronic Transactions Act and the data-protection rules must be followed.

@s 5
**(a) Non-verbal communication.** Non-verbal communication is the sending and receiving of messages **without words**, through body movements, face, voice quality, space, time, appearance and objects. It can repeat, replace, emphasise or contradict the spoken message, shows feelings and attitudes, and is largely unconscious; when words and gestures conflict, people believe the non-verbal signal.

*Elements (types).*

1. **Kinesics (body language):** posture, gestures, head nods, hand movements, walking style. Open posture shows confidence; folded arms may show defensiveness.
2. **Facial expressions:** smile, frown, raised eyebrows show happiness, anger, surprise; the face is the most expressive channel.
3. **Eye contact (oculesics):** direct contact shows attention and honesty; avoiding it may show shyness or lying; staring may threaten.
4. **Paralanguage (vocalics):** tone, pitch, volume, speed, pauses and sounds like "hmm" (how something is said).
5. **Proxemics (use of space):** intimate (0-45 cm), personal (0.5-1.2 m), social (1.2-3.5 m) and public distance (over 3.5 m); seating arrangement.
6. **Haptics (touch):** handshake, pat on the back; meanings vary with culture and relationship.
7. **Chronemics (use of time):** punctuality, waiting time, speed of reply.
8. **Appearance and artefacts:** dress, grooming, hairstyle, accessories, an office's layout; important in interviews and professional settings.
9. **Silence and signs/symbols:** silence can show respect, thought or disagreement; signs, colours, logos and sirens carry messages.

**(b) Group discussion (GD).** A group discussion is a **systematic and purposeful oral exchange of ideas among a small group (usually 6-12 persons) on a topic**, to reach a conclusion, to solve a problem or to take a decision, without a fixed leader. It is used in recruitment and admission tests to assess communication, leadership, knowledge, listening, teamwork and attitude.

*Techniques for participating effectively.*

1. **Prepare:** read widely on current, technical and social topics; know the facts and figures.
2. **Understand the topic** first; use the first few moments to think; if possible, **initiate** with a short, relevant opening (definition, fact or question), but only when you are well prepared.
3. **Listen actively** to the others; take short notes; build on their points ("I agree with Ram's point and I add...").
4. **Speak clearly and briefly:** use simple, correct language, a moderate volume and speed, and give one point at a time with an example or data.
5. **Be polite and respectful:** do not interrupt or dominate, disagree politely, avoid personal remarks and arguing; invite the quiet members to speak.
6. **Use positive body language:** eye contact with everybody, an alert posture, natural gestures; sit properly.
7. **Stay on the topic** and bring the group back if it drifts; keep to time.
8. **Show leadership and teamwork:** guide, summarise and, if needed, **conclude** the discussion with a balanced summary of the group's views.
9. **Stay calm and confident,** do not panic if you have nothing new; quality is better than quantity.

**(b) Importance of visual aids in technical communication.** Visual aids are graphical and physical items (tables, charts, graphs, diagrams, drawings, photographs, maps, flow charts, slides, models, videos, posters) that support the text or speech.

1. **Simplify complex data:** a graph shows the trend in one look where a page of numbers cannot; tables arrange exact values compactly.
2. **Save time and words:** a drawing or flow chart explains a process or a circuit faster and more precisely than a long description.
3. **Better understanding and retention:** people remember about 65% of what they see compared with about 10% of what they only hear; visuals reduce the chance of misunderstanding, also for readers of other languages.
4. **Emphasise key points:** colour, size and position direct the attention to the most important result.
5. **Show relationships and comparisons:** bar and pie graphs for comparison and proportion, line graphs for change, organisation charts for hierarchy, Gantt charts for schedules.
6. **Attract and hold the audience's attention** in presentations, make the document attractive and professional, and make the audience more persuaded.
7. **Support decisions:** managers and clients can quickly find the facts needed, e.g. from a dashboard.
8. **Show things that words cannot** (a machine part, a site layout, microscope images).

*Cautions:* choose the right type of visual, keep it simple, label it (title, units, legend, source), refer to it in the text, number it and do not distort the scale.
