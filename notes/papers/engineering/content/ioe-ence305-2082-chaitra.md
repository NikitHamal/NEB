@s 1
**WASH** = **W**ater, **S**anitation and **H**ygiene. **Sanitation** (the safe collection, containment, transport, treatment and disposal or reuse of human excreta and wastewater) is the barrier that **breaks the faecal–oral route** of diseases (diarrhoea, cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A, worms) and is therefore the **core of WASH**: without it, safe water is re-contaminated and hygiene (hand-washing) fails. Its significance: it **protects the water sources and the environment** from the pollution, **reduces the child mortality, the malnutrition and the stunting** (environmental enteropathy), improves the health and the productivity, the **dignity, safety and the school attendance of girls and women**, saves the health costs and supports the **SDG 6.2** (adequate and equitable sanitation for all, end of open defecation, the Nepal ODF campaign).

@s 2
- **Dry weather flow (DWF):** the flow in a sewer in the **absence of the rainfall** (or the 7 days after the rain): the **domestic sewage + industrial / commercial wastewater + the ground water infiltration**. It is the **base load** used to design the treatment plant and to check the minimum velocity.
- **Wet weather flow (WWF):** the flow **during and just after the rain**: the DWF **plus the storm-water runoff** (in a combined sewer) or the **inflow and the extra infiltration** through the manholes, the cracks and the illegal connections (in a separate sewer). It is many times larger than the DWF, and the combined sewers are designed for it (storm regulators pass the excess, above about 6 × DWF, to the stream).

@s 3
Based on the **carbon source** the microorganisms are:

1. **Autotrophs:** use **inorganic carbon (CO₂ / HCO₃⁻)** to build the cells: *photoautotrophs* (algae, cyanobacteria; the energy from the light) and *chemoautotrophs* (nitrifying bacteria *Nitrosomonas*, *Nitrobacter*, sulphur and iron bacteria; the energy from the oxidation of inorganic compounds).
2. **Heterotrophs:** use **organic carbon** (the organic matter of the wastewater): *chemoheterotrophs* (most bacteria, fungi, protozoa, the activated sludge organisms) and *photoheterotrophs* (the purple non-sulphur bacteria; the energy from the light).
3. (**Mixotrophs** use both the organic and the inorganic carbon.)

@s 4
A **storm water regulator (overflow / relief structure, the weir or the siphon type)** is built in a **combined sewer** at the junction with the overflow channel. It lets the flow up to the set limit (usually 3 to 6 times the DWF) go on to the intercepting sewer and the treatment plant and **diverts the excess storm flow** (already diluted) to the nearby stream or the drain. Hence it is used because it

- **prevents the surcharge and the flooding** of the downstream sewer and of the treatment plant, so the **size and the cost** of the downstream sewer and the plant are much **reduced** (they are designed for about 6 DWF, not the full storm flow);
- protects the treatment processes from the **hydraulic shock and the washout**;
- the diverted overflow is **highly diluted** by the rain (and the first flush is retained), so the pollution of the receiving water is acceptable;
- the structure also holds back the floating matter (the baffle/scum board) and the sewer solids.

@s 5
In $y_t = L\,(1 - e^{-Kt})$ the **BOD rate constant $K$** (at 20 °C) controls the **speed at which the organic matter is oxidised** (the BOD exerted in time $t$); the ultimate BOD $L$ is fixed by the amount of the biodegradable matter.

- **High $K$** (e.g. $K \approx 0.23$ d⁻¹ base *e* or 0.4 d⁻¹ for the simple sugars and raw domestic sewage): the matter is **readily biodegradable**, the BOD is exerted **rapidly**, most of the ultimate BOD is exerted in 5 days (BOD₅/BOD$_u$ is about 0.68 to 0.8), and the stream's oxygen is depleted **quickly and near the discharge point**.
- **Low $K$** (e.g. 0.05 to 0.1 d⁻¹ for the treated effluent, the industrial or the refractory / slowly degradable matter): the oxidation is **slow** (BOD₅ is only a small part, 0.3 to 0.4, of BOD$_u$, so BOD₅ under-estimates the real oxygen demand), and the oxygen sag is **spread out and moves far downstream**.

$K$ also rises with the temperature, $K_T = K_{20}\,1.047^{\,T-20}$.

@s 6
The **sequencing batch reactor (SBR)** is a fill-and-draw activated sludge process in which **all the steps occur in the same tank in sequence** (the cycle of 4 to 24 h):

1. **Fill:** the raw wastewater enters the tank containing the settled biomass from the previous cycle (static, mixed or aerated fill); the substrate is adsorbed and the selector effect builds the good flocs.
2. **React (aeration):** the mixing and the aeration oxidise the BOD and the ammonia (the nitrification; the anoxic phase for the denitrification).
3. **Settle (sedimentation):** the aeration and the mixing stop, the sludge settles under the quiescent conditions (the perfect clarifier).
4. **Decant (draw):** the clear supernatant is withdrawn by the floating decanter.
5. **Idle:** the waiting time (the excess sludge is wasted, the tank is ready for the next cycle).

@s 7
"**10 % sludge**" means a sludge with a **solids content of 10 %** by weight (the **moisture content 90 %**): 100 kg of the wet sludge contains 10 kg of the dry solids and 90 kg of the water. Since the sludge density is about that of water, the volume is **inversely proportional to the solids content**:

$$ \frac{V_1}{V_2} = \frac{P_2}{P_1} $$

*Example:* 100 kg of dry solids per day in a 10 % sludge is 1000 kg of the wet sludge, i.e. about **1 m³/day**. If the same sludge were at 2 % solids it would be 5000 kg = 5 m³/day, and thickening from 2 % to 10 % reduces the volume to one-fifth. (Raw primary sludge is typically 4 to 6 %, the digested sludge 8 to 12 %, the dewatered cake 20 to 30 %.)

@s 8
The time of concentration at the outlet of the storm sewer is the **inlet time plus the time of flow in the sewer**:

$$\begin{gathered}t_c = t_i + t_f \\ t_f = \frac{L}{v} = \frac{600\ \text{m}}{1.0\ \text{m/s}} = 600\ \text{s} = 10\ \text{min}\end{gathered}$$

$$ \boxed{t_c = 8 + 10 = 18\ \text{minutes}} $$

*Interpretation:* 18 minutes is the **time taken by the water from the farthest point of the catchment to reach the outlet**: the whole catchment contributes to the peak discharge only if the rain lasts at least 18 min. So the **design rainfall intensity** for the rational method $Q = CiA/360$ must be read from the intensity–duration–frequency curve for the **duration 18 min**; a longer sewer or a flatter slope (a lower velocity) raises $t_c$, which gives a **lower design intensity** and a smaller peak. Of the 18 min, 10 min (56 %) is the in-sewer travel time, which is the part that can be changed by the design (the slope and the velocity).

@s 9
**Bacterial metabolism** is the sum of the chemical reactions in which the bacteria obtain the energy and the carbon for the growth, and it is the basis of the biological treatment. It has two parts:

- **Catabolism:** the **oxidation (breakdown) of the organic matter** to release the energy, which is stored in ATP. About one-third of the substrate is oxidised.
- **Anabolism (synthesis):** the energy is used to **build the new cells** from about two-thirds of the substrate (C, N, P and the trace nutrients).

**Types of the metabolism** (by the final electron acceptor):

1. **Aerobic respiration** (O₂ is the electron acceptor, the activated sludge, the trickling filter): 

$$ \text{COHNS (organics)} + \text{O}_2 + \text{nutrients} \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{NH}_3 + \text{new cells (C}_5\text{H}_7\text{NO}_2) + \text{energy} $$

2. **Anoxic respiration** (NO₃⁻ as the acceptor, with no free O₂): **denitrification**, $\text{NO}_3^- \rightarrow \text{N}_2$, so the organics are oxidised and the nitrogen is removed.
3. **Anaerobic respiration and fermentation** (no O₂ or NO₃⁻; the acceptors are SO₄²⁻, CO₂ or the organics): **hydrolysis → acidogenesis → acetogenesis → methanogenesis**, giving the CH₄ + CO₂ biogas and little new cells (about 10 %), with a low sludge yield.
4. **Endogenous respiration:** when the substrate is short, the cells oxidise their own cell mass, $\text{C}_5\text{H}_7\text{NO}_2 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 5\text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{NH}_3 + \text{energy}$, which reduces the sludge production.

**Waste removal mechanism:** (i) the suspended and colloidal matter is **bio-flocculated and adsorbed** on the sticky floc (the extracellular polymers); (ii) the **extracellular enzymes hydrolyse** the complex organics into the small soluble molecules; (iii) these are taken up through the cell wall and **oxidised (catabolism) or assimilated (anabolism)**, which converts the soluble BOD into CO₂, H₂O (or CH₄) and the **biomass**; (iv) the biomass, now settleable, is **removed by sedimentation** as the sludge (a part is returned, the excess is wasted), which leaves the clarified effluent with low BOD.

**Operational problems of the activated sludge process and the remedies.**

| Problem | Cause | Remedy |
|---|---|---|
| **Sludge bulking** (high SVI > 150 mL/g, the sludge does not settle; the filamentous organisms, e.g. *Sphaerotilus*, *Microthrix*) | low DO, low F/M, nutrient (N, P) deficiency, low pH, septic or high-sulphide sewage, long SRT | raise the DO (2 mg/L), the selector / the anoxic zone, increase the F/M (waste more sludge), add N and P, chlorinate or add $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ to the return sludge (5 to 10 mg/L), correct the pH, pre-aerate the septic wastewater, polymer addition |
| **Rising sludge (floating sludge)** | **denitrification in the clarifier** (nitrified sludge, N₂ bubbles lift the flocs) | increase the return sludge rate and the withdrawal, reduce the sludge blanket depth, lower the SRT or the aeration to stop the nitrification, or do the denitrification in a proper anoxic zone |
| **Foaming (the brown, thick *Nocardia* foam)** | grease and oil, long SRT, the filamentous *Nocardia* and *Microthrix* | grease trap, the surface skimming, reduce the SRT (waste more), spray the foam with the chlorine water, anti-foam, selectors |
| **Pin floc / dispersed growth** (the turbid effluent) | too high SRT (over-oxidised), toxic or shock load, low F/M | increase the wasting (reduce the SRT / the MLSS), remove the toxic source, equalise the load |
| **Slime / viscous bulking** | nutrient deficiency (the polysaccharide production) | add N and P (BOD : N : P = 100 : 5 : 1) |
| **Washout of the solids (the high effluent SS)** | hydraulic overload, the short-circuiting in the clarifier, the high MLSS and sludge blanket | flow equalisation, step-feed or the storm mode, control the MLSS, clarifier baffles and the weir levelling, the sludge withdrawal |
| **Low DO / the oxygen deficiency, odour** | aerator failure, overload, the high temperature, the fouled diffusers | maintain and clean the aerators / diffusers, add the aeration capacity, reduce the load |
| **Nitrification failure, low pH, toxicity** | low SRT, low alkalinity, toxic industrial discharge | raise the SRT, add alkalinity (lime), control the industrial discharges |

@s 12
The **anaerobic baffled reactor (ABR)** is a rectangular tank divided by **vertical baffles** into several compartments in series; the wastewater is forced **down and then up** (under and over the baffles) so that it flows through the **sludge blanket in each compartment** at a low up-flow velocity (< 1 to 2 m/h). The **sludge (granules and the flocs) is retained** inside, so the solids retention time is long (more than 100 days) while the hydraulic retention time is only 12 to 48 h. Removal mechanisms:

1. **Sedimentation and filtration:** the suspended solids settle in the first compartment (the settler / the hopper) and are **trapped by the sludge blanket** at each up-flow stage (the filtering action).
2. **Adsorption and entrapment** of the colloidal and the dissolved organics on the active anaerobic sludge.
3. **Anaerobic biological degradation** by the sludge in the compartments, with the **separation of the phases along the reactor**: the first compartments are dominated by the **hydrolytic and acidogenic** bacteria (the high load, the low pH), the later ones by the **acetogens and methanogens** (the low load, the neutral pH): $\text{organics} \rightarrow \text{VFAs} \rightarrow \text{CH}_4 + \text{CO}_2$. The BOD is removed as the biogas, not as the oxygen use.
4. The treated effluent leaves with the **BOD removal of about 65 to 90 %** and SS removal 70 to 90 %, the stabilised sludge accumulates and is **desludged every 1 to 3 years**, the **biogas is collected** (the gas vent) and the effluent is usually polished in a planted gravel filter or a pond (decentralised wastewater treatment, DEWATS). Pathogen and nutrient removal is small.

@s 13
A well-working anaerobic sludge digester is judged by the **monitoring of the indicators** and their trends (the process is slow, so the early warning is essential):

1. **Biogas production and composition:** the steady gas yield (about 0.75 to 1.1 m³ per kg of volatile solids destroyed, 15 to 18 m³/1000 persons/d), the **methane content 60 to 70 %** and CO₂ 30 to 40 %. A fall of the gas, a **rising CO₂ percentage** (or the CH₄ below 55 %) shows the upset.
2. **pH:** **6.8 to 7.4**; a falling pH means the acid accumulation (the methanogens are inhibited below 6.5).
3. **Volatile acids (VFA) and alkalinity:** VFA < 200 to 300 mg/L as acetic acid, **alkalinity 2000 to 5000 mg/L as CaCO₃** and the **VFA/alkalinity ratio < 0.1 to 0.3**; a ratio above about 0.4 warns of the souring.
4. **Volatile solids reduction:** 40 to 60 % VS destruction (the stabilisation), and the sludge, when drained, is dark, granular, tarry-smelling but not offensive, and dries well.
5. **Temperature** steady at the design value (the mesophilic 30 to 37 °C; thermophilic 50 to 55 °C; the fluctuation less than 1 °C/day), good mixing and the **loading rate** (the VS loading 1.6 to 3.2 kg/m³/d, the detention 15 to 30 days, the feed not shocked).
6. **Supernatant and the digested sludge quality** (BOD, SS and the ammonia in the supernatant, the sludge dewaterability), the **absence of the foaming, the scum, the toxicity** (heavy metals, sulphide, the ammonia, the H₂S in the gas < 1 %).

If the indicators are out of range (low pH, high VFA/alk): reduce or stop the feed, add alkalinity (lime, sodium bicarbonate), keep the temperature, and check for the toxicants.

@s 14
A **septic tank** is a buried, watertight, usually **two-chamber** rectangular tank (the length : the width = 2 to 3 : 1, the liquid depth 1.0 to 2.0 m) for the on-site treatment of the household blackwater and greywater, with a detention of **1 to 3 days**. Working mechanism:

1. **Sedimentation:** the sewage enters through the **inlet tee (dip pipe)** which prevents the disturbance of the scum, the flow becomes quiescent and the heavy solids **settle to the bottom as the sludge**.
2. **Flotation:** the grease, the oil and the light solids rise and form the **scum layer** at the top. The scum and the sludge are retained by the baffles and the **outlet tee**, taking only the clarified liquid (the middle zone).
3. **Anaerobic digestion:** the settled sludge and the scum are decomposed by the anaerobic bacteria (hydrolysis, acidogenesis, methanogenesis): the volume of the sludge is **reduced 30 to 50 %** and the gases (CH₄, CO₂, H₂S) are released through the **vent pipe**. The first chamber (2/3 of the length) does most of the settling and digestion, the second chamber gives the further settling.
4. **Effluent disposal:** the partially treated effluent (BOD removal about 30 to 50 %, SS 50 to 70 %, with the high nutrients and the pathogens) flows through the outlet tee to the **soak pit, the drain field (the leach field), or a constructed wetland / anaerobic filter** for the secondary treatment.
5. The accumulated **sludge is desludged every 2 to 3 years** (leaving some seed sludge) through the manhole. The **cleaning chemicals, the antibiotics and the large water volumes** should be kept out, because they kill the bacteria and wash out the solids.

**Hydraulic elements for $d/D = 0.6$.** The central angle $\theta = 2\cos^{-1}(1 - 2d/D) = 2\cos^{-1}(-0.2) = 203.1°$ gives

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{a}{A} = 0.6265 \\ \frac{r}{R} = 1.1106\end{gathered}$$

$$\begin{gathered}\frac{v}{V} = \left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^{2/3} = 1.0724 \\ \frac{q}{Q} = \frac{a}{A}\cdot\frac{v}{V} = 0.6718\end{gathered}$$

where $V, Q, A, R$ are the full-pipe values. The sewer is to run 0.6 full at peak, so the full-bore capacity must be

$$ Q_{full} = \frac{Q_{peak}}{0.6718} = \frac{0.5498}{0.6718} = 0.8183\ \text{m}^3/\text{s} $$

**Diameter from Manning's equation** (full flow, $S = 1/500 = 0.002$, $n = 0.013$):

$$ Q_{full} = \frac{1}{n}\cdot\frac{\pi D^2}{4}\left(\frac{D}{4}\right)^{2/3}S^{1/2} = \frac{0.3117}{n}\,D^{8/3}\sqrt S $$

$$ D^{8/3} = \frac{Q_{full}\,n}{0.3117\sqrt S} = \frac{0.8183(0.013)}{0.3117(0.04472)} = 0.7631 $$

$$ D = 0.7631^{3/8} = 0.904\ \text{m} $$

**Adopt $D = 900$ mm** (the nearest commercial size; the pipe runs 0.604 full at peak, practically 0.6).

For $D = 0.9$ m: $A = 0.6362$ m², $Q_{full} = 0.8096$ m³/s, $V_{full} = 1.273$ m/s.

**Velocity at peak flow:** $q/Q = 0.5498/0.8096 = 0.679$ gives $d/D = 0.604$ and $v/V = 1.0746$:

$$ v_{peak} = 1.0746\,(1.273) \approx 1.37\ \text{m/s}\quad(< 3\ \text{m/s, no scour}) $$

**Check of the self-cleansing velocity at the minimum flow.** The minimum (night) flow is taken as **1/3 of the average flow** (the usual assumption, Garg: 1/3 to 1/2): $Q_{min} = 0.2199/3 = 0.0733$ m³/s, so

$$ \frac{q}{Q} = \frac{0.0733}{0.8096} = 0.0905\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{d}{D} = 0.203,\quad \frac{v}{V} = 0.621 $$

$$ v_{min} = 0.621\,(1.273) = 0.79\ \text{m/s} > 0.6\ \text{m/s}\quad\checkmark $$

Even for $Q_{min} = Q_{avg}/2$ the velocity is 0.89 m/s. The sewer is self-cleansing.

$$ \boxed{D = 900\ \text{mm},\ S = 1\text{ in }500;\ v_{peak} = 1.37\ \text{m/s},\ v_{min} = 0.79\ \text{m/s} > 0.6\ \text{m/s}} $$

@s 16
**Mixing of the sewage and the stream** ($Q_s = 1500$ L/s, $Q_r = 600$ L/s, total 2100 L/s):

$$\begin{gathered}\text{DO}_r = 0.90 \times 9.17 = 8.253\ \text{mg/L} \\ \text{DO}_s = 0\end{gathered}$$

$$ \text{DO}_{mix} = \frac{600(8.253) + 1500(0)}{2100} = 2.358\ \text{mg/L} $$

$$ D_0 = 9.17 - 2.358 = 6.812\ \text{mg/L} $$

$$\begin{gathered}\text{BOD}_{5,mix} = \frac{1500(240) + 600(3)}{2100} = 172.3\ \text{mg/L} \\ L_0 = \frac{172.3}{1 - e^{-0.1\times5}} = 437.9\ \text{mg/L}\end{gathered}$$

**Allowed critical deficit:** the minimum DO is 4 mg/L, so $D_c = 9.17 - 4.0 = 5.17$ mg/L; $f_s = K_2/K_1 = 3$.

*Without treatment:* $t_c = \dfrac{1}{K_2-K_1}\ln\left[\dfrac{K_2}{K_1}\left(1 - \dfrac{D_0(K_2-K_1)}{K_1L_0}\right)\right] = 5.34$ d and $D_c = \dfrac{K_1}{K_2}L_0e^{-K_1t_c} = 85.6$ mg/L, far above the 5.17 mg/L allowed: the stream would go **anaerobic**, so treatment is needed.

**Required treatment from the given Streeter–Phelps relation.** With $D_c = 5.17$ and $f_s = 3$:

$$ \left[\frac{L_0}{3(5.17)}\right]^{2} = 3\left[1 - 2\,\frac{D_0}{L_0}\right] $$

Here is the catch. This relation can be satisfied only if the initial deficit is smaller than the allowed critical deficit, $D_0 < D_c$. But **$D_0 = 6.81 > D_c = 5.17$ mg/L**: with sewage of zero DO the stream's DO at the point of mixing is already only 2.36 mg/L, **below the 4 mg/L standard before any oxidation occurs**. Treatment reduces the BOD but cannot raise the DO of the mixture, and the left side minus the right side is positive for every $L_0$ (the minimum of the difference is about +0.7), so **there is no real value of $L_0$, and no degree of BOD removal (even 100 %) can meet the 4 mg/L standard with a DO-free effluent**.

**What can be done (the practical answer).** The treated effluent must also carry the dissolved oxygen (**post-aeration / cascade aeration**) so that the mixture starts above 4 mg/L. The effluent DO needed just to start at 4 mg/L is

$$ \text{DO}_s \ge \frac{4(2100) - 600(8.253)}{1500} = 2.30\ \text{mg/L} $$

For example, with **effluent DO = 4 mg/L** (post-aerated): $\text{DO}_{mix} = (4951.8 + 6000)/2100 = 5.215$ mg/L, $D_0 = 3.955$ mg/L. The relation gives

$$ \left[\frac{L_0}{15.51}\right]^{2} = 3 - \frac{23.73}{L_0}\ \Rightarrow\ L_0 \approx 21.3\ \text{mg/L (mixture)} $$

(checked by the exact oxygen-sag curve: minimum DO = 4.0 mg/L at $t_c = 3.17$ d). The mixed BOD₅ is then $21.3(1 - e^{-0.5}) = 8.38$ mg/L, and the permissible BOD₅ of the effluent

$$ \text{BOD}_{5,e} = \frac{8.38(2100) - 600(3)}{1500} = 10.5\ \text{mg/L} $$

$$ \boxed{\text{Required efficiency} = \frac{240 - 10.5}{240} \approx 95.6\,\%\ \ (\text{effluent BOD}_5 \approx 10\ \text{mg/L, with effluent DO} \ge 4\ \text{mg/L})} $$

That is secondary treatment plus a polishing step (a filter or the extended aeration) with post-aeration. With effluent DO of 2.3 mg/L, the borderline, the required BOD₅ is about 7 mg/L (97 %); with 6 mg/L it is about 12 mg/L (95 %).

*Reading note:* the printed data (DO = 0 sewage) make a pure "BOD removal" answer impossible, so the paper's own formula has no solution; the answer above states this and gives the nearest practicable design. $K_1$ is taken to the base $e$ (BOD₅ = $L_0(1 - e^{-5K_1})$).

@s 17
**Data:** $Q = 5\ \text{MLD} = 5000\ \text{m}^3/\text{d}$, influent BOD = 240 mg/L, effluent BOD ≤ 35 mg/L, the organic loading rate $= 0.25\ \text{kg BOD/m}^3\cdot\text{d}$, hydraulic loading $= 2.5\ \text{m}^3/\text{m}^2\cdot\text{d}$. A standard-rate filter works **without recirculation** ($F = 1$).

**BOD load applied** (to be safe, taking the full raw BOD on the filter):

$$ W = 5000\ \text{m}^3/\text{d} \times 0.240\ \text{kg/m}^3 = 1200\ \text{kg/d} $$

**Volume of the media:**

$$ V = \frac{W}{\text{OLR}} = \frac{1200}{0.25} = 4800\ \text{m}^3 $$

**Plan area** from the hydraulic loading:

$$ A = \frac{Q}{\text{HLR}} = \frac{5000}{2.5} = 2000\ \text{m}^2 $$

**Depth of the media:**

$$ d = \frac{V}{A} = \frac{4800}{2000} = 2.4\ \text{m}\quad(\text{within the standard-rate range 1.8 to 3.0 m}) \checkmark $$

**Diameter.** For two circular filters in parallel, each $A = 1000$ m²:

$$ D = \sqrt{\frac{4(1000)}{\pi}} = 35.7\ \text{m} \Rightarrow \text{provide 2 filters of 36 m diameter} $$

(One filter would be $D = 50.5$ m, which is too large for a rotary distributor; two units also allow the maintenance.)

**Check of the efficiency (NRC equation, 20 °C, single stage, $F = 1$)** with the 4800 m³ and the raw BOD load of 1200 kg/d:

$$ E = \frac{100}{1 + 0.4432\sqrt{\dfrac{W}{VF}}} = \frac{100}{1 + 0.4432\sqrt{1200/4800}} = 81.9\,\% $$

This gives an effluent of $240(1 - 0.819) = 43.5$ mg/L, which is **above 35 mg/L**, so the raw sewage should not be applied directly. A standard-rate filter is always preceded by the **primary sedimentation tank** (BOD removal about 30 %), so the BOD applied to the filter is $0.70 \times 240 = 168$ mg/L and $W = 5000 \times 0.168 = 840$ kg/d:

$$\begin{gathered}E = \frac{100}{1 + 0.4432\sqrt{840/4800}} = 84.4\,\% \\ \text{BOD}_e = 168(1 - 0.844) = 26.3\ \text{mg/L} < 35\ \text{mg/L}\quad\checkmark\end{gathered}$$

(overall BOD removal $= (240 - 26.3)/240 = 89\,\%$). The filter volume of 4800 m³ therefore has a margin: the minimum volume needed for 35 mg/L (a required efficiency of 79.2 %) is about 2400 m³. The plan area of 2000 m² is fixed by the hydraulic loading (2.5 m³/m²·d), so the depth of 2.4 m is retained.
