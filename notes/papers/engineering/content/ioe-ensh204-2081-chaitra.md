**(a) Nature of technical communication.** Technical communication is the exchange of **technical, scientific and professional information** among people who need it to do a job, take a decision or understand a product or process. Its main characteristics (nature) are:

1. **Audience-centred:** the content, vocabulary and level of detail depend on who the readers are (expert, manager, customer, public).
2. **Purpose-oriented:** it informs, instructs, persuades or records; it aims at action (use the machine, approve the budget).
3. **Factual, accurate and objective:** based on data, observations and research; opinions are supported by evidence; no exaggeration or emotion.
4. **Clear, concise and precise:** simple, unambiguous language, standard terminology and units; the active voice and short sentences.
5. **Organised and formatted:** it follows a standard structure (headings, numbering, abstract, summary, references) to make the document easy to scan.
6. **Uses visuals:** tables, graphs, drawings, photographs and symbols are an integral part.
7. **Uses various channels and forms:** reports, proposals, manuals, e-mails, memos, presentations, websites; written, oral and electronic; flows upward, downward, horizontally and externally.
8. **Impersonal and professional in tone,** uses the passive voice where the agent is not important; it also follows ethical and legal rules (copyright, confidentiality).
9. **Interdisciplinary and technology-based,** and often produced by teams.

**(b) Core ethics of engineering.** Engineering ethics are the moral principles that guide the professional conduct of engineers (set out in the codes of ethics of NSPE, IEEE, ASCE and the Nepal Engineering Council). The fundamental canons are:

1. **Hold paramount the safety, health and welfare of the public** (the first duty, above duty to the employer).
2. **Perform services only in the areas of one's competence;** do not accept tasks beyond one's ability and keep learning.
3. **Honesty and integrity:** issue public statements and reports in an objective and truthful manner; never falsify data or qualifications; avoid plagiarism.
4. **Act as faithful agents or trustees of the employer and client,** keep **confidentiality** and avoid or disclose a **conflict of interest**.
5. **Fairness and respect:** treat all persons fairly without discrimination, give credit for others' work, and respect intellectual property.
6. **Responsibility to the environment and society:** use sustainable practices and consider the effect of the project on the environment and the community.
7. **Avoid bribery and corruption;** compete fairly and do not accept improper gifts.
8. **Professional development and support of the profession:** support the colleagues, uphold the dignity and reputation of the profession, and report unethical or unsafe practice (whistle-blowing) when needed.

**(b) Principles of effective technical writing.** The widely accepted principles are the "7 Cs" and related qualities:

1. **Clarity:** the meaning must be understood at first reading: simple words, short sentences, defined technical terms, one idea per paragraph, no ambiguity (*"Switch off the machine"*, not *"Make the machine inoperative at the termination of the process"*).
2. **Conciseness:** no unnecessary words, repetition or padding; give all necessary facts in the fewest words (*"because"* instead of *"due to the fact that"*).
3. **Correctness (accuracy):** correct facts, figures, units, grammar, spelling and punctuation; check calculations and sources.
4. **Completeness:** all the information the reader needs: who, what, when, where, why and how; no gaps that need a second letter.
5. **Coherence and organisation:** a logical sequence of ideas with topic sentences, headings, transitions and parallel structure; the overall pattern (introduction, body, conclusion).
6. **Courtesy and objectivity:** a polite, positive and unbiased tone, the "you" attitude, impersonal style, free of exaggeration, emotion and discriminatory language.
7. **Concreteness:** specific, precise facts and figures instead of vague words (*"the temperature rose by 15 °C in 10 minutes"* instead of *"it became very hot soon"*); examples and visuals.
8. **Audience and purpose awareness:** adapt the level, style and format to the reader and to the purpose of the document.
9. **Proper format, visual design and documentation:** the right format and layout (headings, white space, lists, tables, figures) and a proper citation of the sources.

**(c) Corrected sentences.**

| No. | Corrected sentence | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| i | He and I must arrange **our** books. | possessive of the compound subject *he and I* (first person plural) |
| ii | Neither he nor his wife took **her** purse. | with *neither ... nor* the pronoun agrees with the nearer subject (*wife*, feminine singular) |
| iii | None of the candidates **has** come. (*have* is also accepted in modern usage.) | *none* means *not one*, so the singular verb is the formal choice |
| iv | He is one of the best leaders who **have** served the nation perfectly. | *who* refers to the plural *leaders* |
| v | Your windows need **cleaning** (or *need to be cleaned*). | *need* + gerund has a passive meaning |
| vi | I can't understand her **behaving** like that. | possessive / object + gerund after *understand* |
| vii | He asked **the reason for my coming**. | the noun clause is reduced to a phrase: a simple sentence |
| viii | Once upon a time a man owned a hen **and it laid** a golden egg every day. | the relative clause is replaced by a co-ordinate clause joined with *and*: a compound sentence |
| ix | She is working hard with a view **to winning** this competition. | *to* is a preposition in *with a view to*, so the gerund follows |
| x | There are **no fewer than** thirty students in this class. | *fewer* is used with countable nouns (*less* with uncountable ones) |

**(c) Case-study report: cybersecurity breach (abstract, findings, discussion).**

> **ABSTRACT**<br>
> This report studies the data breach of an online retail company (a representative case). Attackers exploited an unpatched vulnerability in the web application (SQL injection), copied the customer database of 3.4 million records including names, e-mail addresses, phone numbers and hashed passwords, and sold the data on the dark web. The breach was discovered only after 47 days, when customers started to receive fraud calls. The report summarises the findings and discusses the consequences and the lessons.

> **FINDINGS**<br>
> 1. The attack used an unpatched web form that did not validate input (SQL injection); a security update had been available for four months.<br>
> 2. Passwords were stored with a weak hash without salt, so many were cracked quickly; the database was not encrypted.<br>
> 3. There was no intrusion detection, and the logs were not monitored, so the breach lasted 47 days.<br>
> 4. The company informed the customers and the authorities only after 3 weeks of the discovery.<br>
> 5. Consequences: identity theft and phishing against customers, a regulator's fine of about USD 2 million, legal costs and compensation, a 30% fall in sales in three months, a fall in share price and damage to reputation.

> **DISCUSSION**<br>
> The breach was not caused by an advanced attack but by basic security failures: delay in patching, poor coding practice and weak data protection. Secure development (input validation, parameterised queries), regular penetration tests, strong salted hashing (bcrypt / Argon2), encryption of sensitive data, multi-factor authentication, log monitoring and an incident response plan would have prevented or detected the attack early. Prompt and honest communication limits the damage to the customers' trust, and the law now requires quick notification. Engineers should treat security as a design requirement, follow the principle of least privilege and keep the software updated.

**(a) Barriers of active listening and how to overcome them.** *Active listening* is listening with full attention to understand the complete message (words, feelings and intentions), showing interest and confirming the understanding by feedback (nodding, questions, paraphrasing). Barriers:

| Barrier | How to overcome it |
|---|---|
| **Physical / environmental noise** (traffic, phones, bad sound system, uncomfortable room) | choose a quiet place, switch off the phone, sit close, ask for the speaker to repeat |
| **Distraction and daydreaming** (the mind is four times faster than speech) | concentrate, take short notes, summarise mentally, keep eye contact |
| **Prejudice and bias** (about the speaker's age, gender, caste, accent, appearance) | accept the message on its merit; keep an open mind |
| **Pre-judging / thinking of the reply** instead of listening, and interrupting | wait for the speaker to finish; ask questions only after |
| **Information overload and fatigue** | take breaks, focus on main ideas, listen in short periods |
| **Language and accent differences, jargon** | ask for clarification, paraphrase, ask for examples; learn the vocabulary |
| **Lack of interest** or the topic is felt to be difficult or dull | find the personal benefit, set a purpose for listening, ask "what can I learn" |
| **Emotional barriers** (anger, stress, fear) | stay calm, take a deep breath, postpone if necessary |
| **Selective listening and poor memory** | listen to the whole message, take notes, repeat key points |
| **Cultural differences** and wrong assumptions | learn about cultural norms, check the meaning politely |

*General remedies:* face the speaker, give non-verbal encouragement (nod, lean forward), do not interrupt, paraphrase ("So you mean ..."), ask open questions and summarise at the end.

**(b) Public speaking.** Public speaking is the **process of delivering a prepared oral message directly to a live audience** to inform, persuade, motivate or entertain (a lecture, a conference talk, a speech at a ceremony, a project presentation).

*Key strategies for an effective speech.*

1. **Know the purpose, the audience and the occasion;** adapt the content, language and examples to them.
2. **Prepare and organise:** collect reliable material, write an outline with a strong opening (a story, question or fact), a clear body (2-4 main points with examples and data) and a memorable conclusion; one central idea.
3. **Rehearse** aloud several times, time yourself, and record or practise in front of a friend; do not memorise word for word.
4. **Control nervousness:** breathe deeply, prepare well, think positively, arrive early and make friends with the audience.
5. **Use voice well:** clear pronunciation, adequate volume, varied pitch, a moderate rate and effective pauses.
6. **Use body language:** upright posture, natural gestures, a smile, eye contact with all parts of the audience, and purposeful movement.
7. **Use visual aids wisely** (a few clear slides, charts and objects); do not read from the slides.
8. **Engage the audience:** ask questions, use humour and stories, respond to the reactions, and invite questions at the end.
9. **Use simple, concrete, correct language;** avoid jargon and fillers ("um", "you know").
10. **Be honest and well-dressed,** keep to the time limit, handle questions politely and **end strongly** with a summary and a call to action.

**(b)(i) Chart and table.**

| Basis | Table | Chart |
|---|---|---|
| Form | numbers or words arranged in **rows and columns** | a **graphic** representation (bars, lines, boxes, circles) |
| Purpose | to give **exact values** and make look-up and comparison easy | to show **relationships, trends, comparisons or processes** at a glance |
| Content | detailed data | summarised data or structure (organisation chart, flow chart, Gantt chart, pie chart) |
| Reading | needs reading and thinking, slower | quickly understood, attractive, more memorable |
| Precision | high (exact numbers) | lower (approximate values) |
| Space | compact for large amount of data | takes more space |
| Example | marks of 10 students in 4 subjects | organisation chart, flow chart of a water-treatment process |

**(b)(ii) Bar graph and pie graph.**

| Basis | Bar graph | Pie graph (chart) |
|---|---|---|
| Shape | rectangular bars (vertical or horizontal) on two axes | a **circle divided into sectors** (slices) |
| Shows | **comparison** of values of different categories, changes over time | **parts of a whole**: the percentage share of each component |
| Data | one or more series of values; the total is not needed | the parts add up to **100%** (360 degrees) of **one** data set |
| Scale | has a scale on the axis (a value for every bar) | no axes; the angle of a sector $=\dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{total}}\times360^{\circ}$ |
| Number of categories | many categories (and group bars) can be shown | best for 3 to 6 categories; many slices are confusing |
| Accuracy of reading | easy to read the exact heights and differences | difficult to compare similar-size slices |
| Example | production of cement in 5 years; students in each department | budget distribution of a project: material 40%, labour 30%, equipment 20%, other 10% |
