@s 1
**Buffer capacity.** The buffer capacity $\beta$ of a buffer solution is the number of moles of strong acid or strong base that must be added to one litre of the buffer to change its pH by one unit:
$$\beta = \frac{\Delta n}{\Delta\text{pH}}\quad(\text{mol L}^{-1}\ \text{per pH unit})$$
It is greatest when $[\text{salt}] = [\text{acid}]$ ($\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$) and increases with the total concentration of the buffer components.

**Calculation.** Moles in the mixture (total volume $150 + 450 = 600$ ml $= 0.6$ L):
$$\begin{gathered}n_{\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}} = 0.5\times0.150 = 0.075\ \text{mol} \\ n_{\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}} = 0.1\times0.450 = 0.045\ \text{mol}\end{gathered}$$
$$\text{p}K_a = -\log(1.8\times10^{-5}) = 4.745$$

*Initial pH* (Henderson-Hasselbalch):
$$\text{pH}_1 = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{n_{\text{salt}}}{n_{\text{acid}}} = 4.745 + \log\frac{0.045}{0.075} = 4.745 - 0.222 = 4.523$$

*After adding $10$ ml of $0.2$ M HCl:* $n_{\text{HCl}} = 0.2\times0.010 = 0.002$ mol. The H$^+$ reacts with the acetate ion:
$$\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+\to\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$$
$$\begin{gathered}n_{\text{salt}} = 0.045 - 0.002 = 0.043\ \text{mol} \\ n_{\text{acid}} = 0.075 + 0.002 = 0.077\ \text{mol}\end{gathered}$$
$$\text{pH}_2 = 4.745 + \log\frac{0.043}{0.077} = 4.745 - 0.253 = 4.492$$
(The common volume cancels in the ratio.)
$$\boxed{\Delta\text{pH} = 4.492 - 4.523 = -0.031\ \ (\text{pH falls by about }0.03)}$$
The pH changes by only $0.03$ units, showing the buffer action. The buffer capacity for this addition is $\beta = \dfrac{0.002/0.6}{0.031}\approx0.107$ mol L$^{-1}$ per pH unit.

@s 2
**Electrochemical cell.** A device that converts chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy (a **voltaic/galvanic cell**) or uses electrical energy to bring about a non-spontaneous chemical change (an **electrolytic cell**). A voltaic cell has two half-cells (electrodes in their own electrolytes) joined by a salt bridge; oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode.

**Role of the salt bridge.** The salt bridge (a U-tube of an inert electrolyte such as KCl or KNO$_3$ in agar gel, with ions of nearly equal mobility):

1. completes the **internal circuit** by allowing ions to migrate between the half-cells;
2. maintains **electrical neutrality** of both solutions (anions move to the anode compartment as positive ions are produced, cations to the cathode compartment as they are removed);
3. eliminates (reduces to about $1$–$2$ mV) the **liquid junction potential** between the two solutions;
4. prevents the two solutions from mixing directly.

**emf of the cell** $\text{Mn}\,|\,\text{Mn}^{2+}(0.1\ \text{M})\,||\,\text{Fe}^{2+}(1.0\ \text{M})\,|\,\text{Fe}$.

Mn is the anode (oxidation) and Fe the cathode (reduction):
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Mn}\to\text{Mn}^{2+} + 2e^- \\ \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2e^-\to\text{Fe} \\ \text{Mn} + \text{Fe}^{2+}\to\text{Mn}^{2+} + \text{Fe}\end{gathered}$$
The standard electrode (reduction) potentials are $E^\circ_{\text{Mn}^{2+}/\text{Mn}} = -1.18$ V and $E^\circ_{\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}} = -0.44$ V. (The scan prints the iron value as $0.44$ V, the magnitude of the iron couple; as a reduction potential it is $-0.44$ V, and this is the only reading that makes Mn the anode of the cell as drawn.)
$$E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{cathode} - E^\circ_{anode} = -0.44 - (-1.18) = 0.74\ \text{V}$$
Nernst equation ($n = 2$, $25^\circ$C):
$$E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell} - \frac{0.0591}{2}\log\frac{[\text{Mn}^{2+}]}{[\text{Fe}^{2+}]} = 0.74 - 0.02955\log\frac{0.1}{1.0}$$
$$E_{cell} = 0.74 + 0.02955 = 0.7696$$
$$\boxed{E_{cell}\approx0.77\ \text{V}}$$
(positive, so the reaction proceeds spontaneously as written).

@s 3
**Negative catalysis vs catalytic poisoning.**

| Negative catalysis | Catalytic poisoning |
|---|---|
| A substance (**inhibitor** or negative catalyst) **decreases the rate** of a reaction. | A substance (**poison**) **destroys the activity of a positive catalyst**. |
| It acts on the reactants or the reaction path (for example by destroying a chain carrier). | It acts on the catalyst surface (strongly adsorbed, blocking the active sites). |
| Example: glycerol or acetanilide retards decomposition of H$_2$O$_2$; tetraethyl lead retards knocking. | Example: As$_2$O$_3$ poisons Pt in the contact process; CO poisons Fe in the Haber process (H$_2$S poisons Fe). |
| It is a deliberate effect, used to preserve materials. | It is an unwanted effect, and the catalyst is generally regenerated or replaced. |

**How a catalyst works.** A catalyst provides an alternative path with a **lower activation energy** without being consumed. Two common theories:

- *Intermediate compound formation theory* (homogeneous): the catalyst forms an unstable intermediate with a reactant, which reacts further to give the product and regenerate the catalyst, e.g. NO catalysing $2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2\to2\text{SO}_3$: $2\text{NO} + \text{O}_2\to2\text{NO}_2$; $\text{NO}_2 + \text{SO}_2\to\text{SO}_3 + \text{NO}$.
- *Adsorption theory* (heterogeneous): reactant molecules are adsorbed on the active sites of the solid surface, their concentration rises, bonds are weakened, the molecules react on the surface and the products desorb.

**Role of a promoter.** A promoter is a substance that **increases the activity of a catalyst** though it is not itself a catalyst (it may alter the surface structure or create more active sites). Examples: Mo or Al$_2$O$_3$/K$_2$O promotes the iron catalyst in the Haber process; Cu or Ni promotes some oxide catalysts.

@s 5
**a) Formation and depletion of ozone.**

*Formation* (stratosphere, 15–35 km). UV radiation of wavelength $<240$ nm splits oxygen molecules into atoms, which add to O$_2$ (with a third body $M$ carrying away the energy):
$$\begin{gathered}\text{O}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu\ (\lambda<240\ \text{nm})}2\,\text{O} \\ \text{O} + \text{O}_2 + M\to\text{O}_3 + M\end{gathered}$$
*Natural destruction* (balance with formation): $\text{O}_3\xrightarrow{h\nu\ (220\text{–}320\ \text{nm})}\text{O}_2 + \text{O}$ and $\text{O} + \text{O}_3\to2\,\text{O}_2$.

*Man-made depletion.* Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs, e.g. CFCl$_3$, CF$_2$Cl$_2$), halons and nitrogen oxides from aircraft are stable in the troposphere and reach the stratosphere, where UV releases chlorine atoms that destroy ozone in a chain reaction:
$$\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}\cdot + \text{Cl}\cdot$$
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Cl}\cdot + \text{O}_3\to\text{ClO}\cdot + \text{O}_2 \\ \text{ClO}\cdot + \text{O}\to\text{Cl}\cdot + \text{O}_2\end{gathered}$$
$$\text{net: O}_3 + \text{O}\to2\,\text{O}_2$$
One Cl atom can destroy about $10^5$ ozone molecules. Polar stratospheric clouds make the effect largest over Antarctica (the "ozone hole").

*Consequences of depletion.* More UV-B reaches the earth: skin cancer, cataract and eye damage, weakened immune system in humans; damage to crops, phytoplankton and aquatic life (food-chain disruption); damage to paints and plastics; and some climate change effects.

**b) Acid rain.** Rain of pH below $5.6$ (normal rain, pH $\approx5.6$ due to dissolved CO$_2$) is called acid rain. It is caused by the oxides of sulphur and nitrogen released by burning fossil fuels, smelting and vehicles, which are oxidized in air and dissolve in cloud water:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{SO}_2 + \tfrac12\text{O}_2\to\text{SO}_3 \\ \text{SO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\to\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\end{gathered}$$
$$\begin{gathered}2\,\text{NO} + \text{O}_2\to2\,\text{NO}_2 \\ 4\,\text{NO}_2 + 2\,\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2\to4\,\text{HNO}_3\end{gathered}$$
The acids fall as rain, snow or dry deposition, damaging buildings and marble monuments, corroding metals, acidifying lakes and soil and harming forests and aquatic life.

@s 6
**Inorganic polymers.** Polymers whose backbone chain contains **no carbon atoms** (they are made of inorganic elements such as Si, P, S, N, B), or inorganic atoms with some organic side groups. They are usually heat resistant and chemically inert. Examples: silicones (-Si-O-), polyphosphazenes (-P=N-), polymeric sulphur nitride (-S=N-)$_n$, borazine polymers, polysilanes.

**Applications of silicones in engineering.** Silicones (polyorganosiloxanes, $[\text{R}_2\text{SiO}]_n$) are used:

- as **lubricants and greases** that work at $-50$ to $+250^\circ$C (high viscosity index);
- as **electrical insulation** (class H insulation of motors, transformers, cables);
- as **sealants, gaskets and adhesives** (building glazing, expansion joints) and for **water-repellent coatings** on masonry, textiles and glass;
- as **silicone rubber** (O-rings, hoses, aerospace and automotive parts) and **hydraulic fluids**;
- as **anti-foam agents**, mould-release agents, polishes and heat-resistant paints; in medical implants and tubing.

**Polymeric sulphur nitride, $(\text{SN})_x$.** It is an inorganic polymer with alternating S and N atoms, $[-\text{S}=\text{N}-]_x$, studied as the first polymeric **metallic conductor**.

*Preparation.* Tetrasulphur tetranitride is made from S$_2$Cl$_2$ (or sulphur) and ammonia, then converted in two steps:
$$6\,\text{S}_2\text{Cl}_2 + 16\,\text{NH}_3\to\text{S}_4\text{N}_4 + 8\,\text{S} + 12\,\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$$
$$\text{S}_4\text{N}_4\xrightarrow[\text{Ag wool, }300^\circ\text{C}]{\text{vacuum}}2\,\text{S}_2\text{N}_2\xrightarrow{\text{room temp., solid-state polymerization}}(\text{SN})_x$$
*Properties and uses.* It forms golden, lustrous fibrous crystals; it conducts electricity like a metal along the chain direction and becomes a **superconductor below about $0.3$ K**. It explodes on heating above about $240^\circ$C. It is used in research on organic-metal-like conductors, in experimental electronic devices and as the model for conducting polymers.

@s 7
**Non-biodegradable polymer.** A polymer that cannot be decomposed by microorganisms (bacteria, fungi) in the environment, so that it persists for a very long time and causes pollution. Examples: polyethylene, polystyrene, PVC, Teflon, polypropylene, epoxy resins, nylon.

**Polystyrene.** *Preparation:* addition (free radical) polymerization of styrene (vinylbenzene) by bulk, solution, suspension or emulsion methods with benzoyl peroxide as the initiator at $80$–$100^\circ$C:
$$n\,\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}{=}\text{CH}_2\xrightarrow[\ 80\text{–}100^\circ\text{C}\ ]{(\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{COO})_2}[-\text{CH}(\text{C}_6\text{H}_5)-\text{CH}_2-]_n$$
Steps: initiation ($\text{R}\cdot + \text{CH}_2{=}\text{CHPh}\to\text{R–CH}_2\text{–C}\cdot\text{HPh}$), propagation (addition of more styrene), termination (combination or disproportionation).

*Uses:* packaging and insulation as **expanded polystyrene (thermocol)**, disposable cups and containers, toys, radio and TV cabinets, refrigerator parts, laboratory ware, light fittings, ion-exchange resin base (sulphonated), foam insulation boards in construction.

**Epoxy resins.** *Preparation:* condensation of **bisphenol-A with epichlorohydrin** in the presence of NaOH at about $60$–$100^\circ$C; HCl is lost (as NaCl) in each step, giving a linear prepolymer with epoxide end groups:
$$\text{Bisphenol-A (2 OH groups)} + \text{epichlorohydrin}\xrightarrow{\text{NaOH}}\text{glycidyl ether linkages (epoxy prepolymer)} + \text{NaCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
Repeating unit of the prepolymer: $-\text{O–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–C(CH}_3)_2\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–O–CH}_2\text{–CH(OH)–CH}_2-$, with an epoxide (oxirane) ring at each end.

The resin is then **cured (cross-linked) with a hardener**, usually a polyamine such as triethylenetetramine ($\text{H}_2\text{N–CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{–NH–CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{–NH–CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{–NH}_2$), at room temperature; the amine opens the epoxide rings to give a rigid thermoset network.

*Uses:* strong **adhesives** (Araldite), surface coatings and paints for metal and concrete (protective, anticorrosive), floor and tank linings, **fibre-glass reinforced composites** (aircraft, boats, pipes), electrical laminates and printed-circuit boards, encapsulation of electronic parts, and repair of concrete cracks.

**Transition elements.** The elements that have **partly filled $d$ orbitals** in their ground state or in any of their common oxidation states are called transition elements (the $d$-block elements Sc to Cu in the 3d series, and so on). Zn, Cd, Hg (d$^{10}$) are not true transition elements.

**Colour and $d$–$d$ transition.** In a free ion, the five $d$ orbitals are degenerate. In a complex, the ligands' field splits them into two sets, $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ (octahedral), separated by an energy $\Delta_o$ (crystal field splitting).

For a transition metal ion with an **incomplete $d$ subshell** (d$^1$ to d$^9$) an electron in a lower $t_{2g}$ orbital can absorb a photon whose energy equals $\Delta_o = h\nu$ from the visible region and jump to the vacant or half-filled $e_g$ level (a **$d$–$d$ transition**). The rest of the white light is transmitted, so the compound appears in the **complementary colour** of the absorbed light:

- $[\text{Ti(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{3+}$ (d$^1$): absorbs green-yellow light (about $500$ nm) so it appears **violet**.
- $[\text{Cu(H}_2\text{O)}_4]^{2+}$ (d$^9$) absorbs orange-red, so it appears **blue**; $[\text{Cu(NH}_3)_4]^{2+}$ with a stronger ligand absorbs at shorter wavelength, a deeper blue.
- $[\text{Ni(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}$ is green, $[\text{Co(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}$ pink.

The wavelength absorbed depends on $\Delta_o$ (ligand strength, metal and oxidation state), which is why the colour changes with the ligand.

Ions with **$d^0$ (Sc$^{3+}$, Ti$^{4+}$) or $d^{10}$ (Zn$^{2+}$, Cu$^+$)** configurations have no possible $d$–$d$ transition and are **colourless**. (Intense colours such as MnO$_4^-$ and CrO$_4^{2-}$ arise from charge-transfer transitions.)

@s 9
**$d$-block elements.** The elements of groups 3–12 in which the last electron enters the penultimate $(n-1)d$ subshell are called $d$-block elements (general configuration $(n-1)d^{1-10}ns^{1-2}$). They comprise the 3d, 4d, 5d and 6d series.

**Unpaired electrons and paramagnetism.** An electron has a spin and therefore a magnetic moment. Two electrons in the same orbital have opposite spins, so their moments cancel, and an atom or ion with all electrons paired is repelled slightly by a magnetic field (**diamagnetic**). If the ion has **one or more unpaired electrons**, the spin magnetic moments are not cancelled, giving the ion a net permanent magnetic moment which lines up with an applied field, so the substance is drawn into the field (**paramagnetic**). The more unpaired electrons, the larger the moment and the stronger the paramagnetism.

**Magnetic moment** (spin-only formula):
$$\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \text{BM}$$
For $n = 5$ unpaired electrons (e.g. Mn$^{2+}$ or Fe$^{3+}$):
$$\mu = \sqrt{5(5+2)} = \sqrt{35}$$
$$\boxed{\mu\approx5.92\ \text{BM (Bohr magnetons)}}$$

@s 10
**$[\text{Ni(CN)}_4]^{2-}$.**

Ni (Z = 28): $3d^84s^2$; Ni$^{2+}$: $3d^8$. CN$^-$ is a **strong field ligand**, so it forces the two unpaired $3d$ electrons to pair, leaving one $3d$ orbital empty. One $3d$, one $4s$ and two $4p$ orbitals hybridise to $dsp^2$, and the four CN$^-$ donate electron pairs into them.

- Ni$^{2+}$ free ion: $3d$: $\uparrow\downarrow,\ \uparrow\downarrow,\ \uparrow\downarrow,\ \uparrow,\ \uparrow$ (two unpaired); $4s$, $4p$ empty.
- With four strong-field CN$^-$ ligands the two unpaired electrons pair up in one $3d$ orbital: $3d$: $\uparrow\downarrow$ in all four occupied orbitals and **one $3d$ orbital vacant**.
- The vacant $3d$ ($d_{x^2-y^2}$), the $4s$ and two $4p$ ($4p_x$, $4p_y$) orbitals hybridise to four $dsp^2$ orbitals, each accepting an electron pair from a CN$^-$ ligand.

- **Geometry: square planar** ($dsp^2$ hybridisation).
- **Magnetic property: diamagnetic** (no unpaired electrons, $\mu = 0$). Inner-orbital (low spin) complex.

**$[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{2+}$.**

Co (Z = 27): $3d^74s^2$; Co$^{2+}$: $3d^7$ with three unpaired electrons ($t_{2g}^5e_g^2$). Valence-bond treatment (inner orbital, as in the standard textbook account): the six NH$_3$ ligands force pairing of the electrons; two $3d$, one $4s$ and three $4p$ orbitals give **$d^2sp^3$** hybrids, with the extra (seventh) electron promoted to the $5s$ orbital:
- Co$^{2+}$ free ion, $3d$: $\uparrow\downarrow,\ \uparrow\downarrow,\ \uparrow,\ \uparrow,\ \uparrow$ (three unpaired).
- Under the NH$_3$ field two electrons pair up and the seventh electron is promoted to the $5s$ orbital, which frees two $3d$ orbitals. These two $3d$ orbitals, the $4s$ and three $4p$ orbitals hybridise to six $d^2sp^3$ orbitals, which accept six electron pairs from NH$_3$.

- **Geometry: octahedral** ($d^2sp^3$).
- **Magnetic property:** paramagnetic with **one unpaired electron** (in $5s$), $\mu = \sqrt{1(1+2)} = 1.73$ BM, a low-spin inner-orbital complex.

*Note.* This valence-bond account (which needs the unlikely $5s$ electron) is the standard exam answer; experiment shows $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{2+}$ is actually a **high-spin** ($sp^3d^2$, outer-orbital) octahedral complex with three unpaired electrons, $\mu = \sqrt{15} = 3.87$ BM (observed about $4.3$–$5$ BM with orbital contribution). Either way the complex is **octahedral and paramagnetic**.

@s 11
**a) Werner's theory.** Werner proposed that metals show two types of valence:

- **primary (ionisable) valence**, satisfied by negative ions, shown by dotted lines and satisfied by ions that can be freed in solution;
- **secondary (non-ionisable) valence**, equal to the coordination number, satisfied by ligands (neutral molecules or anions) directed to fixed positions in space and **not ionisable**.

In **$[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]\text{Cl}_3$** the six NH$_3$ molecules satisfy the secondary valence (coordination number 6). The three Cl$^-$ satisfy only the primary valence (+3), so they lie outside the coordination sphere and ionise: $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]\text{Cl}_3\to[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{3+} + 3\,\text{Cl}^-$. With AgNO$_3$ all three give white AgCl: $3\,\text{Ag}^+ + 3\,\text{Cl}^-\to3\,\text{AgCl}\downarrow$.

In **$[\text{Co(NH}_3)_3\text{Cl}_3]$** the three NH$_3$ and the three Cl$^-$ together make up the coordination number 6, so all three Cl$^-$ satisfy **both** the primary and the secondary valence. They are inside the coordination sphere, firmly bonded to Co, and no free Cl$^-$ ions are present in solution, so **no precipitate of AgCl** is formed with AgNO$_3$ (the complex is non-electrolytic).

**Explosives.** An explosive is a substance that, on suitable initiation by heat, shock or friction, undergoes very rapid exothermic decomposition producing a large volume of hot gases, a sudden pressure rise and a loud report.

- **Primary (initiating) explosives:** extremely sensitive to heat, shock or friction; explode on slight stimulus and are used to set off other explosives (detonators, percussion caps). Examples: mercury fulminate $\text{Hg(ONC)}_2$, lead azide $\text{Pb(N}_3)_2$, lead styphnate.
- **Low explosives:** burn (deflagrate) at a comparatively slow rate (below about $1$–$2$ km/s) with a pushing effect, producing gas without a detonation wave. Used as propellants, in fireworks and blasting in quarrying. Examples: gunpowder (black powder: KNO$_3$ + charcoal + S), smokeless powder, safety fuse.
- **High explosives:** detonate with a supersonic shock wave (velocity $>3$–$9$ km/s) with great shattering power; they are relatively insensitive and need a detonator. Examples: TNT, RDX, PETN, nitroglycerine, dynamite, picric acid.

**Nitrocellulose.** *Preparation:* cellulose (purified cotton or wood pulp) is **nitrated** with a mixture of concentrated HNO$_3$ and concentrated H$_2$SO$_4$ (sulphuric acid acts as the dehydrating agent) at $30$–$40^\circ$C for several hours; the product is washed free of acid, boiled and stabilized:
$$[\text{C}_6\text{H}_7\text{O}_2(\text{OH})_3]_n + 3n\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4}[\text{C}_6\text{H}_7\text{O}_2(\text{ONO}_2)_3]_n + 3n\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
With about 13 % N, the fully nitrated product is **guncotton** (cellulose trinitrate, explosive); with 10–12 % N (dinitrate, **pyroxylin**) it is used for lacquers and celluloid.

*Uses:* guncotton as a high explosive and for **smokeless powder** (mixed with nitroglycerine to form cordite and ballistite); pyroxylin for **lacquers, nitrocellulose paints, artificial leather, celluloid (with camphor), photographic films** and collodion.

**a) Paint.** A paint is a mechanical dispersion of one or more finely divided pigments in a liquid vehicle (drying oil with thinner), which, when spread as a thin film on a surface, dries to form a hard, adherent, decorative and protective coat.

**Major requisites (constituents).**

1. **Pigment**: gives colour, opacity and bulk and protects against UV and corrosion (white lead, TiO$_2$, ZnO, red lead, carbon black, Prussian blue, chrome yellow).
2. **Vehicle or drying oil** (linseed, tung, soybean oil): forms the continuous tough film on oxidation and polymerization and holds the pigment.
3. **Thinner (solvent)** (turpentine, mineral spirits, benzene): lowers the viscosity so that the paint can be applied and evaporates on drying.
4. **Drier** (naphthenates or linoleates of Pb, Mn, Co): catalyses oxidation and so speeds up the drying of the oil.
5. **Filler or extender** (barytes, gypsum, talc, china clay): adds bulk, reduces cost and improves the film.
6. **Plasticizer** (tricresyl phosphate, castor oil): increases elasticity and prevents cracking.
7. Antiskinning agent and other additives.

**b) Lubricants.** A lubricant is a substance introduced between two moving or sliding surfaces to reduce the friction and wear between them.

**Functions of a lubricant:**

1. **Reduces friction** and the wear and tear of surfaces (and the power loss) by forming a thin film that separates the rubbing surfaces.
2. **Reduces heating and acts as a coolant**, carrying away the heat produced.
3. **Reduces corrosion** of the surfaces by protecting them from air and moisture.
4. **Acts as a seal**, preventing leakage of gases (e.g. piston rings) and keeps out dust and dirt.
5. Reduces noise and vibration (shock absorption) and increases the life and efficiency of the machine.

**S$_\text{N}$1 versus S$_\text{N}$2 stereochemistry.**

*S$_\text{N}$1* (unimolecular nucleophilic substitution) takes place in **two steps**. In the slow step the leaving group departs to give a **planar $sp^2$ carbocation** (achiral at that carbon). The nucleophile is free to attack this flat cation from **either face with nearly equal probability**: attack from the face from which the leaving group left gives **retention**, attack from the opposite face gives **inversion**, so a (nearly) racemic mixture results. (A slight excess of inversion occurs because the departing ion pair shields the front face.)

*S$_\text{N}$2* (bimolecular) is a **single-step, concerted** reaction. The nucleophile must attack the carbon from the **back side**, $180^\circ$ opposite to the leaving group (front-side attack is blocked by the leaving group and electronic repulsion). The three other groups flip over like an umbrella in the wind through a trigonal-bipyramidal transition state, so the product always has the **inverted (Walden inversion) configuration**.

**Mechanism: tertiary alkyl halide with aqueous NaOH (S$_\text{N}$1).** Take tert-butyl bromide, $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–Br}$, in aqueous NaOH (rate $= k[(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}]$, first order, independent of $[\text{OH}^-]$):

*Step 1 (slow, rate-determining): ionisation to the carbocation*
$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–Br}\xrightarrow{\text{slow}}(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+ + \text{Br}^-$$
*Step 2 (fast): attack of the nucleophile (OH$^-$ or water) on the planar carbocation*
$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+ + \text{OH}^-\xrightarrow{\text{fast}}(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–OH}$$
Overall:
$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–Br} + \text{NaOH}(aq)\to(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–OH} + \text{NaBr}$$
The tertiary carbocation is stabilised by hyperconjugation and the inductive effect of three methyl groups, and the bulky tertiary carbon blocks the backside attack of S$_\text{N}$2, so tertiary halides react by S$_\text{N}$1. A small amount of isobutene (E1/E2) may form as a by-product in strongly basic solution.

@s 16
**Bimolecular elimination (E2).** An E2 reaction is a **one-step (concerted) elimination** in which a strong base removes a $\beta$-hydrogen at the same time as the leaving group departs from the $\alpha$-carbon, forming a C=C double bond. The rate depends on the concentrations of **both** the alkyl halide and the base (second order):
$$\text{rate} = k[\text{R–X}][\text{B}^-]$$

**Dehydrohalogenation of a tertiary alkyl halide.** Heating a tertiary alkyl halide such as tert-butyl bromide with a strong base in alcohol (alcoholic KOH or sodium ethoxide, $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{O}^-$) gives the alkene:
$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–Br} + \text{KOH}\xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{alcohol}}(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C}{=}\text{CH}_2 + \text{KBr} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**Mechanism.** The base attacks a hydrogen on a $\beta$-carbon (a methyl group) while the electrons of the C–H bond move to form the $\pi$ bond between $\text{C}_\alpha$ and $\text{C}_\beta$, and the C–Br bond breaks with Br leaving as Br$^-$, all in a single transition state (the H and Br are *anti-periplanar*):
$$\text{B}^- + \text{H–CH}_2\text{–C(CH}_3)_2\text{–Br}\to\left[\text{B}^{\delta-}\cdots\text{H}\cdots\text{CH}_2\cdots\text{C(CH}_3)_2\cdots\text{Br}^{\delta-}\right]^\ddagger\to\text{BH} + \text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)_2 + \text{Br}^-$$

**Features.**

- Single step, no carbocation intermediate; a strong base at high concentration and heat favour E2 for $3^\circ$ halides (a weak base or neutral solvent gives S$_\text{N}$1/E1).
- Reactivity order $3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ$ (more alkyl groups give a more stable, more substituted alkene).
- When several $\beta$-hydrogens are available, the **more substituted alkene (Saytzeff rule) is the major product**, e.g. 2-bromo-2-methylbutane with ethoxide gives 2-methylbut-2-ene (major) and 2-methylbut-1-ene (minor).
- There is a kinetic isotope effect (C–H bond breaks in the rate-determining step) and an anti-periplanar stereochemical requirement.
