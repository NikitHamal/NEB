@s 1
**Factors affecting groundwater movement in the Nepal Himalaya.**

1. *Topography:* steep slopes cause fast run-off and a high hydraulic gradient, but gentle benches, fans and the Bhabar zone (coarse foothill gravels) are the main recharge areas; the Terai plain is the discharge zone with artesian wells.
2. *Rainfall and snow/ice melt:* the monsoon (about 80 percent of rainfall in June to September) recharges aquifers; glaciers and snow feed springs and rivers.
3. *Geology and lithology:* the high permeability of gravel, sand, fractured quartzite and limestone (karst) against low permeability of phyllite, slate and shale.
4. *Structures:* joints, faults, thrusts (MBT, MCT, MFT) and shear zones act as conduits or barriers, so water often emerges as springs along faults and lithological contacts.
5. *Weathered mantle and colluvium:* store water on slopes; springs emerge where the soil meets bedrock.
6. *Vegetation and land use:* forests increase infiltration; roads, urbanisation and deforestation increase run-off and reduce recharge.
7. *Human activity:* over-pumping (Kathmandu valley deep aquifer), irrigation and recharge.
8. *Tectonics and earthquakes:* change spring discharge.

@s 2
**Rock stress.** Rock stress is the force per unit area acting within the rock mass. The in-situ (virgin) stress is due to the weight of overburden, $\sigma_v = \gamma z$ (about $27$ kPa/m), the horizontal stress $\sigma_h = k\sigma_v$ (with $k$ commonly $0.3$ to $3$, higher near the surface in tectonically active regions such as the Himalaya), plus tectonic, residual and topographic stresses. Induced stresses arise around excavations. Knowledge of the magnitude and direction of the principal stresses is required for design of tunnels, caverns, underground powerhouses and slopes.

**In-situ stress measurement methods**

1. *Hydraulic fracturing:* a section of borehole is sealed with packers and pressurised until the wall fractures; the breakdown, shut-in and re-opening pressures give the minor horizontal stress $\sigma_h$ and the major $\sigma_H = 3\sigma_h - P_{re} - P_0 + T$. Works at depth, no instrument in the hole.
2. *Overcoring (stress relief):* a strain gauge cell (USBM, CSIRO/CSIR) is glued in a pilot borehole and then the surrounding rock is overcored; the recovered strains are converted by elasticity to the three-dimensional stress tensor.
3. *Flat-jack test:* a slot is cut in a tunnel wall, and the pressure needed to restore the original distance between reference pins equals the normal stress there.
4. *Borehole breakout and drilling-induced fractures:* from borehole image logs.
5. *Acoustic emission (Kaiser effect) and core discing/anelastic strain recovery* on cores.

@s 3
**Rock Mass Rating (RMR, Bieniawski 1989).** The rock mass is divided into structural regions and the following six parameters are rated and summed:

| Parameter | Maximum rating |
|---|---|
| 1. Uniaxial compressive strength of intact rock | 15 |
| 2. Rock Quality Designation (RQD) | 20 |
| 3. Spacing of discontinuities | 20 |
| 4. Condition of discontinuities (persistence, aperture, roughness, infilling, weathering) | 30 |
| 5. Groundwater (inflow, water pressure ratio) | 15 |
| 6. Adjustment for orientation of discontinuities (very favourable 0 to very unfavourable $-12$ for tunnels) | 0 to $-12$ |

$RMR = \sum(1 \text{ to } 5) + \text{adjustment}$ ($0$ to $100$).

| RMR | 81 to 100 | 61 to 80 | 41 to 60 | 21 to 40 | $<$ 20 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Class | I | II | III | IV | V |
| Description | Very good | Good | Fair | Poor | Very poor |
| Cohesion (kPa) | $>$ 400 | 300 to 400 | 200 to 300 | 100 to 200 | $<$ 100 |
| Friction angle | $>$ 45° | 35° to 45° | 25° to 35° | 15° to 25° | $<$ 15° |
| Stand-up time | 20 yr for 15 m span | 1 yr for 10 m | 1 week for 5 m | 10 h for 2.5 m | 30 min for 1 m |

**Q-system (Barton).** $\;Q = \dfrac{RQD}{J_n}\times\dfrac{J_r}{J_a}\times\dfrac{J_w}{SRF}$, where $J_n$ = joint set number, $J_r$ = joint roughness, $J_a$ = joint alteration, $J_w$ = joint water reduction and $SRF$ = stress reduction factor. The three quotients represent block size, inter-block shear strength and active stress. $Q$ ranges from $0.001$ (exceptionally poor) to $1000$ (exceptionally good); classes: $<0.1$ extremely poor, $0.1$ to $1$ very poor, $1$ to $4$ poor, $4$ to $10$ fair, $10$ to $40$ good, $40$ to $100$ very good, $>100$ extremely good. The support is chosen from the chart of $Q$ against the equivalent dimension $D_e = \text{span}/ESR$. Relation: $RMR = 9\ln Q + 44$.

*Comparison.* RMR is additive (six rated parameters, orientation included, results in a class and stand-up time); Q is multiplicative (six parameters, no orientation, wide logarithmic range, and gives support recommendations directly from a chart).

@s 4
**Hazard, vulnerability and risk.**

- *Hazard:* a potentially damaging natural or man-made phenomenon (e.g. a landslide of given size and probability), the probability of occurrence of an event of a given magnitude in a given area and time.
- *Vulnerability:* the degree of loss (from 0, no damage, to 1, total loss) of an element at risk (people, buildings, roads) when a hazard of given intensity occurs.
- *Risk:* the expected loss, $\;\text{Risk} = \text{Hazard} \times \text{Vulnerability} \times \text{Elements at risk (exposure)}$.

**Importance of landslide.** In Nepal landslides are among the most damaging hazards: they block roads and rivers, destroy houses, farmland and hydropower structures, cause loss of life, create landslide-dammed lakes and feed floods and the sediment load of rivers. Understanding landslides is vital for route alignment, slope design, site selection, hazard zonation and early warning.

@s 5
**Site investigation.** Site investigation is the systematic exploration of the ground and groundwater at a proposed site to get the geological, geotechnical and hydrogeological information needed for the safe, economical design and construction of a project. Stages: desk study, reconnaissance, preliminary investigation, detailed investigation (drilling, testing, geophysics) and monitoring during construction.

**Electrical Resistivity Tomography (ERT).** Current is injected through two electrodes and the potential measured at two others along a line of many electrodes (Wenner, Schlumberger or dipole-dipole arrays); the apparent resistivity $\rho_a = K\Delta V/I$ is inverted to give a 2-D (or 3-D) image of true resistivity down to tens of metres. Importance: delineates bedrock depth and weathering, water table and aquifers, clay layers, cavities and karst, fault and shear zones, landslide slip surfaces and saturated zones, and contamination plumes. It is non-invasive, quick and low-cost, and fills the gaps between boreholes (low resistivity for clay and water, high resistivity for dry rock). Limitations: non-unique inversion and need of borehole calibration.

**Multichannel Analysis of Surface Waves (MASW).** A hammer or weight-drop source generates Rayleigh waves recorded by 12 to 48 geophones in a line; the phase velocity of the dispersive Rayleigh waves is plotted against frequency (dispersion curve) and inverted to give the shear-wave velocity profile $V_s(z)$. Importance: $V_s$ gives the small-strain shear modulus $G_{max} = \rho V_s^2$, which is needed for dynamic and earthquake analysis; $V_{s30}$ site classification; depth of bedrock and soft layers, ground improvement checks and liquefaction assessment. It is non-invasive, works under pavements and in urban areas, and gives a continuous profile.

**Stability analysis (kinematic, stereographic).** Data (dip direction / dip): hill slope $160^\circ/40^\circ$, joint set 1 $J_1 = 340^\circ/50^\circ$, joint set 2 $J_2 = 330^\circ/60^\circ$, friction angle $\phi = 30^\circ$.

**1. Plane failure.** A sliding plane must dip towards the slope face (within $\pm20^\circ$ of $160^\circ$) and daylight. $J_1$ ($340^\circ$) and $J_2$ ($330^\circ$) dip almost exactly **opposite** to the face ($180^\circ$ and $170^\circ$ off), i.e. into the hill: **no plane failure**.

**2. Toppling.** Planes dipping into the slope (dip direction within $\pm10^\circ$ of $160^\circ + 180^\circ = 340^\circ$) may topple if $\psi_p \ge 90^\circ - (\psi_f - \phi) = 90^\circ - (40^\circ - 30^\circ) = 80^\circ$. $J_1$ ($340^\circ$, dip $50^\circ$) and $J_2$ ($330^\circ$, dip $60^\circ$) dip in the right direction but only $50^\circ$ and $60^\circ$ against the required $80^\circ$: **no toppling**.

**3. Wedge failure.** Line of intersection of $J_1$ and $J_2$ from the cross product of the poles: **trend $219.7^\circ$, plunge $31.0^\circ$**. Conditions: (a) the line plunges out of the face, i.e. its trend is within $90^\circ$ of the face dip direction: $219.7^\circ - 160^\circ = 59.7^\circ$ ✓; (b) it daylights, $\psi_i = 31.0^\circ < \psi_f = 40^\circ$ ✓; (c) the plunge is steeper than the friction angle, $\psi_i = 31.0^\circ > \phi = 30^\circ$ ✓ is only marginal.

So the **wedge is kinematically possible and the slope is on the edge of stability**. Simple frictional factor of safety (dry) $\approx \dfrac{\tan\phi}{\tan\psi_i} = \dfrac{\tan30^\circ}{\tan31.0^\circ} = 0.96$, i.e. critical, and rain would make it worse.

**Conclusion and remedial design.** The only possible mode is wedge sliding along the $J_1/J_2$ intersection ($219.7^\circ/31.0^\circ$). Reduce the cut slope angle below the plunge of the intersection ($\psi_f \le 30^\circ$ puts the line inside the friction circle), or use benches, rock bolts / dowels in the wedge, surface and horizontal drains, and catch ditches at the toe.

**Glacial Lake Outburst Flood (GLOF) / Landslide Dam Outburst Flood (LDOF).** A *GLOF* is the sudden release of water from a glacial lake dammed by a moraine or ice, triggered by an ice or rock avalanche into the lake, an earthquake, a piping failure, or melting of ice cores. Peak discharges many times the normal flow travel tens to hundreds of kilometres downstream, carrying debris (e.g. the 1985 Dig Tsho event destroyed the Namche small hydropower project; Tsho Rolpa and Imja are among the most dangerous lakes). An *LDOF* occurs when a river blocked by a landslide or rock avalanche forms a lake that overtops or breaches the natural dam (e.g. Kali Gandaki landslide dam 1988, Jure 2014, the Melamchi flood 2021). Both give very rapid, sediment-laden floods, very destructive to dams, bridges and settlements. Mitigation: monitoring, early warning, siphoning or controlled lowering of the lake, spillways, and hazard zoning and risk-sensitive siting.

**Schmidt hammer test.** A simple, non-destructive spring-loaded hammer: a plunger is pressed against the rock surface; the spring-driven mass strikes it and the **rebound number $R$** (0 to 100) is recorded on the scale. Higher rebound means a harder, stronger rock. The average of about 10 readings (discard the lowest) is correlated with unit weight and uniaxial compressive strength using charts or relations such as $\sigma_c = 10^{(0.00088\,\gamma R + 1.01)}$ MPa (Deere and Miller). It is also used for the joint wall compressive strength (JCS) of discontinuities for the Barton shear strength criterion, for assessing weathering, and for concrete strength. Limitations: surface only, the surface must be smooth and unweathered, sensitive to orientation (use correction charts) and to moisture.

**Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR).** An active remote-sensing system carried on satellites or aircraft that transmits microwave pulses to the ground and records the back-scattered signal; the motion of the platform is used to synthesise a long antenna (aperture), giving high-resolution images. It works day and night and through clouds and rain. *InSAR* (interferometric SAR, using the phase difference between two passes) measures ground displacement to millimetre accuracy and is used for landslide and subsidence monitoring, earthquake deformation, glacier and glacial lake changes, flood mapping, and geological mapping of structures and terrain. Satellites include Sentinel-1 (C-band), ALOS PALSAR (L-band) and TerraSAR-X.
