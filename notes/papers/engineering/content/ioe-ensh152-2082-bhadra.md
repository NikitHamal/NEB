@s 1
**(a) Entropy.** Entropy $S$ is the thermodynamic state function defined by $dS = \dfrac{dQ_{rev}}{T}$ (unit J K⁻¹). It measures the degree of disorder (the number of microstates, $S = k_B\ln\Omega$) of a system. For an isolated system it never decreases ($\Delta S\ge0$), and it is constant for a reversible process.

**(b) Role of He in a He-Ne laser.** The lasing atom is neon, but neon cannot be excited efficiently by the electric discharge directly. Helium (about 10 parts He to 1 part Ne) is excited by electron impact to the metastable levels $2^3S$ (19.8 eV) and $2^1S$ (20.6 eV), which almost coincide with the $3s$ (20.66 eV) and $2s$ (19.78 eV) levels of neon. By collisions of the second kind the He atoms transfer their energy to Ne atoms, producing **population inversion** in neon. So helium acts as the **pumping (energy-transfer) medium**.

**(c)** **Yes.** An electric quadrupole is two equal and opposite dipoles placed end to end (charges $+q, -2q, +q$). The dipole moments cancel, so its net charge and **net dipole moment are zero**; the leading term is the quadrupole moment $Q = 2qd^2$.

**(d) Displacement current.** $I_d = \varepsilon_0\dfrac{d\Phi_E}{dt}$.
- It makes Ampère's law consistent: $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0(I + I_d)$, so the current is continuous through a charging capacitor.
- It shows that a **changing electric field produces a magnetic field**; together with Faraday's law this predicts self-propagating electromagnetic waves of speed $c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$.

@s 2
**(a)** $m = 250\ \text{g} = 0.25$ kg, $k = 85$ N/m, $b = 70\ \text{g/s} = 0.07$ kg/s. For a damped oscillator $m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = 0$:
$$\omega' = \sqrt{\frac km - \frac{b^2}{4m^2}} = \sqrt{\frac{85}{0.25} - \left(\frac{0.07}{2\times0.25}\right)^2} = \sqrt{340 - 0.0196}$$
$$\begin{gathered}\boxed{\omega' = 18.4\ \text{rad s}^{-1}} \\ (f = 2.93\ \text{Hz})\end{gathered}$$
The damping is very light: $\omega'$ is almost equal to $\omega_0 = \sqrt{340} = 18.44$ rad/s.

**(b)** The translational kinetic energy of one mole of a monoatomic gas is $\dfrac32RT$ (three degrees of freedom, $\tfrac12RT$ each). Take $R = 8.31$ J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
- At NTP ($T = 273$ K): $E = \dfrac32(8.31)(273) = \boxed{3.40\times10^3\ \text{J}}$
- At $273^\circ$C ($T = 546$ K): $E = \dfrac32(8.31)(546) = \boxed{6.81\times10^3\ \text{J}}$, which is double, since $E\propto T$.

**(c)** For a plane electromagnetic wave in free space, $E = cB$ and $c^2 = 1/\mu_0\varepsilon_0$.
$$u_B = \frac{B^2}{2\mu_0} = \frac{E^2}{2\mu_0c^2} = \frac{E^2}{2\mu_0}\cdot\mu_0\varepsilon_0 = \frac12\varepsilon_0E^2 = u_E$$
$$\boxed{u_B = u_E = \tfrac12\varepsilon_0E^2}$$
So the energy of the wave is shared equally between its electric and magnetic fields.

**(d) Wave-particle duality** is the statement that matter and radiation show both wave and particle properties: light, which shows interference and diffraction, also behaves as photons in the photoelectric and Compton effects. de Broglie proposed that every moving particle has an associated wave.

*Derivation.* For a photon, $E = h\nu$ and $E = pc$, so
$$p = \frac{h\nu}{c} = \frac h\lambda\ \Rightarrow\ \lambda = \frac hp$$
de Broglie assumed this to hold for matter as well. For a particle of mass $m$ and velocity $v$:
$$\boxed{\lambda = \frac hp = \frac{h}{mv}}$$
If a charged particle of charge $q$ is accelerated through potential $V$, $p = \sqrt{2mqV}$ and $\lambda = \dfrac{h}{\sqrt{2mqV}}$; for an electron $\lambda = \dfrac{12.27}{\sqrt V}$ Å. The wave nature was confirmed by the Davisson–Germer electron-diffraction experiment.

@s 3
**Time period of a torsional pendulum.** A disc (moment of inertia $I$ about the wire) hangs from a wire of length $l$ and radius $r$. When it is rotated through $\theta$ the wire twists.

*Torsional constant.* Take a thin co-axial cylindrical shell of radius $x$ and thickness $dx$ in the wire. A twist $\theta$ produces a shear angle $\phi = \dfrac{x\theta}{l}$. The shear stress is $\eta\phi$ ($\eta$ = modulus of rigidity), the force on the end of the shell is $\eta\dfrac{x\theta}{l}\,2\pi x\,dx$, and its moment about the axis is
$$d\tau = \eta\frac{x\theta}{l}\,2\pi x\,dx\cdot x = \frac{2\pi\eta\theta}{l}x^3dx$$
$$\begin{gathered}\tau = \frac{2\pi\eta\theta}{l}\int_0^rx^3dx = \frac{\pi\eta r^4}{2l}\theta = C\theta \\ C = \frac{\pi\eta r^4}{2l}\end{gathered}$$
*Equation of motion.* The restoring couple is $-C\theta$:
$$I\frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} = -C\theta\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{d^2\theta}{dt^2} + \frac CI\theta = 0$$
This is SHM with $\omega = \sqrt{C/I}$:
$$\boxed{T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac IC} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2lI}{\pi\eta r^4}}}$$

**Determination of the rigidity modulus $\eta$.** Squaring the result,
$$\eta = \frac{8\pi lI}{r^4T^2}$$
The moment of inertia of the suspended disc is not known accurately, so use a ring (or two equal masses) of known moment of inertia $I_r$:
1. Measure the length $l$ of the wire and its radius $r$ (screw gauge).
2. Set the disc oscillating by a small twist; time 20 oscillations to get $T_1$ for the disc alone ($I_0$): $T_1^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2I_0}{C}$.
3. Place the ring (known $I_r = \tfrac12M(R_1^2 + R_2^2)$) on the disc and find the new period $T_2$: $T_2^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2(I_0 + I_r)}{C}$.
4. Subtract: $T_2^2 - T_1^2 = \dfrac{4\pi^2I_r}{C}$, so
$$C = \frac{4\pi^2I_r}{T_2^2 - T_1^2}\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\eta = \frac{2lC}{\pi r^4} = \frac{8\pi lI_r}{r^4\left(T_2^2 - T_1^2\right)}}$$
This avoids the unknown $I_0$. (Because $T\propto1/\sqrt\eta$, the amplitude must be small so that the wire stays within its elastic limit.)

**Or. Numerical.** $m = 100\ \text{g} = 0.1$ kg and $b = 0.5$ kg/s. The amplitude and energy decay as
$$\begin{gathered}A = A_0e^{-bt/2m} \\ E = E_0e^{-bt/m}\end{gathered}$$
(valid since $b^2<4mk$ for an oscillating system; the decay depends only on $b/m$).

**(i)** Amplitude falls to $A_0/2$:
$$e^{-bt/2m} = \frac12\ \Rightarrow\ t = \frac{2m}{b}\ln2 = \frac{2(0.1)}{0.5}(0.693) = \boxed{0.277\ \text{s}}$$
**(ii)** Energy falls to $E_0/2$:
$$e^{-bt/m} = \frac12\ \Rightarrow\ t = \frac mb\ln2 = \frac{0.1}{0.5}(0.693) = \boxed{0.139\ \text{s}}$$
The energy halves in half the time the amplitude takes, because $E\propto A^2$.

@s 4
**Sabine's relation.** *Mean free path.* In a hall of volume $V$ and total surface area $S$, the mean free path of a sound wave between two reflections is $\bar d = \dfrac{4V}{S}$, and the time between collisions with the walls is
$$\tau = \frac{\bar d}{c} = \frac{4V}{cS}$$
where $c$ is the speed of sound.

*Decay of sound.* Let the average absorption coefficient of the surfaces be $\bar\alpha$, i.e. a fraction $\bar\alpha$ of the energy is lost at every reflection, and let $E_0$ be the energy density when the source stops. After $n$ reflections,
$$E_n = E_0(1 - \bar\alpha)^n$$
In time $t$ the number of reflections is $n = \dfrac t\tau = \dfrac{cSt}{4V}$, so
$$E(t) = E_0(1 - \bar\alpha)^{cSt/4V} = E_0\exp\left[\frac{cSt}{4V}\ln(1 - \bar\alpha)\right]$$
*Reverberation time.* It is the time $T$ for the sound intensity to fall to $10^{-6}$ of its initial value (a drop of 60 dB):
$$10^{-6} = \exp\left[\frac{cST}{4V}\ln(1 - \bar\alpha)\right]\ \Rightarrow\ T = \frac{24\ln10\ V}{-cS\ln(1 - \bar\alpha)}$$
For small absorption, $-\ln(1 - \bar\alpha)\approx\bar\alpha$, and the total absorption is $A = \bar\alpha S = \sum\alpha_iS_i$:
$$T = \frac{24\ln10}{c}\frac{V}{\bar\alpha S} = \frac{55.3}{343}\frac VA$$
$$\begin{gathered}\boxed{T = \frac{0.161\,V}{\sum\alpha_iS_i}} \\ (V\ \text{in m}^3,\ A\ \text{in m}^2\ \text{sabin})\end{gathered}$$
This is **Sabine's formula**: the reverberation time is directly proportional to the volume of the hall and inversely proportional to the total absorption (taking $c = 343$ m/s at 20°C).

@s 5
**Clausius latent heat equation.** Consider 1 kg of a substance in two phases (condensed phase 1 and saturated vapour 2) in equilibrium at temperature $T$ and the corresponding saturation pressure. $C_1$ and $C_2$ are the specific heats of the two phases *along the saturation (coexistence) curve*, and $L$ is the latent heat of vaporisation at $T$, a function of $T$.

Take a reversible cycle of four steps:
1. Heat 1 kg of the condensed phase along the saturation curve from $T$ to $T + dT$. Heat absorbed: $C_1dT$.
2. At $T + dT$ evaporate it completely. Heat absorbed: $L + dL$.
3. Cool the saturated vapour from $T + dT$ back to $T$ along the saturation curve. Heat absorbed: $-C_2dT$.
4. At $T$ condense it completely. Heat absorbed: $-L$.

The cycle is reversible and returns the substance to its initial state, so the total entropy change is zero, $\oint\dfrac{dQ}{T} = 0$:
$$\frac{C_1dT}{T} + \frac{L + dL}{T + dT} - \frac{C_2dT}{T} - \frac LT = 0$$
Expand $\dfrac{L + dL}{T + dT}\approx\dfrac{L + dL}{T}\left(1 - \dfrac{dT}T\right)\approx\dfrac LT + \dfrac{dL}{T} - \dfrac{L\,dT}{T^2}$ (neglecting second-order terms):
$$\frac{(C_1 - C_2)dT}{T} + \frac{dL}{T} - \frac{L\,dT}{T^2} = 0$$
Multiplying by $T/dT$:
$$(C_1 - C_2) + \frac{dL}{dT} - \frac LT = 0$$
$$\boxed{C_2 - C_1 = \frac{dL}{dT} - \frac LT}$$
This is Clausius' latent heat equation (also called Clapeyron's second latent-heat equation). **Consequence:** for steam $C_2<0$ (the saturated vapour must lose heat when it is compressed so as to stay saturated, i.e. its specific heat along the saturation curve is negative), since $dL/dT<0$ and $L/T>0$.

**Or. Calorific value.** The **calorific value** of a fuel is the total amount of heat liberated when unit mass (1 kg, or 1 m³ for a gas) of the fuel is burnt completely in air or oxygen. Units: J/kg or kcal/kg (J/m³ for gases). The *gross* (higher) value includes the latent heat of the water vapour formed; the *net* (lower) value excludes it:
$$\text{NCV} = \text{GCV} - 0.09\,H\times587\ \ \text{kcal/kg}$$
where $H$ is the percentage of hydrogen in the fuel.

**Bomb calorimeter (construction).**
- A strong stainless-steel **bomb** with a gas-tight screw lid, an oxygen inlet valve and two electrodes. A platinum crucible holding a weighed pellet (0.5–1 g) of the fuel is hung from one electrode, and a fine ignition wire connects the electrodes and touches the fuel.
- The bomb is filled with pure oxygen at 20–25 atm and sunk in a copper **calorimeter** containing a known mass $W$ of water, fitted with a stirrer and a sensitive (Beckmann) thermometer.
- The calorimeter is surrounded by an air jacket and a water jacket to reduce heat loss.

**Working.** The wire is heated electrically and the fuel burns completely at constant volume. The released heat raises the temperature of the bomb, water and calorimeter from $t_1$ to $t_2$ (after correcting for radiation). Let $w$ be the **water equivalent** of the apparatus (found by burning benzoic acid, whose calorific value is known), $m$ the mass of fuel and $\Delta t$ the corrected rise. Then
$$\boxed{\text{CV} = \frac{(W + w)\,\Delta t}{m}}$$
with small corrections subtracted for the heat from the burning fuse wire and from the formation of nitric and sulphuric acids. Because combustion occurs at constant volume with all the water condensed, the value measured is the **gross** calorific value.

**(a) Fringes in a wedge-shaped film.** A wedge-shaped film is bounded by two plane surfaces inclined at a very small angle $\alpha$ and meeting at an edge. When monochromatic light falls nearly normally, the ray reflected from the top surface and the ray reflected from the bottom surface are coherent, since both come from the same incident ray. Because the thickness $t$ varies *linearly* with distance from the edge, the path difference changes from point to point; points of equal thickness lie on straight lines parallel to the edge. Where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths we get a bright line, where it is an odd number of half-wavelengths a dark line. This is why **equally spaced straight fringes parallel to the edge** are seen.

**Fringe width.** Let the film have refractive index $\mu$. For near-normal incidence ($\cos r\approx1$) the path difference between the two reflected rays at thickness $t$ is $2\mu t$, plus $\lambda/2$ due to the phase change of $\pi$ at the upper (rarer-to-denser) reflection. Hence
$$\begin{gathered}\text{bright:}\ 2\mu t = \left(n + \tfrac12\right)\lambda \\ \text{dark:}\ 2\mu t = n\lambda \\ (n = 0, 1, 2,\ldots)\end{gathered}$$
At distance $x$ from the edge, $t = x\tan\alpha\approx x\alpha$. For the $n$th dark fringe at $x_n$ and the $(n+1)$th at $x_{n+1}$:
$$\begin{gathered}2\mu\alpha x_n = n\lambda \\ 2\mu\alpha x_{n+1} = (n + 1)\lambda\end{gathered}$$
Subtracting, the **fringe width** is
$$\boxed{\beta = x_{n+1} - x_n = \frac{\lambda}{2\mu\alpha}}$$
The same value follows for the bright fringes. If the wedge is an air film formed by a wire (or hair) of diameter $d$ at a distance $l$ from the edge, $\alpha = d/l$ and $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda l}{2d}$ (for $\mu = 1$). So $\beta$ is constant (equally spaced fringes), is larger for a smaller wedge angle and increases with wavelength. At the edge ($t = 0$) the fringe is dark in reflected light.

**(b) Double refraction.** When an unpolarised ray enters a doubly-refracting (anisotropic) crystal such as calcite or quartz, it splits into two plane-polarised rays with mutually perpendicular vibrations: the **ordinary ray** ($o$-ray), which obeys Snell's law and has the same speed in all directions, and the **extraordinary ray** ($e$-ray), which does not obey Snell's law and whose speed depends on direction. Along the optic axis there is no splitting.

**Plane and circular light as special cases of elliptical.** Let two plane-polarised waves with perpendicular vibrations, along $x$ and $y$, of amplitudes $a$ and $b$ and phase difference $\delta$, superpose:
$$\begin{gathered}x = a\cos\omega t \\ y = b\cos(\omega t + \delta)\end{gathered}$$
Eliminating $t$ ($\cos\omega t = x/a$, $\sin\omega t = \sqrt{1 - x^2/a^2}$):
$$\boxed{\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{2xy}{ab}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta}$$
This is the equation of an **ellipse** in general: elliptically polarised light.
- If $\delta = 0$ or $\pi$: $\left(\dfrac xa\mp\dfrac yb\right)^2 = 0$, so $y = \pm\dfrac bax$, a **straight line**. The light is **plane polarised** (the ellipse collapses to a line).
- If $\delta = \pm\dfrac\pi2$ and $a = b$: $x^2 + y^2 = a^2$, a **circle**. The light is **circularly polarised**.
- If $\delta = \pm\dfrac\pi2$ and $a\ne b$: $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2} + \dfrac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$, an ellipse with its axes along $x$ and $y$; for any other $\delta$ the ellipse is tilted.

Thus plane and circular polarisation are the limiting forms of the general elliptical polarisation.

**(c) Smallest Bragg angle.** $d = 0.3\ \text{nm} = 3\times10^{-10}$ m and $\lambda = 30\ \text{pm} = 3\times10^{-11}$ m. Bragg's law $2d\sin\theta = n\lambda$ is smallest for $n = 1$:
$$\sin\theta = \frac{\lambda}{2d} = \frac{3\times10^{-11}}{2\times3\times10^{-10}} = 0.05$$
$$\boxed{\theta = 2.87^\circ}$$

**(d) Two separated lenses.** $f_1 = 15$ cm, $f_2 = 20$ cm and $d = 10$ cm.
$$\frac1F = \frac1{f_1} + \frac1{f_2} - \frac{d}{f_1f_2} = \frac1{15} + \frac1{20} - \frac{10}{300} = \frac{4 + 3 - 2}{60} = \frac1{12}$$
$$\boxed{F = 12\ \text{cm}}$$
*Principal points.* The first principal point $H_1$ is at distance $\alpha$ from $L_1$ and the second, $H_2$, at distance $\beta$ from $L_2$:
$$\begin{gathered}\alpha = \frac{Fd}{f_2} = \frac{12\times10}{20} = 6\ \text{cm} \\ \beta = -\frac{Fd}{f_1} = -\frac{12\times10}{15} = -8\ \text{cm}\end{gathered}$$
$H_1$ lies **6 cm to the right of the first lens** and $H_2$ lies **8 cm to the left of the second lens** (that is, 2 cm from $L_1$; the principal planes are "crossed").

*Image position for an object 40 cm in front of $L_1$.* Using the principal planes, the object distance from $H_1$ is $u = 40 + 6 = 46$ cm:
$$\frac1v = \frac1F - \frac1u = \frac1{12} - \frac1{46}\ \Rightarrow\ v = \frac{12\times46}{34} = 16.2\ \text{cm from }H_2$$
Since $H_2$ is 8 cm left of $L_2$, the image is $16.2 - 8 = \boxed{8.2\ \text{cm to the right of the second lens}}$ (that is, 18.2 cm from the first lens).

*Check, lens by lens.* $L_1$: $\dfrac1{v_1} = \dfrac1{15} - \dfrac1{40} = \dfrac5{120}$, so $v_1 = 24$ cm. This image lies 14 cm beyond $L_2$ and acts as a virtual object for it ($u_2 = +14$ cm): $\dfrac1{v_2} = \dfrac1{20} + \dfrac1{14} = \dfrac{17}{140}$, so $v_2 = 8.24$ cm ✓. The image is real, inverted and diminished: $m = \dfrac{24}{-40}\times\dfrac{8.24}{14} = -0.35$.

**(a) Potential of a short dipole.** Let the dipole consist of charges $+q$ and $-q$ separated by $2a$, centred at $O$, with dipole moment $p = q\,(2a)$. A point $P$ is at distance $r$ from $O$ with $OP$ making angle $\theta$ with the dipole axis. The distances from the charges are
$$\begin{gathered}r_1 = \text{(from }+q) \approx r - a\cos\theta \\ r_2 = \text{(from }-q)\approx r + a\cos\theta \\ (r\gg a)\end{gathered}$$
$$V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac1{r_1} - \frac1{r_2}\right) = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{r_2 - r_1}{r_1r_2} = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2a\cos\theta}{r^2 - a^2\cos^2\theta}$$
For $r\gg a$, $r^2 - a^2\cos^2\theta\approx r^2$:
$$\boxed{V = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{\vec p\cdot\hat r}{r^2}}$$
- **Axial line** ($\theta = 0^\circ$ or $180^\circ$): $V = \pm\dfrac{p}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}$ (positive on the side of $+q$).
- **Equatorial line** ($\theta = 90^\circ$): $\cos\theta = 0$, so $\boxed{V = 0}$. (Every point of the perpendicular bisector is at the same distance from $+q$ and $-q$.)

**Or. Field of a charged ring on its axis.** A ring of radius $R$ carries total charge $Q$ uniformly. Take an element $dq = \dfrac{Q}{2\pi R}dl$. At a point on the axis at distance $y$ from the centre, the distance to the element is $s = \sqrt{R^2 + y^2}$. The field of $dq$ has magnitude $dE = \dfrac{dq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0s^2}$. By symmetry the components perpendicular to the axis cancel in pairs, so only the axial part $dE\cos\phi$ survives, where $\cos\phi = y/s$:
$$E = \int\frac{dq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0s^2}\frac ys = \frac{y}{4\pi\varepsilon_0s^3}\int dq$$
$$\boxed{E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{Qy}{(R^2 + y^2)^{3/2}}}$$
along the axis (away from the ring for $Q>0$). At the centre ($y = 0$) $E = 0$, and for $y\gg R$, $E\to\dfrac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0y^2}$ (point charge). The field is largest at $y = R/\sqrt2$, where $E_{\max} = \dfrac{Q}{6\sqrt3\,\pi\varepsilon_0R^2}$.

**(b) Charging an RC circuit.** The charge on the capacitor is $q = q_0\left(1 - e^{-t/\tau}\right)$ with $\tau = RC$. For $q = 0.99q_0$:
$$1 - e^{-t/\tau} = 0.99\ \Rightarrow\ e^{-t/\tau} = 0.01\ \Rightarrow\ \frac t\tau = \ln100$$
$$\boxed{t = 4.6\,\tau}$$
About 4.6 time constants are needed.

@s 8
In a cyclotron the deuteron moves in a semicircle in the magnetic field with $qvB = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$, so $v = \dfrac{qBr}{m}$ and the time for a half circle is $\dfrac{\pi m}{qB}$, independent of speed. The accelerating emf must therefore alternate at the cyclotron (resonance) frequency
$$f = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}$$
**Magnetic flux density:**
$$B = \frac{2\pi fm}{q} = \frac{2\pi(10\times10^6)(3.32\times10^{-27})}{1.6\times10^{-19}} = \boxed{1.30\ \text{T}}$$
**Velocity on emerging** ($r = 0.32$ m):
$$v = \frac{qBr}{m} = 2\pi fr = 2\pi(10^7)(0.32) = \boxed{2.01\times10^{7}\ \text{m s}^{-1}}$$
The kinetic energy is $\tfrac12mv^2 = 6.7\times10^{-13}\ \text{J}\approx4.2$ MeV. Since $v/c\approx0.07$, the non-relativistic treatment is justified.

@s 9
Assume the source is a point source radiating uniformly in all directions. The intensity (average power per unit area) at $d = 1.8$ m is
$$I = \frac{P}{4\pi d^2} = \frac{250}{4\pi(1.8)^2} = 6.14\ \text{W m}^{-2}$$
For a plane electromagnetic wave $I = \dfrac12\varepsilon_0cE_0^2$, so
$$E_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2I}{\varepsilon_0c}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(6.14)}{(8.85\times10^{-12})(3\times10^8)}} = \boxed{68\ \text{V m}^{-1}}$$
$$B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{68}{3\times10^8} = \boxed{2.27\times10^{-7}\ \text{T}}$$
(Maximum values of the fields; the rms values are smaller by $\sqrt2$.)

@s 10
**Quantisation of energy of a particle in an infinite well.** Let the electron be confined to $0<x<a$ with the potential $V = 0$ inside and $V = \infty$ outside and at the walls. The wave function must vanish outside, and by continuity $\psi(0) = \psi(a) = 0$.

Inside the well the time-independent Schrödinger equation is
$$\begin{gathered}-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\psi\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + k^2\psi = 0 \\ k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\end{gathered}$$
The general solution is $\psi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx$.
- At $x = 0$: $\psi(0) = B = 0$, so $\psi = A\sin kx$.
- At $x = a$: $A\sin ka = 0$. For a non-trivial solution ($A\ne0$), $\sin ka = 0$:
$$\begin{gathered}ka = n\pi\ \Rightarrow\ k = \frac{n\pi}{a} \\ n = 1, 2, 3,\ldots\end{gathered}$$
($n = 0$ gives $\psi\equiv0$, no particle.) Substituting $k$ into $k^2 = 2mE/\hbar^2$:
$$\boxed{E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2} = \frac{n^2h^2}{8ma^2}}$$
Only these discrete values are allowed: the energy is **quantised** and is proportional to $n^2$. The boundary conditions on $\psi$, not any extra assumption, force the quantisation.

*Normalisation:* $\int_0^a|\psi|^2dx = A^2\dfrac a2 = 1$ gives $A = \sqrt{2/a}$, so
$$\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac2a}\sin\frac{n\pi x}{a}$$
The lowest energy ($n = 1$), $E_1 = \dfrac{h^2}{8ma^2}$, is the **zero-point energy**: the electron can never be at rest inside the well. The level spacing $E_{n+1} - E_n = (2n + 1)E_1$ increases with $n$.
