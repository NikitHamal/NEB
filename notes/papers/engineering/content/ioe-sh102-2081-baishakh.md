@s 1
**(a) Negative energy.** The energy of a bound system is called negative because the zero of potential energy is taken at infinite separation, where the particles are free and at rest. For an attractive force the system has less energy than the free particles, so its total energy $E = K + U < 0$. The electron in hydrogen is the standard example, $E_n = -13.6/n^2$ eV. The magnitude $|E|$ is the binding energy, the work that must be supplied to separate the system.

**(b) Optical fibre.** It works on **total internal reflection**. The core has refractive index $n_1$ and the cladding has $n_2 < n_1$. A ray travelling in the core that meets the core–cladding boundary at an angle greater than the critical angle $\theta_c = \sin^{-1}(n_2/n_1)$ is reflected back completely into the core. Repeated total reflections carry the light along the fibre with almost no loss, even round bends.

**(c)** Torque on a dipole is $\vec\tau = \vec p\times\vec E$, so $\tau = pE\sin\theta$. It is maximum, $\tau_{\max} = pE$, when $\theta = 90^\circ$, i.e. when the dipole is **perpendicular to the field**.

**(d) Maxwell's modification.** Ampere's law $\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0 I$ is not valid when the current is not continuous. While a capacitor charges, current $I$ flows in the wire but none crosses the gap, so two surfaces bounded by the same loop (one cutting the wire, one passing between the plates) give different answers. It also contradicts the continuity equation, because $\nabla\cdot(\nabla\times\vec B) = 0$ whereas $\nabla\cdot\vec J = -\partial\rho/\partial t \ne 0$. Maxwell added the **displacement current** $I_d = \varepsilon_0\,\dfrac{d\Phi_E}{dt}$:
$$\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0\left(I + \varepsilon_0\frac{d\Phi_E}{dt}\right)$$
A changing electric field therefore produces a magnetic field. This symmetry with Faraday's law predicts electromagnetic waves.

@s 2
**(a)** The equation of motion is $m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = 0$. With $\gamma = b/2m$ and $\omega_0^2 = k/m$ the underdamped solution is
$$\begin{gathered}x = A_0e^{-bt/2m}\cos(\omega' t + \phi) \\ \omega' = \sqrt{\omega_0^2 - b^2/4m^2}\end{gathered}$$
So the amplitude is $\boxed{A(t) = A_0\,e^{-bt/2m}}$, decaying exponentially with time. The three cases are:
- **Underdamped** ($b/2m < \omega_0$): oscillations inside a decaying envelope.
- **Critically damped** ($b/2m = \omega_0$): the fastest return to equilibrium with no oscillation.
- **Overdamped** ($b/2m > \omega_0$): slow, non-oscillatory creep back to equilibrium.

**(b)** Heat absorbed at constant temperature $T = 100^\circ\text{C} = 373$ K:
$$Q = mL = 5000\ \text{g}\times540\ \text{cal/g} = 2.7\times10^6\ \text{cal}$$
$$\Delta S = \frac QT = \frac{2.7\times10^6}{373} = \boxed{7.24\times10^3\ \text{cal/K}\ (\approx 3.03\times10^4\ \text{J/K})}$$

**(c)** In an electromagnetic wave $E_0/B_0 = c$, so
$$B_0 = \frac{E_0}{c} = \frac{2}{3\times10^8} = \boxed{6.67\times10^{-9}\ \text{T}}$$
The distance of 10 m is not needed because $E_0$ at that point is given.

**(d) Heisenberg's uncertainty principle.** It is impossible to determine simultaneously and exactly both the position and the momentum of a particle. If $\Delta x$ and $\Delta p_x$ are the uncertainties,
$$\Delta x\,\Delta p_x \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}\quad\left(\text{often written }\ge\frac h{4\pi}\right)$$
The same applies to energy and time, $\Delta E\,\Delta t\ge\hbar/2$. *Explanation:* to locate an electron with a microscope we must use light of short wavelength $\lambda$, so $\Delta x\sim\lambda$. But a photon of that wavelength carries momentum $h/\lambda$ and the Compton recoil it gives the electron makes $\Delta p\sim h/\lambda$. Then $\Delta x\,\Delta p\sim h$. The limit is a property of nature, not of the instrument, and matters only on the atomic scale. For example it explains why an electron cannot exist inside a nucleus.

@s 3
**Bar (compound) pendulum.** Let the bar of mass $m$ be pivoted at a point at distance $l$ from the centre of gravity $G$, and let $k$ be its radius of gyration about $G$. The moment of inertia about the pivot is $I = mk^2 + ml^2$. For a small angular displacement $\theta$ the restoring torque is $-mgl\sin\theta\approx -mgl\,\theta$, so
$$I\ddot\theta = -mgl\,\theta\ \Rightarrow\ \omega^2 = \frac{mgl}{m(k^2+l^2)}\ \Rightarrow\ T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{k^2 + l^2}{gl}}$$
For a minimum, set $\dfrac{dT}{dl} = 0$. Since $T^2\propto\dfrac{k^2}{l} + l$,
$$\frac{d}{dl}\left(\frac{k^2}{l} + l\right) = -\frac{k^2}{l^2} + 1 = 0\ \Rightarrow\ l = k$$
The second derivative is $\dfrac{2k^2}{l^3} > 0$, so this is a minimum. Hence the time period is minimum when the distance of the pivot from the centre of gravity equals the radius of gyration, and
$$\boxed{T_{\min} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{2k}{g}}}$$

**Or.** The equation of motion of the driven oscillator is $m\ddot x + b\dot x + kx = F_0\cos\alpha t$. In the steady state $x = A\cos(\alpha t - \varphi)$ with
$$\begin{gathered}A = \frac{F_0}{\sqrt{m^2(\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2)^2 + b^2\alpha^2}} \\ \tan\varphi = \frac{b\alpha}{m(\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2)}\end{gathered}$$
Here $\omega_0^2 = k/m = 100/0.1 = 1000\ \text{s}^{-2}$ and $\alpha^2 = 2500\ \text{s}^{-2}$. Then $m(\omega_0^2 - \alpha^2) = 0.1(1000 - 2500) = -150$ and $b\alpha = 1\times50 = 50$.
$$A = \frac{2}{\sqrt{150^2 + 50^2}} = \frac{2}{158.1} = \boxed{1.26\times10^{-2}\ \text{m} = 1.26\ \text{cm}}$$
$$\tan\varphi = \frac{50}{-150} = -\frac13\ \Rightarrow\ \varphi = 180^\circ - 18.4^\circ = \boxed{161.6^\circ\ (2.82\ \text{rad})}$$
The displacement **lags** the driving force by $161.6^\circ$. The driving frequency is above the natural frequency ($\omega_0 = 31.6$ rad/s), so the phase lag is more than $90^\circ$.

@s 4
**Reverberation** is the persistence of sound in a hall after the source has stopped, caused by repeated reflections from the walls, ceiling and floor. The **reverberation time** $T$ is the time taken for the sound intensity to fall to $10^{-6}$ of its initial value (a drop of 60 dB).

**Sabine's relation.** Let the hall have volume $V$, total surface area $S$ and mean absorption coefficient $\bar\alpha$, and let $c$ be the speed of sound. For a diffuse sound field the mean free path between two reflections is
$$\bar\lambda = \frac{4V}{S}$$
so the number of reflections per second is $n = \dfrac{c}{\bar\lambda} = \dfrac{cS}{4V}$. At each reflection the energy is multiplied by $(1-\bar\alpha)$. After time $t$ there have been $nt$ reflections, so the energy density is
$$E = E_0(1-\bar\alpha)^{nt}\ \Rightarrow\ \ln\frac{E}{E_0} = \frac{cSt}{4V}\ln(1-\bar\alpha)\approx -\frac{cS\bar\alpha}{4V}\,t\quad(\bar\alpha\ \text{small})$$
Putting $E/E_0 = 10^{-6}$ at $t = T$:
$$\frac{cS\bar\alpha}{4V}T = 6\ln10 = 13.8\ \Rightarrow\ T = \frac{4\times13.8\,V}{c\,S\bar\alpha}$$
With $c = 343$ m/s and $S\bar\alpha = \sum\alpha_iS_i$ (the total absorption in metric sabins),
$$\boxed{T = \frac{0.161\,V}{\sum\alpha_iS_i}}$$
This is **Sabine's formula**. A larger hall or less absorption gives a longer reverberation time.

@s 5
**Pyrheliometer.** A pyrheliometer is an instrument that measures the intensity (heat energy per unit area per unit time) of **direct solar radiation**. The Ångström electrical compensation pyrheliometer is the standard type.

**Construction.** It has two identical thin strips $A$ and $B$ of blackened manganin (or platinum). A fine thermocouple junction is soldered to the back of each strip, and the two junctions are connected in opposition to a sensitive galvanometer $G$. The strips sit side by side in a metal tube with a series of diaphragms that cut out stray light and define the area exposed to the sun. A shutter allows either strip to be covered. Each strip can be heated electrically by a battery, rheostat and ammeter in a separate circuit.

**Working.** Strip $A$ is exposed to the sun and warms up, while $B$ is shaded. The galvanometer shows a deflection because of the temperature difference. A current $i$ is now passed through $B$, and adjusted until the deflection becomes **zero**. The two strips are then at the same temperature, so the heat that radiation delivers to $A$ equals the electrical heat in $B$. If $R$ is the resistance of a strip, $a$ its absorption coefficient and $A_s$ its exposed area,
$$S\,a\,A_s = i^2R\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{S = \frac{i^2R}{a\,A_s}}$$
The strips are then interchanged (so that $B$ is exposed and $A$ is heated) and the mean of the two readings is taken to cancel any small difference between the strips. Multiplying $S$ by the Earth–Sun distance factor and atmospheric correction gives the solar constant.

**Or. Clausius–Clapeyron equation.** Take a substance of unit mass at $T$ and pressure $P$ at which a change of phase occurs, with specific volumes $V_1$ (initial phase) and $V_2$ (final phase). Let a Carnot cycle run between $T$ and $T - dT$, the corresponding saturation pressures being $P$ and $P - dP$.
- Isothermal at $T$: the substance changes phase and absorbs the latent heat $L$ ($V_1\to V_2$).
- Adiabatic expansion to $T - dT$, then isothermal compression at $T - dT$ (rejecting heat), then adiabatic compression back.

The cycle is an infinitesimally thin parallelogram, so the work done is $W = dP\,(V_2 - V_1)$. The efficiency of a Carnot cycle is $\eta = \dfrac{W}{Q_1} = \dfrac{dT}{T}$, so
$$\frac{dP\,(V_2 - V_1)}{L} = \frac{dT}{T}\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{L}{T(V_2 - V_1)}}$$
**Boiling point.** For liquid $\to$ vapour, $V_2\gg V_1$ and $L>0$, so $dP/dT>0$. An increase of pressure **raises** the boiling point (water boils above $100^\circ$C in a pressure cooker, and below it on a mountain).

**Melting point.** For solid $\to$ liquid, $L>0$.
- For most substances the liquid has the larger volume ($V_2>V_1$), so $dP/dT>0$ and pressure **raises** the melting point.
- For ice, water is denser ($V_2<V_1$), so $dP/dT<0$ and an increase of pressure **lowers** the melting point (about $0.0075$ K per atm). Pressure under a skate blade melts the ice beneath it, which explains regelation.

@s 6
**(a) Soap film.** Light is incident at $i$ with $\sin i = 4/5$, $\mu = 1.33$. By Snell's law
$$\begin{gathered}\sin r = \frac{\sin i}{\mu} = \frac{0.8}{1.33} = 0.6015 \\ \cos r = \sqrt{1 - 0.3618} = 0.7989\end{gathered}$$
For a film in air, reflection at the top surface involves a phase change of $\pi$, so the reflected light is **dark** when
$$2\mu t\cos r = n\lambda$$
The two consecutive dark bands at $\lambda_1 = 6.1\times10^{-5}$ cm and $\lambda_2 = 6.0\times10^{-5}$ cm "overlap" means they are dark at the same thickness with adjacent orders, $n$ for the longer wavelength and $n+1$ for the shorter:
$$n\lambda_1 = (n+1)\lambda_2\ \Rightarrow\ n(6.1 - 6.0) = 6.0\ \Rightarrow\ n = 60$$
$$t = \frac{n\lambda_1}{2\mu\cos r} = \frac{60\times6.1\times10^{-5}}{2\times1.33\times0.7989} = \frac{3.66\times10^{-3}}{2.125}$$
$$\boxed{t = 1.72\times10^{-3}\ \text{cm} = 17.2\ \mu\text{m}}$$
Check with the second wavelength: $2\mu t\cos r = 61\times6.0\times10^{-5} = 3.66\times10^{-3}$ cm ✓.

**(b) Retardation plates.** A retardation (wave) plate is a thin slice of a doubly refracting crystal (quartz, calcite, mica) cut **parallel to the optic axis**. Light entering normally splits into the $e$-ray (vibrating parallel to the axis) and $o$-ray (vibrating perpendicular to it), which travel in the same direction at different speeds. On emerging they have an optical path difference $(\mu_o - \mu_e)t$, i.e. a phase difference $\delta$ (a retardation). The quarter-wave plate ($\delta = \pi/2$) and half-wave plate ($\delta = \pi$) are the usual ones.

**Thickness for elliptical light.** Let plane polarised light of amplitude $A$ fall on the plate with its vibration making an angle $\theta$ with the optic axis. The components are $a = A\cos\theta$ (e-ray) and $b = A\sin\theta$ (o-ray). After the plate,
$$\begin{gathered}x = a\cos\omega t \\ y = b\cos(\omega t + \delta) \\ \delta = \frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(\mu_o - \mu_e)t\end{gathered}$$
Eliminating $t$:
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{2xy}{ab}\cos\delta = \sin^2\delta$$
This is an ellipse for general $\delta$. For a **quarter-wave plate**, $\delta = (2n+1)\pi/2$ and $\cos\delta = 0$:
$$\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} = 1$$
an ellipse with its axes along and across the optic axis. It is elliptically (not circularly) polarised provided $a\ne b$, i.e. $\theta\ne45^\circ$ (and $\theta\ne0^\circ, 90^\circ$). Setting $\delta = (2n+1)\pi/2$ gives the thickness:
$$\boxed{t = \frac{(2n+1)\lambda}{4\,|\mu_o - \mu_e|},\qquad n = 0, 1, 2,\ldots}$$
The thinnest plate has $t = \lambda/4|\mu_o - \mu_e|$. For quartz at 5890 Å, $|\mu_e - \mu_o| = 0.0092$, giving $t\approx0.016$ mm. At $\theta = 45^\circ$ the same plate gives circularly polarised light.

**(c) Single slit.** For a slit of width $a$ the intensity at angle $\theta$ is
$$\begin{gathered}I = I_0\left(\frac{\sin\alpha}{\alpha}\right)^2 \\ \alpha = \frac{\pi a\sin\theta}{\lambda}\end{gathered}$$
where $I_0$ is the principal (central) maximum. Minima occur at $\alpha = \pm\pi, \pm2\pi,\ldots$. Setting $dI/d\alpha = 0$ gives $\tan\alpha = \alpha$, whose roots are close to $\alpha\approx\pm\tfrac32\pi,\ \pm\tfrac52\pi,\ldots$ (the secondary maxima lie midway between minima). For the first secondary maximum, $\alpha\approx\tfrac{3\pi}{2}$, $\sin\alpha = -1$:
$$\frac{I_1}{I_0} = \left(\frac{\sin(3\pi/2)}{3\pi/2}\right)^2 = \frac{4}{9\pi^2} = 0.045$$
$$\boxed{I_1 \approx 4.5\%\ \text{of}\ I_0}$$
(Using the exact root $\alpha = 4.493$ of $\tan\alpha = \alpha$ gives 4.7%, so $4.5\%$ is the usual approximation.) The second secondary maximum has $4/(25\pi^2) = 1.6\%$.

**(d) Two lenses.** For the first lens $u_1 = -4$ cm, $f_1 = 3$ cm. Using $\dfrac1v - \dfrac1u = \dfrac1f$:
$$\frac1{v_1} = \frac13 - \frac14 = \frac1{12}\ \Rightarrow\ v_1 = +12\ \text{cm}$$
The first image $I_1$ forms 12 cm to the right of $L_1$. The second lens is 2 cm from $L_1$, so $I_1$ is $12 - 2 = 10$ cm **beyond** $L_2$: it acts as a virtual object, $u_2 = +10$ cm, with $f_2 = 4$ cm.
$$\frac1{v_2} = \frac1{f_2} + \frac1{u_2} = \frac14 + \frac1{10} = \frac7{20}\ \Rightarrow\ v_2 = +\frac{20}7 = 2.86\ \text{cm}$$
Magnification $m = m_1m_2 = \dfrac{v_1}{u_1}\cdot\dfrac{v_2}{u_2} = (-3)\left(\dfrac{2}{7}\right) = -\dfrac67$.

**Result:** the final image is **2.86 cm to the right of the second lens** (4.86 cm from the first lens), **real, inverted** and **diminished** ($\tfrac67$ of the object size).

@s 7
**(a) Field on the axis of a ring.** Let the ring of radius $R$ carry charge $Q$ uniformly and let $P$ be at distance $z$ on the axis. An element $dq = Q\,dl/2\pi R$ is at distance $r = \sqrt{R^2 + z^2}$ from $P$ and produces
$$dE = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{dq}{r^2}$$
By symmetry the components perpendicular to the axis cancel in pairs, so only the axial part survives, $dE_z = dE\cos\theta$ with $\cos\theta = z/r$:
$$E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\int\frac{dq}{r^2}\frac zr = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{zQ}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}$$
**Maximum.** Set $\dfrac{dE}{dz} = 0$:
$$\frac{d}{dz}\left[\frac{z}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\right] = \frac{(R^2+z^2) - 3z^2}{(R^2+z^2)^{5/2}} = 0\ \Rightarrow\ R^2 = 2z^2\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{z = \frac{R}{\sqrt2}}$$
For $z$ just below this value $dE/dz>0$, and just above it $dE/dz<0$, so it is a maximum, with
$$E_{\max} = \frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{2}{3\sqrt3\,R^2}$$

**Or. Potential of a dipole.** Let a dipole of charges $+q$ at $B$ and $-q$ at $A$, separation $2a$, centre $O$, dipole moment $p = 2aq$. For a point $P$ at distance $r$ from $O$ with $OP$ making angle $\theta$ with the dipole axis, let $BP = r_1$ and $AP = r_2$. Then
$$V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\left(\frac1{r_1} - \frac1{r_2}\right)$$
By the cosine rule, $r_1^2 = r^2 + a^2 - 2ar\cos\theta$ and $r_2^2 = r^2 + a^2 + 2ar\cos\theta$. For $r\gg a$, neglecting $a^2$:
$$\begin{gathered}\frac1{r_1}\approx\frac1r\left(1 + \frac{a\cos\theta}{r}\right) \\ \frac1{r_2}\approx\frac1r\left(1 - \frac{a\cos\theta}{r}\right)\end{gathered}$$
$$V = \frac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\cdot\frac{2a\cos\theta}{r^2} = \boxed{\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2}}$$
**Axial line.** On the axis (end-on), $\theta = 0$ on the side of $+q$:
$$V_{\text{axial}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{p}{r^2}$$
(exactly, $V = \dfrac{q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\dfrac{2a}{r^2 - a^2}$, which reduces to this for $r\gg a$). It is $-p/4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2$ on the side of $-q$ ($\theta = 180^\circ$). On the equatorial line ($\theta = 90^\circ$) $V = 0$.

**(b)** Initially $C_0 = \varepsilon_0A/d = 1.32$ pF. After doubling the separation and filling the gap with wax of dielectric constant $K$:
$$C = \frac{K\varepsilon_0A}{2d} = \frac K2C_0\ \Rightarrow\ K = \frac{2C}{C_0} = \frac{2\times2.57}{1.32} = \boxed{3.89}$$

@s 8
In a cyclotron the magnetic force provides the centripetal force: $qvB = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}$, so $v = \dfrac{qBr}{m}$ and the period $T = \dfrac{2\pi m}{qB}$ is independent of speed. Resonance requires the applied frequency to equal this cyclotron frequency:
$$f = \frac{qB}{2\pi m}\ \Rightarrow\ B = \frac{2\pi fm}{q} = \frac{2\pi\times10^7\times3.32\times10^{-27}}{1.6\times10^{-19}}$$
$$\boxed{B = 1.30\ \text{T}}$$
The speed at the exit radius $r = 0.32$ m is
$$v = 2\pi fr = 2\pi\times10^7\times0.32 = \boxed{2.01\times10^7\ \text{m/s}}$$
(Check: $qBr/m = 1.6\times10^{-19}\times1.304\times0.32/3.32\times10^{-27} = 2.01\times10^7$ m/s ✓.) This is about 6.7% of $c$, so ignoring relativity is justified. The kinetic energy is $\tfrac12mv^2 = 6.7\times10^{-13}$ J $\approx4.2$ MeV.

**Particle in an infinitely deep well.** Let the potential be $V = 0$ for $0<x<L$ and $V = \infty$ for $x\le0$ and $x\ge L$. The particle cannot be outside, so $\psi = 0$ there. Inside, the time-independent Schrödinger equation is
$$\begin{gathered}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + k^2\psi = 0 \\ k^2 = \frac{2mE}{\hbar^2}\end{gathered}$$
The general solution is $\psi = A\sin kx + B\cos kx$. The wavefunction must be continuous, so it must vanish at the walls.
- At $x = 0$: $\psi(0) = B = 0$, so $\psi = A\sin kx$.
- At $x = L$: $A\sin kL = 0$. Since $A\ne0$, $kL = n\pi$, $n = 1, 2, 3,\ldots$ ($n=0$ gives $\psi\equiv0$).

So $k = n\pi/L$ and, since $E = \hbar^2k^2/2m$,
$$\boxed{E_n = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2mL^2} = \frac{n^2h^2}{8mL^2},\qquad n = 1, 2, 3,\ldots}$$
Only these discrete values are allowed, so the energy is **quantized**. The ground state is $E_1 = h^2/8mL^2$, never zero (zero-point energy), and the spacing grows as $n^2$.

**Wavefunction.** Normalise: $\displaystyle\int_0^L A^2\sin^2\frac{n\pi x}{L}\,dx = A^2\frac L2 = 1\ \Rightarrow\ A = \sqrt{2/L}$.
$$\boxed{\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac2L}\,\sin\frac{n\pi x}{L},\qquad 0<x<L}$$
$\psi_n$ has $n-1$ nodes inside the well.
