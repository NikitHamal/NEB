@s 1
| Property | Definition | Measure | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strength** | Maximum stress a material can withstand before yielding or failing (compression, tension, shear, bending) | Yield stress or ultimate stress (N/mm²), the height of the stress-strain curve | High-tensile steel, about 500 to 600 N/mm² in tension; granite has high compressive strength |
| **Hardness** | Resistance of the surface to indentation, scratching, abrasion or wear | Brinell, Rockwell, Vickers or Mohs scale; indentation test | Diamond (Mohs 10), granite for flooring, hardened steel for tools |
| **Toughness** | Ability to absorb energy (by plastic deformation) up to fracture, resisting impact and shock without breaking | Area under the stress-strain curve; Charpy or Izod impact test (joules) | Mild steel, timber and rubber are tough; glass is hard but not tough |

How they differ: strength refers to the stress capacity, hardness to the surface resistance, and toughness to the energy absorbed before fracture. A material can be hard but brittle (glass, cast iron, hardened steel: low toughness), strong but not tough (concrete in compression), or tough yet moderately strong (rubber, mild steel). Material selection needs all three: e.g. a rail needs strength, hardness (wear) and toughness (impact).

@s 2
**Dressing of stones** is the process of working a stone, after it is quarried, to the required shape, size, and surface finish, so that it can be used in masonry, flooring or facing. It also removes irregular projections and gives bed and joint faces that fit properly.

**Methods of dressing**

1. **Hand dressing** (done by skilled masons using hammers, chisels, punches, pitching tools, axes and drafting chisels): quarry stone is first *scabbled* or *spalled* with a hammer to remove rough edges; the *rough hammer-dressed* finish is then improved by *chisel-drafting* (draft margins cut around the edge), *punched*, *tooled* (parallel chisel marks), *boasted* and *polished*.
2. **Machine dressing** (done in factories): stone is cut into slabs by saws (diamond or wire saws), planed, turned, chamfered, ground and polished by polishing machines, giving accurate dimensions, high output and better finish, used for marble, granite and ashlar work.

Types of finish: rough-tooled (rock-faced), hammer-dressed, chisel-drafted, plain ashlar, tooled, sunk or moulded, and polished.

@s 3
**Constituents of brick earth and their roles**

| Constituent | Per cent | Role in manufacture and effect on brick |
|---|---|---|
| Alumina (clay) | 20 to 30 | Plasticity for moulding, binds the particles, holds the shape. Too much: shrinks, warps and cracks on drying and burning |
| Silica (free sand) | 50 to 60 | Prevents shrinkage, cracking and warping; gives strength, hardness and durability; the brick keeps its shape. Too much: brittle, weak |
| Lime | 3 to 5 (max. 10) | Flux: melts at kiln temperature and binds the silica; reduces shrinkage. Lumps give lime blowing (pitting); too much makes the brick lose shape (vitrification) |
| Iron oxide | 5 to 6 | Lowers the fusion temperature of the silica (flux), gives the red colour and hardness. More makes the colour dark blue or black |
| Magnesia | about 1 | In small quantity gives a yellow tint and lowers shrinkage; excess causes decay |

Harmful matter: alkalies (efflorescence, fusion), pebbles, gravel and lumps of lime (weak spots), organic matter (porous bricks) and sulphates.

**Properties of good bricks.** Uniform size, shape, with sharp edges and plane faces; uniform red colour; hard and well burnt so that it gives a clear ringing sound when two bricks are struck; compressive strength not less than 10 N/mm² for first class bricks; water absorption not more than 20 % after 24 hours; free from cracks, lumps of lime and soluble salts (no efflorescence); not scratched by the finger nail; fracture is uniform, bright and homogeneous; low thermal conductivity, and good fire resistance.

@s 4
**Fat lime** (high calcium lime) is made by burning almost pure limestone; the quicklime produced is slaked to give a smooth, white, rich paste. Storage and handling follow its behaviour with moisture, CO$_2$ and heat.

**Storage**

- *Quicklime* must be kept dry: it absorbs moisture and CO$_2$ and air-slakes into a useless powder. Store in lumps in a dry, closed, well-roofed shed with a raised plank or concrete floor and thick walls, away from rain and from combustible materials, and use it as soon as possible. Powdered quicklime and hydrated lime are stored in air-tight bags or drums, in a dry place.
- *Slaked fat lime* is made into lime putty: slaked in a masonry pit, with just enough water (about 2.5 to 3 times the volume) and covered with a layer of sand or water to keep the air (CO$_2$) out; matured for at least 2 to 3 weeks, in which time it improves in plasticity (and it can be kept for months or years).
- Keep the stored lime away from children, and do not stack it near timber (heat of slaking can start a fire).

**Handling**

- Slake quicklime slowly in a pit or box, by adding water to the lime (not the lime to the water in big quantity) and stirring with a long-handled hoe, as it generates a lot of heat and can spit; keep the lime and water mix covered.
- Wear gloves, goggles, boots and a dust mask; it is caustic and burns the skin and eyes. In case of contact wash with plenty of water.
- Use only the amount needed for the day; mix the slaked lime with sand (1:2 or 1:3) shortly before use, and protect the finished work from rain and rapid drying until carbonated.

@s 5
**Compounds of cement clinker (Bogue's compounds).** The principal oxides (CaO about 60 to 67 %, SiO$_2$ 17 to 25 %, Al$_2$O$_3$ 3 to 8 %, Fe$_2$O$_3$ 0.5 to 6 %) combine in the kiln to give four main compounds.

| Compound | Name and formula | Per cent | Function in cement |
|---|---|---|---|
| C$_3$S | Tricalcium silicate (alite), 3CaO·SiO$_2$ | 40 to 55 | Hydrates fast and gives most of the *early* strength (up to 7 days) and high heat of hydration; sets in a few hours |
| C$_2$S | Dicalcium silicate (belite), 2CaO·SiO$_2$ | 15 to 30 | Hydrates slowly; gives the *later* strength (after 28 days, up to a year), low heat, better resistance to chemical attack |
| C$_3$A | Tricalcium aluminate, 3CaO·Al$_2$O$_3$ | 5 to 11 | Reacts at once with water (flash set), controlled by gypsum; large heat; contributes to early strength little; low resistance to sulphate attack |
| C$_4$AF | Tetracalcium alumino-ferrite, 4CaO·Al$_2$O$_3$·Fe$_2$O$_3$ | 8 to 14 | Acts as a flux in the kiln (lowers the clinkering temperature), gives the grey colour, adds little strength |

(Gypsum, 2 to 4 %, is added to clinker to regulate the setting of C$_3$A.)

**Why pozzolana is used in PPC.** A pozzolana (fly ash, calcined clay, volcanic ash, silica fume) is a siliceous or alumino-siliceous material which has no binding property alone, but in the presence of water reacts with the calcium hydroxide Ca(OH)$_2$ (liberated when C$_3$S and C$_2$S hydrate) to form extra cementitious calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H). It is added (15 to 35 %) because it:

- uses the weak, soluble lime, which improves durability, lowers permeability and gives better resistance to sulphates, chlorides, acids and sea water, and to alkali-silica reaction,
- reduces the heat of hydration (suitable for mass concrete, dams),
- improves workability and the later (long-term) strength,
- is cheaper, reduces the clinker content and CO$_2$ emission, and uses industrial waste; though the early strength gain is slower, and longer curing is needed.

@s 6
| Point | Mud mortar | Cement mortar |
|---|---|---|
| Binding material | Clay or earth (sometimes mixed with straw, cow dung, lime) | Portland cement |
| Mix | Mud, sand and water, no fixed ratio | Cement : sand 1:3 to 1:6 by volume with water |
| Setting | Dries and hardens by evaporation; no chemical reaction | Sets and hardens by hydration, even under water |
| Strength | Very low (< 0.5 N/mm²), weak in tension | High (5 to 20 N/mm²) |
| Water resistance | Poor, it softens and washes out when wet | Good and durable |
| Cost | Cheapest, available locally | Costly |
| Workability | Easy; can be reworked | Needs timely use (30 min) and curing |
| Uses | Rural low-cost houses, temporary structures, mud and stone walls in dry areas, bonded plaster (lipai) | Load-bearing masonry, foundations, plastering, pointing, RCC works, water retaining structures |

@s 7
**Methods of preserving timber.** Timber is preserved against fungi (decay), insects (termites, borers), fire and weather by poisoning or by sealing the surface and cells with preservatives, after proper seasoning.

1. **Painting, varnishing, or coating** with tar or oil paint: only the surface is protected; the timber must be dry first.
2. **Charring:** the surface of the portion buried in the soil (post ends) is burnt to a depth of 10 to 15 mm; the layer of carbon prevents fungi and insects.
3. **Creosoting:** impregnation by brush, spray, dipping or *hot and cold bath* (hot at 90 to 100 °C then cold bath, so that cooling creates a vacuum and sucks in the oil) with coal-tar creosote. Used for poles, sleepers, fence posts, piles.
4. **Pressure impregnation:** *Bethell (full-cell)* process, the timber in a closed cylinder under vacuum, and preservative is forced in at 8 to 14 kg/cm²; *Lowry* and *Rueping* (empty-cell) processes use less preservative and leave the cells coated.
5. **Water-borne salts:** copper sulphate, zinc chloride, copper-chrome-arsenic (ASCU or CCA), borax and boric acid solutions are applied by dipping or pressure. Odourless and paintable, used inside buildings.
6. **Sap displacement (Boucherie process):** a preservative (copper sulphate) is passed under gravity into the butt end of a freshly cut log, which displaces the sap.
7. **Oil-borne (creosote, pentachlorophenol) and fire-retardant treatments:** for timber exposed to weather or where fire resistance is needed (ammonium phosphate, borax).

@s 8
**Metals and alloys.** A *metal* is a pure chemical element which is lustrous, hard (generally), ductile and malleable, and a good conductor of heat and electricity, with a crystalline structure, e.g. iron, copper, aluminium, zinc, lead. An *alloy* is a mixture of two or more elements (at least one a metal) fused together and solidified, having metallic properties which differ from (and are usually better than) those of its constituents, e.g. steel (Fe + C), brass (Cu + Zn), bronze (Cu + Sn), duralumin (Al + Cu).

**Surface hardening (case hardening).** In surface hardening the outer layer (case) of a steel part is made hard and wear resistant while the core stays soft, tough and ductile. It is useful for gears, cams, shafts, bearings and tools, which need a hard surface against wear and a tough core to bear shocks. Processes:

| Process | Method |
|---|---|
| Carburising | The low-carbon steel (0.1 to 0.25 % C) is heated to 900 to 950 °C in contact with a carbon-rich medium (pack: charcoal, gas: methane, liquid: cyanide bath), so that carbon diffuses into the surface (0.5 to 2 mm), and the part is then quenched and tempered |
| Nitriding | Alloy steel (Al, Cr, Mo) is heated to 500 to 550 °C in ammonia gas, and hard nitrides form at the surface (no quenching is needed, less distortion) |
| Cyaniding and carbonitriding | Heating in a bath of sodium cyanide (or in a gas of ammonia and carbon) which adds both carbon and nitrogen to a thin case; then quenching |
| Flame hardening | The surface is heated rapidly with an oxy-acetylene flame above the critical temperature, and at once quenched with a water spray |
| Induction hardening | A high frequency current heats the surface layer, which is then quenched |

The result is a very hard surface (Rockwell C 55 to 65) over a tough core.

@s 9
**Ingredients of paint**

| Ingredient | Examples | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Base | White lead, zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, red lead, iron oxide | Forms the body of the film, gives opacity, covering power and protection |
| Vehicle (carrier) | Linseed oil, tung oil, alkyd and acrylic resins | Holds the pigment, spreads it and forms the dried, tough, adhering film |
| Pigment | Chrome yellow, ultramarine, carbon black | Gives the colour |
| Drier | Litharge, manganese or cobalt salts | Speeds up the drying of the oil film |
| Thinner (solvent) | Turpentine, mineral spirit, water | Brings the paint to a brushing consistency, evaporates on drying |
| Extender | Barytes, gypsum, silica, china clay | Reduces the cost, adds body and hardness |

**Characteristics of a good paint**

- It spreads easily and uniformly with a brush or spray, and has good covering (hiding) power.
- It dries within a reasonable time to a hard, smooth, glossy or matt film, with no stickiness or cracks.
- The film is durable, elastic, adheres well, and does not crack, flake or peel.
- It resists weather, sunlight, moisture, chemicals and fungal growth, and keeps its colour (does not fade or chalk).
- It does not settle or form a skin in the container, and is economical, non-toxic and washable.

@s 10
**(a) Types of plastic materials.** Plastics are synthetic polymers made by polymerisation of monomers (from petroleum) which can be moulded into shape under heat and pressure.

| Type | Behaviour | Examples and uses in construction |
|---|---|---|
| Thermoplastics | Soften on heating and harden on cooling, repeatedly (recyclable) | PVC (pipes, conduits, window frames, flooring), polyethylene (HDPE pipes, damp-proof membranes, water tanks), polypropylene (pipes, fibres), polystyrene (insulation, foam), acrylic or PMMA (sheets, skylights), nylon |
| Thermosetting plastics | Set permanently by heat (cross-linking), do not soften again | Bakelite (phenol formaldehyde: switches, laminates), urea formaldehyde (adhesives, boards), melamine (laminates, decorative sheets), epoxy resins (adhesives, coatings, repair), polyester and polyurethane |
| Elastomers | Elastic, rubber-like | Synthetic rubbers, neoprene (bearings, gaskets), silicone sealants |
| Fibre-reinforced plastics | Resin with glass or carbon fibre | GRP sheets, rebars, tanks, strengthening of structures |

General properties: light weight, corrosion resistant, water proof, good electrical and thermal insulation, easily moulded and coloured, low cost; but they have low strength and stiffness, weak against heat and fire, and degrade under UV.

**(b) Gypsum products.** Gypsum is hydrated calcium sulphate, CaSO$_4$·2H$_2$O. Heating (calcining) at 120 to 180 °C drives off three-fourths of the water, giving *plaster of Paris* (CaSO$_4$·½H$_2$O), which on adding water sets again to gypsum in 10 to 30 minutes with a slight expansion. Products: plaster of Paris (casting, moulds, false ceilings, ornamental work, finishing coat "punning"); gypsum plaster (wall plaster, smooth and fire-resistant, no shrinkage); plasterboard or drywall, gypsum blocks and partition panels (light, fire and sound insulation); and ground raw gypsum, added at 2 to 4 % in cement as a setting regulator. It is not for use in wet places (it softens in water).

**(c) Aerated autoclaved concrete (AAC) blocks.** A lightweight cellular precast block made of fine sand or fly ash, cement, lime, gypsum, water and a little (about 0.05 to 0.1 %) aluminium powder. Aluminium reacts with lime and water, releasing hydrogen bubbles, so the mix rises like a cake and forms a cellular structure; it is cut into blocks and cured in an autoclave at about 180 to 190 °C under 12 bar steam for 8 to 12 hours, producing tobermorite (calcium silicate hydrate). Properties: density 400 to 800 kg/m³, compressive strength 3 to 5 MPa, good thermal and sound insulation, high fire resistance (up to 4 hours), resistant to termites, accurate size (thin mortar joints, less plaster), easily cut and fixed. It reduces the dead load and earthquake force. Weaknesses: low strength and absorbs water. Used for infill and partition walls in framed buildings.

**(d) Properties and use of tar.** Tar is a dark brown or black viscous liquid obtained by the destructive distillation of coal (coal tar), wood or peat. *Properties:* dark colour with a characteristic smell; a mixture of hydrocarbons, phenols and free carbon; more fluid and temperature sensitive than bitumen (becomes soft in summer and brittle in winter); excellent adhesion to aggregates and wood; waterproof; antiseptic and toxic to fungi, insects and termites; hardens and ages with time as the oils evaporate; graded as RT-1 to RT-5 (road tars). *Uses:* surface dressing, priming, and patch repair of roads; as a binder of macadam (tar macadam); preservative coating (creosote) for timber, poles and piles; waterproofing and damp-proofing of foundations and roofs; coating of pipes; and in the manufacture of paint, pitch and roofing felts. Because it is carcinogenic and gives off fumes it is now used less than bitumen.
