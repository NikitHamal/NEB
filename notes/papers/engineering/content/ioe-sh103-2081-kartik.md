@s 1
**Daniell cell.** It is a galvanic cell that converts chemical energy into electrical energy using the reaction between zinc and copper(II) ions: $\text{Zn(s)}|\text{ZnSO}_4(aq)\,\|\,\text{CuSO}_4(aq)|\text{Cu(s)}$. Zn is the anode, $\text{Zn}\to\text{Zn}^{2+} + 2e^-$, and Cu is the cathode, $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^-\to\text{Cu}$. The salt bridge completes the circuit. Its standard emf is $0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10$ V.

**The given cell.** The anode is Cu and the cathode is Ag. Cell reaction: $\text{Cu} + 2\text{Ag}^+\to\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{Ag}$, $n = 2$.
$$E^\circ_{\text{cell}} = 0.80 - 0.34 = 0.46\ \text{V}$$
$$E_{\text{cell}} = E^\circ - \frac{0.0591}{2}\log\frac{[\text{Cu}^{2+}]}{[\text{Ag}^+]^2} = 0.46 - 0.02955\log\frac{0.2}{(0.1)^2} = 0.46 - 0.02955\log20$$
$$\boxed{E_{\text{cell}} = 0.46 - 0.02955\times1.301 = 0.4216\ \text{V}\approx0.42\ \text{V}}$$

**Or. Polarisation** is the deviation of an electrode potential from its equilibrium value when a net current passes through the cell. **Overpotential (overvoltage)** is the *extra* potential, beyond the thermodynamic (reversible) value, needed to make an electrode reaction proceed at a given rate:
$$\eta = E_{\text{applied}} - E_{\text{eq}}$$
Typical examples: hydrogen overpotential on Pb or Hg is large (about 1 V) and on Pt it is nearly zero. The causes are slow electron transfer (activation), slow mass transport (concentration) and ohmic drops.

**Butler–Volmer equation** relates the current density $j$ at an electrode to the overpotential:
$$\boxed{j = j_0\left[\exp\left(\frac{\alpha_aF\eta}{RT}\right) - \exp\left(-\frac{\alpha_cF\eta}{RT}\right)\right]}$$
where $j_0$ is the exchange current density, $\alpha_a$ and $\alpha_c$ are the anodic and cathodic transfer coefficients ($\alpha_a + \alpha_c = 1$ for a one-step reaction) and $F$ is Faraday's constant. For a large $|\eta|$ one term dominates, giving the **Tafel equation**, $\eta = a + b\log j$.

*Applications:* (i) the kinetics of electrode reactions and the determination of $j_0$ and $\alpha$ (Tafel plots); (ii) corrosion rate and protection; (iii) design of electrolysis cells, electroplating and electrolysers (energy loss); (iv) fuel cells and battery electrodes; (v) choice of electrocatalysts.

@s 2
**Mechanism of buffer action.** A buffer resists changes in pH on adding small amounts of acid or base. Take the basic buffer $\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$ (weak base) + $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ (salt, fully ionised, gives a large reserve of $\text{NH}_4^+$):
- On adding an **acid** ($\text{H}^+$), it is neutralised by the weak base: $\text{NH}_3 + \text{H}^+\to\text{NH}_4^+$, so $[\text{H}^+]$ hardly rises.
- On adding a **base** ($\text{OH}^-$), it is neutralised by the common ion reserve: $\text{NH}_4^+ + \text{OH}^-\to\text{NH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$, so $[\text{OH}^-]$ hardly rises.

The ratio $[\text{salt}]/[\text{base}]$ changes only slightly, so the pH, which depends on this ratio through $\text{pOH} = \text{p}K_b + \log\dfrac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{base}]}$, stays almost constant.

**Numerical.** NaOH added $= \dfrac{0.2}{40} = 0.005$ mol in 1 L. It reacts with the salt: $\text{NH}_4^+ + \text{OH}^-\to\text{NH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.
$$\begin{gathered}[\text{NH}_4^+] = 0.2 - 0.005 = 0.195\ \text{M} \\ [\text{NH}_3] = 0.1 + 0.005 = 0.105\ \text{M}\end{gathered}$$
$$\text{pOH} = 4.74 + \log\frac{0.195}{0.105} = 4.74 + 0.269 = 5.009$$
$$\boxed{\text{pH} = 14 - 5.009 = 8.99}$$
(Before adding NaOH, $\text{pOH} = 4.74 + \log2 = 5.04$ and pH $= 8.96$; the pH changed by only 0.03.)

@s 3
**Photocatalysis.** A photocatalyst is a substance (usually a semiconductor such as TiO₂, ZnO, CdS) that speeds up a reaction under light without being consumed. When light of energy $h\nu\ge E_g$ is absorbed, an electron–hole pair is produced, $\text{TiO}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}e^-_{CB} + h^+_{VB}$. The hole oxidises adsorbed water or OH⁻ to hydroxyl radicals, $h^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O}\to{}^\bullet\text{OH} + \text{H}^+$, and the electron reduces O₂ to superoxide, $e^- + \text{O}_2\to\text{O}_2^{\bullet-}$. These reactive species mineralise organic pollutants to CO₂ and H₂O.
*Applications:* splitting of water to H₂, degradation of dyes and pesticides in waste water, air purification, self-cleaning glass and anti-fogging, killing of bacteria, and reduction of CO₂ to fuels.

**Electrocatalysis.** An electrocatalyst is a catalyst that speeds up an **electrode reaction** (lowering its overpotential) by providing a surface on which the reactants adsorb and the electron-transfer step occurs more easily. The catalyst is part of the electrode material or is coated on it.
*Examples:* Pt (or Ni, MoS₂) for the hydrogen evolution reaction $2\text{H}^+ + 2e^-\to\text{H}_2$; Pt/C for the oxygen reduction at the cathode of a fuel cell; IrO₂/RuO₂ for the oxygen evolution during water electrolysis; Pt–Ru for methanol oxidation. The activity depends on the strength of adsorption of the intermediates (Sabatier principle: not too weak, not too strong).
*Applications:* fuel cells, water electrolysers, metal–air batteries, CO₂ reduction, and electrosynthesis.

**Difference.** In photocatalysis the energy comes from **light** and the reaction occurs at the surface of a suspended semiconductor; in electrocatalysis the energy comes from an applied (or generated) **potential** and the reaction occurs at an electrode.

@s 4
**IR-active molecules.** A molecule is IR active if a vibration of it produces a **change in its dipole moment**. Examples: HCl, CO, H₂O, NH₃, and the asymmetric stretch and bend of CO₂. Homonuclear diatomics (H₂, N₂, O₂, Cl₂) and the symmetric stretch of CO₂ do not change the dipole moment, so they are IR-inactive.

**NMR spectroscopy.**
*Principle:* nuclei with a non-zero spin (¹H, ¹³C, ¹⁹F, ³¹P) behave as tiny magnets. In an external magnetic field $B_0$ they take up $2I+1$ orientations of different energy (for ¹H, $\alpha$ with the field, lower energy, and $\beta$ against it). Radio-frequency radiation of frequency
$$\nu = \frac{\gamma B_0}{2\pi}$$
flips the spin from $\alpha$ to $\beta$ (**resonance**). The exact frequency depends on the electronic environment, which shields the nucleus, so different protons absorb at slightly different **chemical shifts** $\delta = \dfrac{\nu - \nu_{\text{TMS}}}{\nu_{\text{spectrometer}}}\times10^6$ ppm (TMS = 0). The number of signals, their positions, areas (the number of protons) and splitting (neighbours) are read from the spectrum.

*Applications:* (i) structure determination of organic compounds (number and type of H and C environments, connectivity); (ii) checking the purity of a sample and identifying isomers; (iii) following reaction kinetics and equilibria; (iv) **MRI** in medicine (¹H of water in tissue); (v) quantitative analysis of mixtures, and study of hydrogen bonding and conformations.

**Or. X-rays** are electromagnetic radiation of very short wavelength ($0.01$–$10$ nm, i.e. $0.1$–$100$ Å), produced when fast electrons strike a metal target (Cu K$\alpha$, $\lambda = 1.54$ Å, is the usual source) and are suddenly decelerated, plus the characteristic line emission of the target.

**X-ray diffraction (XRD).** The spacing between atomic planes in a crystal is of the same order as the wavelength of X-rays, so a crystal acts as a three-dimensional diffraction grating. Constructive interference of the beams reflected from successive planes occurs when **Bragg's law** is satisfied:
$$\boxed{n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta}$$

*XRD for organic compounds* (single-crystal or powder method; the intensity of diffracted X-rays is recorded against $2\theta$):
- The pattern is a **fingerprint** of the crystalline phase, so unknown organic solids (drugs, pigments, polymers) can be identified by comparing with databases (ICDD), including **polymorphs** of the same compound.
- From the angles, $d$-spacings are found and the **unit cell dimensions** follow; from single-crystal data the full 3-D molecular structure, bond lengths and angles and packing are obtained.
- The **degree of crystallinity** is found from the ratio of sharp peaks to the broad amorphous halo (polymers).
- **Crystallite size** by the Scherrer equation, $D = \dfrac{K\lambda}{\beta\cos\theta}$, and the purity of a phase.

@s 5
**Postulates of crystal field theory.**
1. The metal–ligand bond is entirely **ionic/electrostatic**: the ligands are point charges (anions) or point dipoles (neutral molecules), and there is no orbital overlap.
2. The five $d$ orbitals of the free metal ion are degenerate, but when ligands approach, the electrons of the metal $d$ orbitals are repelled and **the degeneracy is lifted** (crystal-field splitting).
3. In an **octahedral** complex the $d_{x^2-y^2}$ and $d_{z^2}$ orbitals ($e_g$), which point toward the ligands, are raised ($+0.6\Delta_o$), and the $d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{xz}$ orbitals ($t_{2g}$), which point between ligands, are lowered ($-0.4\Delta_o$). In a **tetrahedral** complex the order is reversed and $\Delta_t = \tfrac49\Delta_o$.
4. The magnitude of the splitting $\Delta$ depends on the nature of the ligand (spectrochemical series: $\text{I}^-<\text{Br}^-<\text{Cl}^-<\text{F}^-<\text{OH}^-<\text{H}_2\text{O}<\text{NH}_3<\text{en}<\text{NO}_2^-<\text{CN}^-<\text{CO}$), the charge and the position of the metal.
5. If $\Delta_o<P$ (pairing energy) the electrons occupy the $e_g$ orbitals before pairing (**high spin**, weak-field ligands); if $\Delta_o>P$ they pair in $t_{2g}$ (**low spin**, strong-field ligands).
6. Colour arises from $d$–$d$ transitions between the split levels, and the stability from the crystal field stabilisation energy.

**Applications of cerium.** Catalytic converters for cars (CeO₂ stores and releases oxygen); glass polishing powder for lenses, mirrors and screens; UV-absorbing glass and decolouriser; lighter flints (mischmetal, Fe–Ce alloys); Ce(IV) as a strong oxidising agent in cerimetric titrations; phosphors in lamps and displays; self-cleaning ovens; and a diesel fuel additive.

**VBT for $[\text{Ni(CN)}_4]^{2-}$.**
- The oxidation state of Ni is $+2$ (four CN⁻ gives $-4$, total charge $-2$), so Ni²⁺ is $[\text{Ar}]3d^8$.
- CN⁻ is a **strong-field** ligand, so it forces the two unpaired 3d electrons to pair up, leaving one 3d orbital ($3d_{x^2-y^2}$) empty:

| | 3d | 4s | 4p |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ni²⁺ (free) | ⇅ ⇅ ⇅ ↑ ↑ | ○ | ○ ○ ○ |
| In complex | ⇅ ⇅ ⇅ ⇅ ○ | ○ | ○ ○ ○ |
| Hybrids | | | one 3d + one 4s + two 4p = **dsp²**; four CN⁻ donate pairs |

- Four dsp² hybrid orbitals point to the corners of a square, so the shape is **square planar**.
- There are no unpaired electrons, so the complex is **diamagnetic** (low spin, inner-orbital complex).

@s 7
**(a) Five principles of green chemistry.**
1. **Waste prevention**: avoid creating waste instead of treating it later.
2. **Atom economy**: design the synthesis so the maximum fraction of the atoms of the reactants appears in the final product.
3. **Less hazardous chemical syntheses**: use and make substances that have little or no toxicity to human health and the environment.
4. **Safer solvents and auxiliaries**: avoid auxiliary substances (solvents, separation agents) or make them innocuous (water, supercritical CO₂, ionic liquids).
5. **Design for energy efficiency**: carry out reactions at ambient temperature and pressure whenever possible. (Also: use of renewable feedstocks, catalysts instead of stoichiometric reagents, design for degradation, reduction of derivatives, real-time pollution analysis, and inherently safer chemistry.)

**Biodegradable waste into energy.** Biodegradable waste (food waste, animal dung, crop residue, sewage sludge) is converted into energy mainly by **anaerobic digestion** (biogas production) in a digester in the absence of air, in four stages:
1. *Hydrolysis*: complex polymers (carbohydrates, fats, proteins) are broken into sugars, fatty acids and amino acids by enzymes.
2. *Acidogenesis*: these are fermented into volatile fatty acids, alcohols, H₂ and CO₂.
3. *Acetogenesis*: these are converted into acetic acid, H₂ and CO₂.
4. *Methanogenesis*: methanogenic bacteria give methane: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}\to\text{CH}_4 + \text{CO}_2$ and $\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\to\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

Biogas is about 55–65% CH₄ and 35–45% CO₂ and is used for cooking, lighting and electricity; the digested slurry is a rich fertiliser. Other routes: *incineration* (heat and power), *pyrolysis/gasification* (syngas), *fermentation* to ethanol, and *landfill gas* recovery.

**(b) Water quality standard.** It is a set of **permissible limits** for the physical, chemical and biological parameters of water (pH, turbidity, TDS, hardness, chloride, nitrate, heavy metals, coliform, BOD, COD, ...) fixed by an authority (WHO, or the Nepal Drinking Water Quality Standards 2062: turbidity 5 NTU, pH 6.5–8.5, TDS 1000 mg/L, chloride 250 mg/L, nitrate 50 mg/L, arsenic 0.05 mg/L, zero coliform) so that water is safe for its intended use.

**Turbidity** is the cloudiness of water caused by suspended particles (clay, silt, organic matter, microorganisms). It is measured by a nephelometer in NTU. High turbidity reduces light penetration and photosynthesis, shields microbes from disinfection, clogs filters and indicates possible contamination.

**BOD (biochemical oxygen demand)** is the amount of dissolved oxygen consumed by microorganisms to decompose the biodegradable organic matter in a water sample in **5 days at 20°C** (mg O₂/L): $\text{BOD}_5 = (\text{DO}_i - \text{DO}_5)\times\text{dilution factor}$. Clean water has BOD below about 2 mg/L; raw sewage has 200–400 mg/L. A high BOD depletes the oxygen and kills fish.

**COD (chemical oxygen demand)** is the amount of oxygen needed to oxidise **all** the organic (and oxidisable inorganic) matter chemically with a strong oxidant (boiling acidic $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$, 2 hours), mg O₂/L. COD is always $\ge$ BOD because it includes non-biodegradable matter; the ratio BOD/COD shows how biodegradable the waste is.

@s 8
**Classification of nanomaterials by dimension** (a nanomaterial has at least one dimension of 1–100 nm):
- **0-D**: all three dimensions at the nanoscale. Examples: nanoparticles (Au, Ag), quantum dots, fullerenes.
- **1-D**: two dimensions at the nanoscale, one outside it. Examples: nanowires, nanorods, nanotubes.
- **2-D**: one dimension at the nanoscale. Examples: graphene, nanosheets, thin films.
- **3-D**: no dimension is confined, but the structure is made of nanoscale building blocks. Examples: nanocrystalline bulk solids, nanocomposites, bundles of nanotubes.

**Chemical vapour deposition (CVD).** In this bottom-up method a **volatile precursor** is decomposed or reacted in the vapour phase on a heated substrate to deposit a solid nanomaterial.
*Procedure:* (1) the precursor (e.g. CH₄, C₂H₂, SiH₄, a metal halide or organometallic compound) is vaporised and carried by an inert gas (Ar, N₂) or H₂ into a reaction chamber (a quartz tube furnace); (2) the substrate (Si, SiO₂, Cu foil, often coated with catalyst nanoparticles of Fe, Ni or Co) is heated to 500–1100°C and the gas decomposes at its surface; (3) the atoms nucleate and grow as nanotubes, nanowires, graphene or thin films; (4) volatile by-products are pumped away.
*Examples:* carbon nanotubes from C₂H₂ on Fe/Ni catalyst at 700–900°C; graphene from CH₄/H₂ on copper foil at about 1000°C; $\text{SiH}_4\to\text{Si} + 2\text{H}_2$ for silicon nanowires and films.
*Advantages:* high purity, uniform, large-area films with controlled thickness, scalable. *Disadvantages:* high temperature, toxic or flammable precursors, costly apparatus.

@s 9
**(a) Addition polymers** are formed by the repeated addition of unsaturated monomer molecules (containing C=C double bonds) to one another **without loss of any small molecule**, so the repeating unit has the same composition as the monomer. They are made by chain-growth polymerisation. *Examples:* polythene (from ethene), PVC, polystyrene, polypropylene, PTFE, PMMA.

**Cationic polymerisation.** It is initiated by an electrophile (a carbocation) and is suitable for monomers with **electron-donating groups** that stabilise the carbocation, such as isobutylene, vinyl ethers and styrene. The initiator is a Lewis acid ($\text{BF}_3$, $\text{AlCl}_3$, $\text{SnCl}_4$) with a co-catalyst (water, HCl), and it is carried out at low temperature ($-80$ to $-100^\circ$C) in a non-polar solvent. Take isobutylene, $\text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)_2$, with $\text{BF}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$:
1. **Initiation**: the catalyst complex gives a proton, which adds to the monomer to give a stable tertiary carbocation (with the counter-ion $[\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-$).
$$\text{BF}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{H}^+[\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-,\qquad \text{H}^+ + \text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)_2\to(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+$$
2. **Propagation**: the carbocation attacks the next monomer, and the positive charge moves to the new chain end.
$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+ + n\,\text{CH}_2{=}\text{C(CH}_3)_2\to(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–[CH}_2\text{C(CH}_3)_2]_n\text{–CH}_2\text{–C}^+(\text{CH}_3)_2$$
3. **Termination**: by loss of a proton from the chain end to the counter-ion (giving a terminal C=C and regenerating the catalyst), by combination with the counter-ion, or by chain transfer to the monomer.
$$\sim\text{CH}_2\text{–C}^+(\text{CH}_3)_2\ [\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-\to\ \sim\text{CH}{=}\text{C(CH}_3)_2 + \text{H}^+[\text{BF}_3\text{OH}]^-$$
The product is polyisobutylene (butyl rubber is a copolymer with a little isoprene).

**(b) Fibre reinforced polymer (FRP).** A composite material in which strong, stiff **fibres** (glass, carbon, aramid) are embedded in a polymer **matrix** (epoxy, polyester, nylon). The fibres carry the load; the matrix binds them, transfers the stress and protects them. FRP is light, strong, stiff and corrosion-resistant.

**Polythiazyl, $(\text{SN})_x$.** *Preparation:* tetrasulphur tetranitride, $\text{S}_4\text{N}_4$ (made from $\text{S}_2\text{Cl}_2$ or sulphur with ammonia), is passed over silver wool at about 300°C to give disulphur dinitride, $\text{S}_2\text{N}_2$, which condenses on a cold surface and polymerises on standing at room temperature (solid-state ring-opening) to golden, fibrous $(\text{SN})_x$:
$$\text{S}_4\text{N}_4\xrightarrow[\ \text{Ag wool}\ ]{300^\circ\text{C}}2\,\text{S}_2\text{N}_2\xrightarrow{\ 25^\circ\text{C}\ }(\text{SN})_x$$
*Uses:* the first polymer found to behave as a metallic conductor and a superconductor below 0.3 K; used in research on conducting polymers, molecular electronics and as a model of one-dimensional metals.

**Polycarbonate.** *Preparation:* **bisphenol-A** reacts with **phosgene** ($\text{COCl}_2$) in the presence of NaOH (interfacial polycondensation), or with diphenyl carbonate (melt transesterification), with loss of HCl (or phenol):
$$n\,\text{HO–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–C(CH}_3)_2\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–OH} + n\,\text{COCl}_2\to[\text{–O–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–C(CH}_3)_2\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–O–CO–}]_n + 2n\,\text{HCl}$$
*Uses:* a transparent, tough, heat-resistant thermoplastic used for compact discs, bullet-resistant glass and riot shields, safety helmets, spectacle lenses, headlamp covers, greenhouse panels, electrical housings and water bottles.

**Or (b). Conducting polymers** are organic polymers with a conjugated backbone (alternating single and double bonds) that conduct electricity after being doped (oxidised, p-type, or reduced, n-type), e.g. polyacetylene, polyaniline, polypyrrole, polythiophene.

**Polyaniline (PANI).** *Synthesis:* oxidative polymerisation of aniline in 1 M HCl at 0–5°C with ammonium persulphate $(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8$ as the oxidant (or electrochemically). The aniline radical cations couple head-to-tail through the para position to give the green, conducting **emeraldine salt**; treating with NH₄OH gives the blue insulating emeraldine base.
$$n\,\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2\xrightarrow[\ \text{HCl, 0–5°C}\ ]{(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8}[\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–NH–}]_n$$
*Applications:* anticorrosion coatings, antistatic and EMI-shielding layers, gas and pH sensors, rechargeable batteries and supercapacitors, electrochromic displays, LED materials.

**Sulphur-based polymer: poly(p-phenylene sulphide), PPS.** *Synthesis:* polycondensation of p-dichlorobenzene with sodium sulphide in a polar solvent (N-methylpyrrolidone) at about 250°C:
$$n\,\text{Cl–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–Cl} + n\,\text{Na}_2\text{S}\to[\text{–C}_6\text{H}_4\text{–S–}]_n + 2n\,\text{NaCl}$$
PPS is a semicrystalline engineering plastic that becomes conducting on doping with AsF₅ or I₂. *Applications:* electrical and electronic parts, automobile components, chemical-resistant pump and valve parts, filter fabrics for hot gases, and conducting materials. (Polythiazyl $(\text{SN})_x$ is another sulphur-based conducting polymer.)

**(a) Requisites of a good paint.**
1. It should have good **covering (hiding) power**, i.e. opacity, and spread easily and uniformly.
2. It should adhere strongly to the surface (**good adhesion**) and form a hard, tough, **flexible** film that does not crack or peel.
3. It should **dry quickly** to give a smooth, glossy and uniform film, free from blisters.
4. The film must be **durable** and resistant to weather (sun, rain, heat), water, chemicals, abrasion and corrosion.
5. The colour must be stable (not fade), the pigment must stay in suspension, and the paint should be non-toxic and cheap.

**Two impacts on the environment.**
- *Volatile organic compounds (VOCs)* from solvents and thinners evaporate during painting and drying. They take part in forming ground-level ozone and photochemical smog, and cause breathing problems.
- *Toxic pigments and additives* (lead, chromium, cadmium compounds, biocides) and waste paint and wash water contaminate soil and water bodies and bio-accumulate. (Also: the disposal of empty containers and overspray.)

**(b) Glycerol trinitrate (nitroglycerine).**
*Preparation:* by **nitration of glycerol** with a mixture of concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulphuric acid. Glycerol is added slowly to the mixed acid kept in a lead or steel nitrator, with cooling (ice/brine) and stirring, so that the temperature stays **below 10–15°C** because the reaction is highly exothermic and the product explodes if it overheats. The $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ supplies $\text{NO}_2^+$ and absorbs the water formed.
$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3 + 3\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow[\ <15^\circ\text{C}\ ]{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4}\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{ONO}_2)_3 + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The oily nitroglycerine floats above the acid layer, is separated, washed with water and sodium carbonate solution to remove the acid, and dried.

*Uses:* (i) the main ingredient of **dynamite** (absorbed in kieselguhr or wood pulp, Nobel 1867), and of blasting gelatine, used in mining and quarrying; (ii) in smokeless powders and propellants (cordite, ballistite); (iii) in medicine as a **vasodilator** (glyceryl trinitrate, GTN, 0.5 mg tablets) for angina pectoris.
