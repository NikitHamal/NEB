@s 1
**Standard electrode potential.** The potential developed at an electrode (metal in contact with a solution of its ions) when the ion concentration is **$1$ M** (activity 1), at $25^\circ$C and for gases $1$ atm pressure, measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE, assigned $0.00$ V). By convention it is quoted as the **reduction potential** $E^\circ$; it measures the tendency of the species to be reduced.

**Galvanic vs electrolytic cell.**

| Galvanic (voltaic) cell | Electrolytic cell |
|---|---|
| Converts chemical energy into electrical energy | Converts electrical energy into chemical energy |
| The cell reaction is spontaneous ($\Delta G<0$) | The reaction is non-spontaneous and driven by an external source ($\Delta G>0$) |
| Anode is negative, cathode is positive | Anode is positive, cathode is negative |
| Two half-cells in separate vessels joined by a salt bridge | Both electrodes dip in one electrolyte (usually one vessel) |
| Example: Daniell cell, dry cell | Example: electrolysis of molten NaCl, electroplating |

**emf of the cell** $\text{Zn}\,|\,\text{Zn}^{2+}(0.01\ \text{M})\,||\,\text{Fe}^{2+}(0.1\ \text{M})\,|\,\text{Fe}$ (the scan prints $\text{Fe}^{+}$, read as $\text{Fe}^{2+}$). Zn (lower $E^\circ$) is the anode:
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Zn} + \text{Fe}^{2+}\to\text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Fe} \\ n = 2\end{gathered}$$
$$E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{cathode} - E^\circ_{anode} = -0.44 - (-0.76) = 0.32\ \text{V}$$
$$E_{cell} = E^\circ_{cell} - \frac{0.0591}{2}\log\frac{[\text{Zn}^{2+}]}{[\text{Fe}^{2+}]} = 0.32 - 0.02955\log\frac{0.01}{0.1}$$
$$E_{cell} = 0.32 + 0.02955 = 0.3496$$
$$\boxed{E_{cell}\approx0.35\ \text{V}}$$

@s 2
**Buffer capacity.** The buffer capacity $\beta$ of a buffer solution is the number of moles of strong acid or strong base that must be added to one litre of the buffer to change its pH by one unit:
$$\beta = \frac{\Delta n}{\Delta\text{pH}}\quad(\text{mol L}^{-1}\ \text{per pH unit})$$
It is greatest when $[\text{salt}] = [\text{acid}]$ ($\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a$) and increases with the total concentration of the buffer components.

**Calculation.** Moles in the mixture (total volume $150 + 450 = 600$ ml $= 0.6$ L):
$$\begin{gathered}n_{\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}} = 0.5\times0.150 = 0.075\ \text{mol} \\ n_{\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}} = 0.1\times0.450 = 0.045\ \text{mol}\end{gathered}$$
$$\text{p}K_a = -\log(1.8\times10^{-5}) = 4.745$$

*Initial pH* (Henderson-Hasselbalch):
$$\text{pH}_1 = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{n_{\text{salt}}}{n_{\text{acid}}} = 4.745 + \log\frac{0.045}{0.075} = 4.745 - 0.222 = 4.523$$

*After adding $10$ ml of $0.2$ M HCl:* $n_{\text{HCl}} = 0.2\times0.010 = 0.002$ mol. The H$^+$ reacts with the acetate ion:
$$\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+\to\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$$
$$\begin{gathered}n_{\text{salt}} = 0.045 - 0.002 = 0.043\ \text{mol} \\ n_{\text{acid}} = 0.075 + 0.002 = 0.077\ \text{mol}\end{gathered}$$
$$\text{pH}_2 = 4.745 + \log\frac{0.043}{0.077} = 4.745 - 0.253 = 4.492$$
(The common volume cancels in the ratio.)
$$\boxed{\Delta\text{pH} = 4.492 - 4.523 = -0.031\ \ (\text{pH falls by about }0.03)}$$
The pH changes by only $0.03$ units, showing the buffer action. The buffer capacity for this addition is $\beta = \dfrac{0.002/0.6}{0.031}\approx0.107$ mol L$^{-1}$ per pH unit.

@s 3
**Homogeneous catalyst.** The catalyst is in the **same phase** as the reactants (all gases or all in solution). Examples: NO in the lead-chamber process ($2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2\xrightarrow{\text{NO(g)}}2\text{SO}_3$, all gases); H$^+$ in the hydrolysis of ester ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOCH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\xrightarrow{\text{H}^+(aq)}\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{CH}_3\text{OH}$); Fe$^{3+}$ in $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ decomposition.

**Heterogeneous catalyst.** The catalyst is in a **different phase** from the reactants, usually a solid with gaseous or liquid reactants (contact catalysis). Examples: Fe in the Haber process ($\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\xrightarrow{\text{Fe(s)}}2\text{NH}_3$), $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ in the contact process, Ni in the hydrogenation of oils, Pt in the oxidation of ammonia (Ostwald).

**Intermediate compound formation theory.** According to this theory, the catalyst first **combines with one of the reactants to form an unstable intermediate compound**; this intermediate has a lower energy path, so it reacts with the second reactant to give the products and **regenerates the catalyst**. Thus the reaction $A + B\to AB$ (slow, high $E_a$) is replaced by
$$\begin{gathered}A + C\to AC\ (\text{intermediate}) \\ AC + B\to AB + C\end{gathered}$$
each step having a lower activation energy than the uncatalysed reaction.

**Examples.**

1. *Lead chamber process:* $2\text{NO} + \text{O}_2\to2\text{NO}_2$ (intermediate), then $\text{NO}_2 + \text{SO}_2\to\text{SO}_3 + \text{NO}$ (NO regenerated).
2. *Contact process:* $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5 + \text{SO}_2\to\text{V}_2\text{O}_4 + \text{SO}_3$ and $\text{V}_2\text{O}_4 + \tfrac12\text{O}_2\to\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$.
3. *Ether formation:* $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\to\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{HSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$, then $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{HSO}_4 + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}\to(\text{C}_2\text{H}_5)_2\text{O} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.

*Limitation:* it cannot explain catalysis by solids where no stable intermediate is observed (adsorption theory covers these).

@s 4
**Water pollution.** The contamination of water bodies (rivers, lakes, groundwater, sea) by the addition of foreign substances (chemical, physical, biological) so that its quality is degraded and it becomes unfit for drinking, agriculture, industry or aquatic life.

**Major sources.**

1. **Domestic sewage and wastewater** (organic matter, detergents, pathogens).
2. **Industrial effluents** (heavy metals, acids, dyes, phenols, oils; tanneries, textiles, paper, mining, electroplating).
3. **Agricultural runoff** (fertilizers: nitrates and phosphates; pesticides; animal waste).
4. **Thermal discharge** from power plants and **oil spills**, marine transport.
5. **Landfill leachate, mine drainage, radioactive wastes, siltation due to erosion, and atmospheric deposition (acid rain).**

**Control measures.**

- Treat sewage and industrial wastes (primary: screening and sedimentation; secondary: activated sludge or trickling filter; tertiary: nutrient removal, disinfection) **before discharge**.
- Recycle and reuse wastewater; adopt cleaner production and effluent standards (zero-liquid discharge).
- Use fertilizers and pesticides in a controlled way, adopt organic farming and buffer strips along waterways.
- Use phosphate-free biodegradable detergents; prevent dumping of solid waste and oil in water bodies.
- Provide cooling ponds/towers for heated water, safe disposal of radioactive and hazardous wastes.
- Public awareness, strict legislation and monitoring (BOD, COD, DO and heavy metals), and protecting catchment areas.

@s 5
**a) Formation and depletion of ozone.**

*Formation* (stratosphere, 15–35 km). UV radiation of wavelength $<240$ nm splits oxygen molecules into atoms, which add to O$_2$ (with a third body $M$ carrying away the energy):
$$\begin{gathered}\text{O}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu\ (\lambda<240\ \text{nm})}2\,\text{O} \\ \text{O} + \text{O}_2 + M\to\text{O}_3 + M\end{gathered}$$
*Natural destruction* (balance with formation): $\text{O}_3\xrightarrow{h\nu\ (220\text{–}320\ \text{nm})}\text{O}_2 + \text{O}$ and $\text{O} + \text{O}_3\to2\,\text{O}_2$.

*Man-made depletion.* Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs, e.g. CFCl$_3$, CF$_2$Cl$_2$), halons and nitrogen oxides from aircraft are stable in the troposphere and reach the stratosphere, where UV releases chlorine atoms that destroy ozone in a chain reaction:
$$\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}\text{CF}_2\text{Cl}\cdot + \text{Cl}\cdot$$
$$\begin{gathered}\text{Cl}\cdot + \text{O}_3\to\text{ClO}\cdot + \text{O}_2 \\ \text{ClO}\cdot + \text{O}\to\text{Cl}\cdot + \text{O}_2\end{gathered}$$
$$\text{net: O}_3 + \text{O}\to2\,\text{O}_2$$
One Cl atom can destroy about $10^5$ ozone molecules. Polar stratospheric clouds make the effect largest over Antarctica (the "ozone hole").

*Consequences of depletion.* More UV-B reaches the earth: skin cancer, cataract and eye damage, weakened immune system in humans; damage to crops, phytoplankton and aquatic life (food-chain disruption); damage to paints and plastics; and some climate change effects.

*Consequences:* more UV-B on earth causes skin cancer, cataract, damage to crops and marine life.

@s 6
**Conducting polymer.** A polymer that can **conduct electricity** because of a conjugated backbone (alternating single and double bonds) with delocalised $\pi$ electrons; it is usually made conducting by **doping** (oxidation with I$_2$, AsF$_5$ or reduction with Na) to create mobile charge carriers. Examples: polyacetylene, polyaniline, polypyrrole, polythiophene. *Uses:* batteries, antistatic coatings, sensors, LEDs, solar cells, electromagnetic shielding.

**Polyurethane.** *Preparation:* **step-growth (addition) polymerization** of a diisocyanate (e.g. toluene-2,4-diisocyanate, TDI, or MDI) with a diol or polyol (e.g. ethylene glycol, polyether polyol) in the presence of a tertiary-amine catalyst:
$$n\,\text{OCN–R–NCO} + n\,\text{HO–R'–OH}\longrightarrow[-\text{CO–NH–R–NH–CO–O–R'–O}-]_n$$
The $-\text{NH–CO–O}-$ urethane link repeats. Water in the mixture releases CO$_2$ (via the isocyanate and amine), which blows foams.
*Uses:* flexible foams for mattresses, cushions and car seats; rigid foam for refrigerator and building insulation; **spandex (Lycra)** fibres; paints and varnishes, adhesives, synthetic leather, shoe soles, rollers and tyres.

**Teflon (polytetrafluoroethylene, PTFE).** *Preparation:* **free-radical addition polymerization** of tetrafluoroethylene, which is made by pyrolysis of chlorodifluoromethane at $600$–$800^\circ$C ($2\,\text{CHClF}_2\to\text{CF}_2{=}\text{CF}_2 + 2\,\text{HCl}$). The monomer is polymerised in water under $\approx7$–$10$ atm and $\approx70$–$80^\circ$C with ammonium persulphate (or $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$) as the initiator:
$$n\,\text{CF}_2{=}\text{CF}_2\xrightarrow[\ 7\text{–}10\ \text{atm,}\ 70\text{–}80^\circ\text{C}\ ]{(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_8}[-\text{CF}_2-\text{CF}_2-]_n$$
*Properties:* very high chemical resistance, very low coefficient of friction, stable from $-250$ to $+260^\circ$C, excellent electrical insulation.
*Uses:* **non-stick coating** on cookware, gaskets, seals and valve linings, bearings, insulation of wires and cables, chemical-plant pipes and linings, surgical grafts and tape for plumbing (PTFE tape).

@s 7
**Inorganic polymers.** Polymers whose backbone chain contains **no carbon atoms** (they are made of inorganic elements such as Si, P, S, N, B), or inorganic atoms with some organic side groups. They are usually heat resistant and chemically inert. Examples: silicones (-Si-O-), polyphosphazenes (-P=N-), polymeric sulphur nitride (-S=N-)$_n$, borazine polymers, polysilanes.

**Applications of silicones in engineering.** Silicones (polyorganosiloxanes, $[\text{R}_2\text{SiO}]_n$) are used:

- as **lubricants and greases** that work at $-50$ to $+250^\circ$C (high viscosity index);
- as **electrical insulation** (class H insulation of motors, transformers, cables);
- as **sealants, gaskets and adhesives** (building glazing, expansion joints) and for **water-repellent coatings** on masonry, textiles and glass;
- as **silicone rubber** (O-rings, hoses, aerospace and automotive parts) and **hydraulic fluids**;
- as **anti-foam agents**, mould-release agents, polishes and heat-resistant paints; in medical implants and tubing.

**Polymeric sulphur nitride, $(\text{SN})_x$.** It is an inorganic polymer with alternating S and N atoms, $[-\text{S}=\text{N}-]_x$, studied as the first polymeric **metallic conductor**.

*Preparation.* Tetrasulphur tetranitride is made from S$_2$Cl$_2$ (or sulphur) and ammonia, then converted in two steps:
$$6\,\text{S}_2\text{Cl}_2 + 16\,\text{NH}_3\to\text{S}_4\text{N}_4 + 8\,\text{S} + 12\,\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$$
$$\text{S}_4\text{N}_4\xrightarrow[\text{Ag wool, }300^\circ\text{C}]{\text{vacuum}}2\,\text{S}_2\text{N}_2\xrightarrow{\text{room temp., solid-state polymerization}}(\text{SN})_x$$
*Properties and uses.* It forms golden, lustrous fibrous crystals; it conducts electricity like a metal along the chain direction and becomes a **superconductor below about $0.3$ K**. It explodes on heating above about $240^\circ$C. It is used in research on organic-metal-like conductors, in experimental electronic devices and as the model for conducting polymers.

@s 8
**i) Transition metal compounds are paramagnetic.** Transition metals and most of their ions have **partly filled $d$ orbitals containing unpaired electrons** (for example Mn$^{2+}$ $3d^5$, five unpaired; Fe$^{3+}$ $3d^5$; Cu$^{2+}$ $3d^9$, one unpaired). Each unpaired electron has a spin magnetic moment that is not cancelled, so the species has a net permanent magnetic moment and is **attracted into a magnetic field (paramagnetic)**. The moment is approximately
$$\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}\ \text{BM}$$
where $n$ is the number of unpaired electrons (e.g. $n = 3$ gives $3.87$ BM for Cr$^{3+}$, Co$^{2+}$ high spin). The more unpaired electrons, the more paramagnetic the compound. Compounds with $d^0$ or $d^{10}$ configuration (Sc$^{3+}$, Zn$^{2+}$, Cu$^+$) or with all electrons paired (low-spin $d^6$ in $[\text{Fe(CN)}_6]^{4-}$) are **diamagnetic**.

**ii) Variable valence (oxidation state).** The $(n-1)d$ and $ns$ electrons have **very small energy differences**, so besides the two $ns$ electrons a varying number of $(n-1)d$ electrons can also take part in bonding. Successive ionisation energies rise gradually, and the energy released in bond formation compensates for the loss of the $d$ electrons. This gives several oxidation states differing by one unit (unlike the $s$-block, where they differ by two):

| Element | Oxidation states |
|---|---|
| Mn | $+2,+3,+4,+6,+7$ |
| Fe | $+2,+3$ (also $+6$) |
| Cr | $+2,+3,+6$ |
| Cu | $+1,+2$ |
| V | $+2,+3,+4,+5$ |

The highest oxidation state of the early transition elements equals the total number of $(n-1)d + ns$ electrons (e.g. Mn in KMnO$_4$ is $+7$). Stability of the higher states falls across the series and, for the heavier members, the higher states become more stable.

@s 9
**Transition elements.** Elements which have **partly (incompletely) filled $d$ subshells** in the neutral atom or in any of their common oxidation states.

**Zn, Cd and Hg are not typical transition elements.** Their atoms have the configuration $(n-1)d^{10}ns^2$, and the $d$ subshell is **completely filled** in the ground state and in all their common oxidation states ($\text{Zn}^{2+}$: $3d^{10}$). Consequently, unlike the typical members, they

- show no variable oxidation state (only $+2$; Hg also $+1$ as $\text{Hg}_2^{2+}$),
- form colourless, diamagnetic compounds (no $d$–$d$ transitions),
- have low melting points, are volatile and soft (Hg is a liquid) and have a weak tendency to form complexes and catalysts.

**Cupric (Cu$^{2+}$) salts are blue; cuprous (Cu$^+$) salts are colourless.** Cu$^{2+}$ has the configuration $3d^9$, with a **vacant place (hole) in the $d$ subshell** and one unpaired electron. In the hydrated ion $[\text{Cu(H}_2\text{O)}_4]^{2+}$, the $d$ orbitals split into $t_{2g}$ and $e_g$ levels and the electron can absorb a photon of red/orange light to make a **$d$–$d$ transition**; the transmitted light is the complementary blue. Cu$^+$ has the configuration $3d^{10}$, with **all $d$ orbitals full**, so no $d$–$d$ transition is possible: it absorbs no visible light and its salts (CuCl, Cu$_2$O when pure aside) are colourless (and diamagnetic).

@s 10
**$[\text{NiCl}_4]^{2-}$.** Ni (Z = 28): $3d^84s^2$; Ni$^{2+}$: $3d^8$ with two unpaired electrons. Cl$^-$ is a **weak field ligand**, so it cannot force the electrons to pair. The $4s$ and three $4p$ orbitals (one $4s$ + three $4p$) hybridise to four $sp^3$ orbitals, which accept four electron pairs from the four Cl$^-$ ions:

- $3d$: $\uparrow\downarrow,\ \uparrow\downarrow,\ \uparrow\downarrow,\ \uparrow,\ \uparrow$ (unchanged, two unpaired).
- $4s$, $4p_x$, $4p_y$, $4p_z$: four $sp^3$ hybrid orbitals filled by the ligand pairs.

**Geometry: tetrahedral. Magnetic behaviour: paramagnetic** with two unpaired electrons, $\mu = \sqrt{2(2+2)} = \sqrt8 = 2.83$ BM (outer-orbital, high-spin complex).

**$[\text{Ni(CN)}_4]^{2-}$.** CN$^-$ is a **strong field ligand**. It pairs the two unpaired $3d$ electrons of Ni$^{2+}$ in one $3d$ orbital, leaving one $3d$ orbital vacant. This vacant $3d$, the $4s$ and two $4p$ orbitals hybridise to four $dsp^2$ orbitals, which accept electron pairs from four CN$^-$:

- $3d$: all four occupied orbitals have paired electrons ($8$ electrons in four orbitals), one $3d$ orbital empty.

**Geometry: square planar. Magnetic behaviour: diamagnetic** ($\mu = 0$; no unpaired electrons), an inner-orbital low-spin complex.

@s 11
**a) Werner's theory.** Werner proposed that metals show two types of valence:

- **primary (ionisable) valence**, satisfied by negative ions, shown by dotted lines and satisfied by ions that can be freed in solution;
- **secondary (non-ionisable) valence**, equal to the coordination number, satisfied by ligands (neutral molecules or anions) directed to fixed positions in space and **not ionisable**.

In **$[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]\text{Cl}_3$** the six NH$_3$ molecules satisfy the secondary valence (coordination number 6). The three Cl$^-$ satisfy only the primary valence (+3), so they lie outside the coordination sphere and ionise: $[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]\text{Cl}_3\to[\text{Co(NH}_3)_6]^{3+} + 3\,\text{Cl}^-$. With AgNO$_3$ all three give white AgCl: $3\,\text{Ag}^+ + 3\,\text{Cl}^-\to3\,\text{AgCl}\downarrow$.

In **$[\text{Co(NH}_3)_3\text{Cl}_3]$** the three NH$_3$ and the three Cl$^-$ together make up the coordination number 6, so all three Cl$^-$ satisfy **both** the primary and the secondary valence. They are inside the coordination sphere, firmly bonded to Co, and no free Cl$^-$ ions are present in solution, so **no precipitate of AgCl** is formed with AgNO$_3$ (the complex is non-electrolytic).

@s 12
**Low explosives.** They **burn (deflagrate)** rapidly but not instantaneously (rate below about $1$–$2$ km/s) and produce a pushing action; no shock wave is formed. They are used as propellants, in fireworks and for blasting soft rocks. Examples: gunpowder (black powder), smokeless powder, safety fuse.

**High explosives.** They **detonate**, with decomposition faster than the speed of sound ($3$–$9$ km/s) and a powerful shattering (brisance) effect, and are comparatively insensitive (need a detonator). Examples: TNT, TNG (nitroglycerine), RDX, PETN, dynamite, picric acid.

**TNT (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene).**

*Preparation:* toluene is nitrated in three stages with a mixture of concentrated HNO$_3$ and concentrated H$_2$SO$_4$ (the temperature is raised at each stage as nitration gets harder):
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_3\xrightarrow[30\text{–}50^\circ\text{C}]{\text{HNO}_3/\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4}\text{mononitrotoluene}\xrightarrow[60\text{–}80^\circ\text{C}]{\text{HNO}_3/\text{oleum}}\text{dinitrotoluene}\xrightarrow[100\text{–}115^\circ\text{C}]{\text{HNO}_3/\text{oleum}}\text{2,4,6-trinitrotoluene}$$
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{CH}_3 + 3\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4}\text{C}_6\text{H}_2(\text{NO}_2)_3\text{CH}_3 + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
The product is washed and purified by recrystallisation from alcohol or washing with sodium sulphite.

*Uses:* military shells, bombs, grenades and mines; mining, quarrying and demolition (often as amatol with ammonium nitrate); as the standard for expressing explosive power (TNT equivalent).

**TNG (trinitroglycerine, nitroglycerine, glyceryl trinitrate).**

*Preparation:* **nitration of glycerol** by adding glycerol slowly to a cold mixture of concentrated nitric acid and concentrated sulphuric acid, keeping the temperature below $10$–$15^\circ$C (cooling and stirring avoid a runaway explosion); the oily layer is separated, washed with water and sodium carbonate:
$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3 + 3\,\text{HNO}_3\xrightarrow[<15^\circ\text{C}]{\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4}\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{ONO}_2)_3 + 3\,\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
*Uses:* for making **dynamite** (absorbed in kieselguhr, invented by Nobel), blasting gelatin, gelignite and smokeless powders (cordite, ballistite); in medicine as a **vasodilator** (angina pectoris).

**a) Lubricants.** A lubricant is a substance introduced between two moving or sliding surfaces to reduce the friction and wear between them.

**Functions of a lubricant:**

1. **Reduces friction** and the wear and tear of surfaces (and the power loss) by forming a thin film that separates the rubbing surfaces.
2. **Reduces heating and acts as a coolant**, carrying away the heat produced.
3. **Reduces corrosion** of the surfaces by protecting them from air and moisture.
4. **Acts as a seal**, preventing leakage of gases (e.g. piston rings) and keeps out dust and dirt.
5. Reduces noise and vibration (shock absorption) and increases the life and efficiency of the machine.

**b) Requisites (properties) of a good paint.**

1. Good **covering (hiding) power** and uniform spreading, with a suitable viscosity so that it is easy to apply by brush or spray.
2. Good **adhesion** to the surface, forming a hard, tough and flexible film that does not crack, peel or blister.
3. It should **dry in a reasonable time** (not too fast, not too slow) and give a **glossy or smooth attractive finish** with a stable colour and no fading.
4. **Resistance to weathering**, moisture, sunlight (UV), chemicals, abrasion and corrosion, so that it protects the surface for a long time.
5. It should be **non-toxic**, not settle or form lumps in storage (stable shelf life), and be **economical**.

These are obtained by a proper choice of the constituents: pigment (colour and opacity), vehicle or drying oil (film former), thinner (flow), drier (speeds drying), filler/extender, plasticizer (elasticity) and additives (anti-skinning, fungicide).

**Isomerism in 3-hexene.** $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}{=}\text{CHCH}_2\text{CH}_3$ shows **geometrical (cis-trans) isomerism**, because rotation about the C=C double bond is restricted by the $\pi$ bond and each double-bond carbon carries two different groups (H and ethyl), so two spatial arrangements exist: ***cis*-3-hexene** (the two ethyl groups on the same side, $Z$) and ***trans*-3-hexene** (opposite sides, $E$). (It also shows position and chain isomerism with other hexenes.)

**Unimolecular nucleophilic substitution (S$_\text{N}$1).** In an S$_\text{N}$1 reaction the rate depends on only **one species**, the substrate (first order): $\text{rate} = k[\text{R–X}]$. It takes place in **two steps** through a carbocation intermediate and is typical for tertiary haloalkanes.

**Example:** tert-butyl bromide with water or aqueous NaOH.

*Step 1 (slow, rate-determining): ionisation*
$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–Br}\xrightarrow{\text{slow}}(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+ + \text{Br}^-$$
*Step 2 (fast): nucleophilic attack on the planar carbocation*
$$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+ + \text{OH}^-\xrightarrow{\text{fast}}(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C–OH}$$
*Characteristics:* a carbocation intermediate (rearrangements possible), the planar cation is attacked from both faces so a chiral substrate gives **racemisation** (retention + inversion); the order of reactivity is $3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ>\text{CH}_3$X; it is favoured by polar protic solvents (water, alcohol), good leaving groups and weak nucleophiles.

**Factors governing the S$_\text{N}$2 mechanism** (bimolecular, one step, $\text{rate} = k[\text{R–X}][\text{Nu}^-]$, backside attack with inversion):

1. **Structure of the substrate (steric hindrance):** $\text{CH}_3\text{X}>1^\circ>2^\circ\gg3^\circ$; bulky groups on or near the carbon block the backside attack. Tertiary halides do not undergo S$_\text{N}$2.
2. **Nature (strength) of the nucleophile:** a strong, negatively charged and less hindered nucleophile (I$^-$, CN$^-$, OH$^-$, RS$^-$) favours S$_\text{N}$2; its concentration affects the rate.
3. **Nature of the leaving group:** a better (weaker base) leaving group speeds up the reaction: I$^->$Br$^->$Cl$^->$F$^-$, tosylate being excellent.
4. **Solvent:** **polar aprotic solvents** (acetone, DMSO, DMF, acetonitrile) favour S$_\text{N}$2 because they solvate the cation but leave the nucleophile "naked"; protic solvents hydrogen-bond to the nucleophile and slow the reaction.
5. **Concentration and temperature** (higher concentration of Nu favours S$_\text{N}$2 over S$_\text{N}$1).

**E2 (bimolecular elimination).** A **one-step concerted** reaction in which a strong base removes a $\beta$-hydrogen while the leaving group departs from the $\alpha$-carbon and the C=C forms; the rate is second order, $\text{rate} = k[\text{R–X}][\text{B}^-]$.

**Example:** dehydrohalogenation of 2-bromopropane (or tert-butyl bromide) with alcoholic KOH:
$$\text{CH}_3\text{–CHBr–CH}_3 + \text{KOH}\xrightarrow[\Delta]{\text{alcohol}}\text{CH}_3\text{–CH}{=}\text{CH}_2 + \text{KBr} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
**Mechanism.** The base abstracts the $\beta$-hydrogen, the C–H electrons form the $\pi$ bond and the C–Br bond breaks with Br leaving as Br$^-$, all in a single transition state in which H and Br are **anti-periplanar**:
$$\text{B}^- + \text{H–C}_\beta\text{–C}_\alpha\text{–Br}\to\left[\text{B}^{\delta-}\cdots\text{H}\cdots\text{C}_\beta\cdots\text{C}_\alpha\cdots\text{Br}^{\delta-}\right]^\ddagger\to\text{B–H} + \text{C}_\beta{=}\text{C}_\alpha + \text{Br}^-$$
When there is a choice of $\beta$-hydrogen the **more substituted alkene (Saytzeff product) predominates**, e.g. 2-bromobutane gives but-2-ene (major) over but-1-ene (minor).

**Differences between E1 and E2.**

| E1 (unimolecular) | E2 (bimolecular) |
|---|---|
| Two steps via a carbocation intermediate | One concerted step, one transition state |
| $\text{rate} = k[\text{R–X}]$ (first order) | $\text{rate} = k[\text{R–X}][\text{base}]$ (second order) |
| Weak base or no base; polar protic solvent; heat | Strong base (RO$^-$, OH$^-$, NH$_2^-$); high concentration of base |
| Order $3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ$ (carbocation stability) | Order $3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ$ (alkene stability), $1^\circ$ also possible with a hindered base |
| Rearrangements of the carbocation are possible | No rearrangement |
| No stereochemical requirement, Saytzeff product | Anti-periplanar geometry required (stereospecific); Saytzeff product (Hofmann with a bulky base) |
| Competes with S$_\text{N}$1 | Competes with S$_\text{N}$2 |
| No kinetic isotope effect (C–H not broken in the slow step) | Kinetic isotope effect present ($k_H/k_D\approx3$–$8$) |
