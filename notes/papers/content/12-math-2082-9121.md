**(a) Binomial theorem.** For any positive integer $n$,
$$(a+b)^n={}^nC_0\,a^n+{}^nC_1\,a^{n-1}b+{}^nC_2\,a^{n-2}b^2+\dots+{}^nC_n\,b^n=\sum_{r=0}^{n}{}^nC_r\,a^{n-r}b^r.$$
The expansion has $n+1$ terms and the general term is $T_{r+1}={}^nC_r\,a^{n-r}b^r$.

**(b)** For the infinite geometric series $a+ar+ar^2+\dots$ with common ratio $r$, the sum exists only when $|r|<1$ and then
$$S_\infty=\frac{a}{1-r}.$$
For example, $1+\frac12+\frac14+\dots=\dfrac{1}{1-\frac12}=2$.

**(c)** The domain of a function $f:A\to B$ is the set of all permissible values of the independent variable $x$ for which $f(x)$ is defined, i.e. the set of all first elements of the ordered pairs of the function. For $f(x)=\sqrt{x-1}$ the domain is $x\ge1$.

**(d)** In the polar form $Z=r[\cos\theta+i\sin\theta]$ of a complex number $x+iy$, $r=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ is the *modulus* (the distance of the point $(x,y)$ from the origin on the Argand plane, always non-negative) and $\theta=\tan^{-1}\dfrac yx$ is the *argument* or amplitude (the angle that the line joining the origin and the point makes with the positive $x$-axis, measured anticlockwise).

**(e)** The inverse $A^{-1}$ of a matrix $A$ exists only when $A$ is a square matrix and it is non-singular, i.e. its determinant is not zero, $|A|\ne0$. Then $A^{-1}=\dfrac{\text{adj}\,A}{|A|}$.

@s 13
Let $P(n):\ 1^2+2^2+3^2+\dots+n^2=\dfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}$.

**Step 1 (basis).** For $n=1$: LHS $=1^2=1$ and RHS $=\dfrac{1\cdot2\cdot3}{6}=1$. So $P(1)$ is true.

**Step 2 (assumption).** Assume that $P(k)$ is true for some positive integer $k$:
$$1^2+2^2+\dots+k^2=\frac{k(k+1)(2k+1)}{6}.$$

**Step 3 (induction step).** We prove that $P(k+1)$ is true, i.e. that the sum up to $(k+1)^2$ is $\dfrac{(k+1)(k+2)(2k+3)}{6}$. Adding $(k+1)^2$ to both sides of the assumption:
$$1^2+\dots+k^2+(k+1)^2=\frac{k(k+1)(2k+1)}{6}+(k+1)^2$$
$$=\frac{(k+1)\left[k(2k+1)+6(k+1)\right]}{6}=\frac{(k+1)\left(2k^2+7k+6\right)}{6}.$$
Factorising $2k^2+7k+6=(k+2)(2k+3)$,
$$1^2+\dots+(k+1)^2=\frac{(k+1)(k+2)(2k+3)}{6}=\frac{(k+1)\left[(k+1)+1\right]\left[2(k+1)+1\right]}{6}.$$
This is the statement $P(k+1)$.

**Conclusion.** $P(1)$ is true, and $P(k)$ true implies $P(k+1)$ true. Hence, by the principle of mathematical induction, $P(n)$ is true for all positive integers $n$. $\blacksquare$

**(a)** By the sine rule, $\dfrac{a}{\sin A}=\dfrac{b}{\sin B}=\dfrac{c}{\sin C}=k$, so $b=k\sin B$ and $c=k\sin C$. Then
$$\frac{b-c}{b+c}=\frac{\sin B-\sin C}{\sin B+\sin C}=\frac{2\cos\frac{B+C}{2}\sin\frac{B-C}{2}}{2\sin\frac{B+C}{2}\cos\frac{B-C}{2}}=\cot\frac{B+C}{2}\,\tan\frac{B-C}{2}.$$

**(b)** Use $\sin2B=2\sin B\cos B$, $1+\cos2B=2\cos^2B$ and $1-\cos2B=2\sin^2B$.
$$\text{Numerator}=\sin2B+(1+\cos2B)=2\sin B\cos B+2\cos^2B=2\cos B(\sin B+\cos B)$$
$$\text{Denominator}=\sin2B+(1-\cos2B)=2\sin B\cos B+2\sin^2B=2\sin B(\cos B+\sin B)$$
$$\frac{\sin2B+\cos2B+1}{\sin2B-\cos2B+1}=\frac{2\cos B(\sin B+\cos B)}{2\sin B(\sin B+\cos B)}=\frac{\cos B}{\sin B}=\cot B.\ \blacksquare$$

@s 18
**(a)** A function $f(x)$ has the limit $L$ at $x=a$, written $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L$, if $f(x)$ gets as close as we like to the single real number $L$ as $x$ gets close to $a$ (from both the left and the right, $x\ne a$). That is, the left-hand limit equals the right-hand limit, both being $L$: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=L$.

**(b) Two types of discontinuity.**
1. *Removable discontinuity:* the limit exists but is not equal to the value of the function (or the function is not defined there). Example: $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ at $x=1$: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to1}f(x)=2$ but $f(1)$ is undefined. Defining $f(1)=2$ removes the break.
2. *Jump (non-removable) discontinuity:* the left and right limits both exist but are different. Example: $f(x)=1$ for $x\ge0$ and $f(x)=0$ for $x<0$ at $x=0$: LHL $=0$, RHL $=1$. (Infinite discontinuity, e.g. $f(x)=\dfrac1x$ at $x=0$, is a third type.)

**(c)** *Power rule:* $\dfrac{d}{dx}(x^n)=nx^{n-1}$ for any real $n$; for example $\dfrac{d}{dx}x^5=5x^4$.
*Product rule:* if $u$ and $v$ are differentiable functions of $x$, then $\dfrac{d}{dx}(uv)=u\dfrac{dv}{dx}+v\dfrac{du}{dx}$; for example $\dfrac{d}{dx}\left[x^2\sin x\right]=x^2\cos x+2x\sin x$.

**(a)** A function $f(x)$ is continuous at $x=a$ if three conditions hold: (1) $f(a)$ is defined; (2) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)$ exists, i.e. LHL $=$ RHL; and (3) $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)$. The second condition says that the limit must exist, so if the limit does not exist at $x=a$, the function cannot be continuous there.

*Example 1 (limit exists, continuous):* $f(x)=x^2$ at $x=2$: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}x^2=4=f(2)$, so $f$ is continuous at $x=2$.
*Example 2 (limit fails, not continuous):* $f(x)=1$ for $x\ge0$ and $f(x)=0$ for $x<0$ at $x=0$. Here LHL $=0$ and RHL $=1$, so the limit does not exist at $x=0$, even though $f(0)=1$ is defined; the graph jumps and $f$ is discontinuous at $x=0$.
Hence the existence of the limit value is a necessary condition for continuity.

**(b)** The chain rule is used for a function of a function: if $y=f(u)$ and $u=g(x)$ then $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{dy}{du}\cdot\dfrac{du}{dx}$.
*Example:* $f(x)=(3x+1)^5$. Put $u=3x+1$, so $f=u^5$:
$$f'(x)=5u^4\cdot\frac{du}{dx}=5(3x+1)^4\cdot3=15(3x+1)^4.$$
(Another example is $f(x)=\sin(x^2)$, $f'(x)=2x\cos(x^2)$.)

**(c)** Evaluate the integral:
$$\int_0^4(x^3+3x)\,dx=\left[\frac{x^4}{4}+\frac{3x^2}{2}\right]_0^4=\left(\frac{256}{4}+\frac{3\cdot16}{2}\right)-0=64+24=88.$$
**Yes**, it gives a rational number: 88, an integer, which can be written as $\dfrac{88}{1}$. *Reason:* the antiderivative of a polynomial with rational coefficients is again a polynomial with rational coefficients; evaluated at the rational limits 0 and 4 it gives sums, products and quotients of rational numbers, which are rational. (Geometrically, 88 is the area under the curve $y=x^3+3x$ from $x=0$ to $x=4$.)
