@s 12
**(a)** The *moment of inertia* of a body about an axis is the sum of the products of the mass of each particle and the square of its perpendicular distance from the axis, $I = \sum m r^2$. It is the rotational analogue of mass: it measures the opposition of the body to a change in its rotational motion about that axis. SI unit: kg m².

**(b)** Take the rod of mass $m$ and length $L$ with the axis AB through one end. The mass per unit length is $\dfrac mL$. Consider a small element of length $dx$ at a distance $x$ from AB, with mass $dM = \dfrac mL\,dx$. Its moment of inertia is $dI = x^2\,dM = \dfrac mL\,x^2\,dx$. Integrating over the whole rod,
$$I = \frac{m}{L}\int_0^L x^2\,dx = \frac{m}{L}\cdot\frac{L^3}{3} = \frac{mL^2}{3}$$
(Check with the parallel axis theorem: $I_{cm} + m\left(\dfrac L2\right)^2 = \dfrac{mL^2}{12} + \dfrac{mL^2}{4} = \dfrac{mL^2}{3}$.)

**(c)** $I = 0.32$ kg m², $\omega = 120$ rad/s, $P = 50$ W.

(i) Rotational kinetic energy
$$K = \tfrac12 I\omega^2 = \tfrac12(0.32)(120)^2 = 0.16\times14400 = 2304\ \text{J}$$

(ii) The wheel rotates steadily, so there is no angular acceleration and the driving torque of the motor exactly balances the frictional couple. The motor power is $P = \tau\omega$, so
$$\tau = \frac{P}{\omega} = \frac{50}{120} = 0.417\ \text{N m}$$
The frictional couple opposing the rotation is therefore about 0.42 N m. (The 50 W supplied is all dissipated as heat by friction.)

**Or (a)** $k = 18$ N/m, $m = 0.15$ kg. Pulling the mass down by 4 cm and releasing it from rest makes 4 cm the amplitude, $A = 0.04$ m.

(i) *Simple harmonic motion* is the to-and-fro motion of a particle about a mean position in which the restoring force (or acceleration) is directly proportional to the displacement from the mean position and always directed towards it: $a = -\omega^2 x$.

(ii) The angular frequency is
$$\omega^2 = \frac{k}{m} = \frac{18}{0.15} = 120\ \text{s}^{-2}$$
Maximum acceleration $a_{max} = \omega^2 A = 120\times0.04 = 4.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$.

@s 13
**(a)** Consider a soap film stretched on a rectangular wire frame, one side of which is a movable wire of length $l$. A film has two free surfaces, so the surface tension $T$ pulls the wire with a force
$$F = 2Tl$$
Let the wire be pulled slowly outward through a small distance $dx$ against this force. The work done is
$$dW = F\,dx = 2Tl\,dx$$
The area of the film (both surfaces together) increases by $\Delta A = 2l\,dx$, so $dW = T\,\Delta A$. This work is stored as the surface energy of the extra surface. Hence
$$T = \frac{W}{\Delta A} = \frac{\text{work done (surface energy)}}{\text{increase in surface area}}$$
Surface tension is numerically equal to the surface energy per unit area (J m⁻² = N m⁻¹).

**(b)(i)** Let each small drop have radius $r$ and the large drop radius $R$. The total volume is conserved:
$$N\cdot\tfrac43\pi r^3 = \tfrac43\pi R^3 \;\Rightarrow\; R = N^{1/3}r$$
The terminal velocity of a sphere falling through a fluid is $v = \dfrac{2r^2(\rho - \sigma)g}{9\eta}$, so for the same material $v\propto r^2$. Therefore
$$\frac{v_2}{v_1} = \left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^2 = N^{2/3}\;\Rightarrow\; v_2 = N^{2/3}\,v_1$$

**(ii)** For $N = 2$ and $v_1 = 0.4$ m/s,
$$v_2 = 2^{2/3}\times0.4 = 1.587\times0.4 = 0.635\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$

@s 14
**(a)** An *indicator diagram* is the graph of pressure against volume (P-V graph) drawn for a working substance during a thermodynamic process or cycle. The area under the curve gives the work done, and the area enclosed by a closed cycle gives the net work done per cycle.

**(b)** Process II is at constant pressure $4P_1$ while the volume increases from $V_1$ to $3V_1$:
$$W_{II} = P\,\Delta V = 4P_1(3V_1 - V_1) = 8P_1V_1$$
The work is positive (done by the gas) because the gas expands. This is the area of the rectangle under line II.

**(c)** Reading the figure:
- *Process I* ($V_1$ constant, pressure rises from $P_1$ to $4P_1$): **isochoric** (isovolumetric). No work is done.
- *Process II* (pressure $4P_1$ constant, volume $V_1\to3V_1$): **isobaric expansion**.
- *Process III* (smooth curve from $3V_1, 4P_1$ to $5V_1, P_1$): **adiabatic expansion**. The temperature falls (since $PV$ falls from $12P_1V_1$ to $5P_1V_1$), and the curve is steeper than the isotherm through the same starting point, which would reach only $\tfrac{12}{5}P_1$ at $5V_1$. Pressure here falls more than an isotherm allows, as in an expansion without heat input.
- *Process IV* (pressure $P_1$ constant, volume decreases from $5V_1$ back to $V_1$): **isobaric compression**.

@s 15
**(a)** *Newton's formula.* Newton assumed that the compressions and rarefactions in a sound wave take place slowly enough for the temperature to stay constant, so that the process is isothermal ($PV$ = constant, bulk modulus $K = P$) and
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{P}{\rho}}$$
For air at NTP ($P = 1.013\times10^5$ Pa, $\rho = 1.293$ kg m⁻³) this gives about 280 m/s, whereas the measured value is about 332 m/s.

*Laplace's correction.* Laplace argued that the compressions and rarefactions follow each other so quickly (hundreds of times per second), and air is such a poor conductor of heat, that there is no time for heat to flow between the compressed and rarefied layers. The process is therefore **adiabatic**: $PV^\gamma$ = constant, and the bulk modulus is $K = \gamma P$, where $\gamma = C_p/C_v$. The corrected formula is
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}}$$
For air $\gamma = 1.41$, giving $v = \sqrt{1.41\times\dfrac{1.013\times10^5}{1.293}} = 332$ m/s, in agreement with experiment.

**(b)** The velocity of sound is proportional to the square root of the absolute temperature, $v\propto\sqrt{T}$:
$$\frac{v_{25}}{v_0} = \sqrt{\frac{298}{273}}\;\Rightarrow\; v_{25} = 330\times1.0448 = 344.8\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$
Wavelength
$$\lambda = \frac{v_{25}}{f} = \frac{344.8}{256} = 1.35\ \text{m}$$

@s 16
**(a)** The potentiometer wire AB of uniform cross-section carries a steady current from the driver cell $E_0$, so the potential difference between any two points on it is proportional to the length between them: $V = k\,l$, where $k$ is the potential gradient.

*Key K open.* The cell of emf $E$ and internal resistance $r$ is connected through the galvanometer to the wire and no current flows from it. The balance point is at $P$, at length $l_1$ from A. Then
$$E = k\,l_1 \qquad (1)$$

*Key K closed.* A known resistance $R$ is now connected across the cell, so a current $I = \dfrac{E}{R + r}$ flows in the cell. The terminal potential difference falls to $V = IR$ and the balance point shifts to $P'$ at length $l_2 < l_1$:
$$V = k\,l_2 \qquad (2)$$
Dividing (1) by (2),
$$\frac{E}{V} = \frac{l_1}{l_2}$$
But $E = I(R + r)$ and $V = IR$, so $\dfrac EV = \dfrac{R + r}{R}$. Equating,
$$\frac{R + r}{R} = \frac{l_1}{l_2}\;\Rightarrow\; r = R\left(\frac{l_1 - l_2}{l_2}\right)$$
Measuring $l_1$, $l_2$ and knowing $R$ gives the internal resistance of the cell.

**(b)** A galvanometer is converted into an ammeter by connecting a **low resistance, called a shunt $S$, in parallel** with the galvanometer coil. Let the galvanometer have resistance $G$ and full-scale current $I_g$, and let the ammeter be required to read up to $I$. The current $I_g$ flows through the galvanometer and the remainder $I - I_g$ passes through the shunt. The two are in parallel, so the potential difference is the same:
$$I_g\,G = (I - I_g)\,S\;\Rightarrow\; S = \frac{I_g\,G}{I - I_g}$$
The effective resistance of the ammeter, $\dfrac{GS}{G + S}$, is smaller than $S$, so the ammeter hardly disturbs the circuit in which it is connected in series.

@s 17
**(a)** *Ampere's circuital law:* the line integral of the magnetic field around any closed path equals $\mu_0$ times the net current $I$ enclosed by the path,
$$\oint\vec B\cdot d\vec l = \mu_0 I$$
Let a long straight conductor carry a current $I$ and let us find $B$ at a point P at a perpendicular distance $r$ from it. By symmetry, the field lines are circles centred on the wire, and the magnitude of $B$ is the same at every point on a circle of radius $r$ (the Amperian loop). The field is along the tangent, so $\vec B$ is parallel to $d\vec l$ everywhere on the loop:
$$\oint B\,dl\cos0^{\circ} = B\oint dl = B(2\pi r)$$
Applying the law, $B(2\pi r) = \mu_0 I$, hence
$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r}$$
The field is inversely proportional to the distance from the wire and its direction is given by the right-hand grip rule.
