### Group A

@s 1
Two functions of management: (i) **planning**, which means deciding in advance what is to be done, how, when and by whom; (ii) **organising**, which means grouping activities, assigning duties and authority and creating relationships. (Staffing, directing/leading, coordinating and controlling are the other functions.)

@s 2
Two benefits of planning: (i) it gives **direction** by setting clear objectives, so that everyone works toward the same goal; (ii) it **reduces uncertainty and risk** because managers forecast future conditions and prepare for them. It also helps in saving cost and in controlling.

@s 3
**Operational decisions** are taken by **lower level management (supervisors and first-line managers)**. They are routine, short-term decisions on daily work, such as assigning jobs to workers, scheduling shifts and issuing materials.

@s 4
In a divisional structure the organisation is grouped by products, markets or regions, and each division is headed by a divisional manager who has the functions needed to run it.

@s 5
Supervision is important because the supervisor is the **link between management and workers**: he or she directs daily work, maintains discipline, solves workers' problems and ensures that targets of quantity and quality are met, so that plans are carried out smoothly.

@s 6
**Motivation** is the process of stimulating and inspiring people to act willingly and enthusiastically so that organisational goals are achieved while their own needs are also satisfied. It is the inner drive that directs behaviour toward a goal.

@s 7
Communication between managers of **different departments at the same level** is **horizontal (lateral) communication**. The human resources manager, production manager and marketing manager are of the same rank, so information flows sideways between them for coordination.

@s 8
**Talent management** is the process of attracting, selecting, developing, motivating and retaining skilled and high-performing employees, so that the organisation always has the right people with the right abilities to achieve its strategy.

@s 9
Two natures of family business management: (i) **ownership and control rest with family members**, and the top positions are usually held by relatives; (ii) **decisions are often informal and emotional**, mixing family relationships with business matters, and the business is generally passed on to the next generation.

@s 10
Supply chain management is important because it **links suppliers, producers, distributors and customers** so that the right goods reach the right place at the right time. It lowers inventory and transport costs, avoids delay and shortage, and raises customer satisfaction and competitive strength.

@s 11
Two usages of a business letter: (i) it is a **written record and proof** of an agreement, order, complaint or inquiry that can be referred to later; (ii) it is used to **communicate with outside parties** such as customers, suppliers, banks and government offices, and to build goodwill and promote sales.

### Group B

@s 12
Management in an organisation is practised at three levels. They differ in the scope of authority, the type of decisions, the time horizon and the skills they need.

| Basis | Top level | Middle level | Lower level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positions | Board of directors, CEO, managing director | Departmental, divisional and branch managers | Supervisors, foremen, section officers |
| Main role | Sets mission, objectives and overall policy | Converts policy into departmental plans and coordinates departments | Carries out plans through workers and supervises daily work |
| Decisions | **Strategic**, long term, high risk | **Tactical**, medium term | **Operational**, short term, routine |
| Time spent | Mostly planning and organising | Balanced between organising, leading and coordinating | Mostly directing and controlling |
| Skills needed | Conceptual and human skills | Human and some conceptual and technical skills | Technical and human skills |
| Authority | Highest authority and responsibility to owners | Moderate authority, accountable to top level | Least authority, accountable to middle level |
| Number of managers | Very few | More than top level | Largest number |
| Contact | With owners, government and the external environment | With top level and lower level | With workers and operating staff |

**Conclusion.** The three levels form a chain: top management decides *what* the organisation will achieve, middle management decides *how* each department will contribute, and lower management makes sure the work is actually *done*. Weakness at any one level breaks the chain.

@s 13
**Scientific management** was developed by **Frederick Winslow Taylor** (USA), known as the father of scientific management, and later extended by Henry Gantt, the Gilbreths and others.

**Four limitations of scientific management.**
1. **Mechanical view of workers.** It treats workers as machines driven only by pay, ignoring their social and psychological needs, so it can cause monotony and dissatisfaction.
2. **Opposition from labour unions.** Workers and unions felt that higher output meant exploitation and fear of unemployment, so many resisted it.
3. **Heavy cost and time to install.** Time and motion studies, new tools, training and planning departments require large investment that small firms cannot bear.
4. **Narrow, shop-floor focus.** It concentrates on operational efficiency and ignores the problems of finance, marketing and top management; functional foremanship also breaks the unity of command.
5. **Other limits.** Standard times may be unrealistic, it kills individual creativity and initiative, and it assumes that one best method exists for every job.

Despite these limitations, the ideas of work study, standardisation and incentive wages are still widely used in factories and service firms.

@s 14
Decision making is the process of choosing the best alternative from the available alternatives. It plays a significant role in business for the following reasons.
1. **Basis of all functions.** Planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling all need decisions; without them no function can proceed.
2. **Achievement of objectives.** Sound decisions choose the course of action that leads most directly to the objectives of the business.
3. **Use of resources.** It decides how money, materials, machines and people will be allocated, so it avoids waste and raises efficiency.
4. **Facing change and uncertainty.** Managers decide how to react to competitors, technology, price changes and customer tastes.
5. **Motivation and morale.** When employees take part in decisions, they feel valued and are more committed to carry them out.
6. **Coordination.** Decisions on policies and procedures bring uniform action across departments.
7. **Growth and survival.** Decisions on expansion, diversification and investment decide whether the firm grows or fails.

As the saying goes, "management is decision making". A wrong decision at a key moment can cost a business years of progress.

**Or (Q14). Four types of plan with examples.**
1. **Objectives.** The end results the firm wants, e.g. to earn a net profit of Rs 5 million this year.
2. **Policies.** General guidelines for decisions, e.g. "the company will promote from within wherever possible".
3. **Procedures.** Step-by-step methods of carrying out work, e.g. the steps for recruiting a clerk, from advertisement to appointment letter.
4. **Rules.** Specific, compulsory dos and don'ts, e.g. "smoking is prohibited in the factory".
5. **Budgets and programmes** are further types: a sales budget or an annual training programme.

@s 15
**Matrix organisation** combines the functional and the project (or product) structures. Employees report to **two bosses**: a functional manager (for their specialist discipline) and a project manager (for the work on the project). It is used by construction, consulting and software firms that run several projects at once.

```figure caption="Matrix organisation structure (functions across projects)."
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.2,2.5)); ax.axis('off'); ax.set_xlim(0,10); ax.set_ylim(0,5)
ax.text(5,4.7,'General Manager',ha='center',va='center',fontsize=8,bbox=dict(boxstyle='round',fc='#fbe3c8',ec=INK,lw=0.9))
cols=[3.9,6.0,8.1]; fn=['Engineering','Finance','Marketing']
for x,n in zip(cols,fn): ax.text(x,3.9,n,ha='center',va='center',fontsize=7,bbox=dict(boxstyle='round',fc='#d9e9fa',ec=INK,lw=0.9))
rows=[2.9,1.9,0.9]; pj=['Project A','Project B','Project C']
for y,n in zip(rows,pj): ax.text(1.4,y,n+'\nmanager',ha='center',va='center',fontsize=7,bbox=dict(boxstyle='round',fc='#d9e9fa',ec=INK,lw=0.9))
for x in cols:
    for y in rows: ax.plot(x,y,'o',ms=7,mfc='#ffffff',mec=ACCENT,mew=1.3)
    ax.plot([x,x],[3.6,0.6],color=GRID,lw=0.8,zorder=0)
for y in rows: ax.plot([2.3,8.6],[y,y],color=GRID,lw=0.8,zorder=0)
ax.text(5.5,0.2,'Each circle: a specialist reporting to a functional and a project manager',ha='center',fontsize=6.5)
```

**Benefits (2 marks).**
1. **Efficient use of specialists.** Experts are shared between projects, so skills are not duplicated and idle time falls.
2. **Better coordination and flexibility.** The project manager focuses on completing the project on time and the structure adapts quickly to changes in project needs.
3. **Development of staff.** Employees gain wider experience in both specialist and project work, which prepares them for senior roles.
