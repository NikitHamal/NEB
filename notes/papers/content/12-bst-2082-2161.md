### Group A

@s 1
Two functions of management: (i) **planning**, which means deciding in advance what is to be done, how, when and by whom; (ii) **organising**, which means grouping activities, assigning duties and authority and creating relationships. (Staffing, directing/leading, coordinating and controlling are the other functions.)

@s 2
Two benefits of planning: (i) it gives **direction** by setting clear objectives, so that everyone works toward the same goal; (ii) it **reduces uncertainty and risk** because managers forecast future conditions and prepare for them. It also helps in saving cost and in controlling.

@s 3
**Operational decisions** are taken by **lower level management (supervisors and first-line managers)**. They are routine, short-term decisions on daily work, such as assigning jobs to workers, scheduling shifts and issuing materials.

@s 12
Management in an organisation is practised at three levels. They differ in the scope of authority, the type of decisions, the time horizon and the skills they need.

| Basis | Top level | Middle level | Lower level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positions | Board of directors, CEO, managing director | Departmental, divisional and branch managers | Supervisors, foremen, section officers |
| Main role | Sets mission, objectives and overall policy | Converts policy into departmental plans and coordinates departments | Carries out plans through workers and supervises daily work |
| Decisions | **Strategic**, long term, high risk | **Tactical**, medium term | **Operational**, short term, routine |
| Time spent | Mostly planning and organising | Balanced between organising, leading and coordinating | Mostly directing and controlling |
| Skills needed | Conceptual and human skills | Human and some conceptual and technical skills | Technical and human skills |
| Authority | Highest authority and responsibility to owners | Moderate authority, accountable to top level | Least authority, accountable to middle level |
| Number of managers | Very few | More than top level | Largest number |
| Contact | With owners, government and the external environment | With top level and lower level | With workers and operating staff |

**Conclusion.** The three levels form a chain: top management decides *what* the organisation will achieve, middle management decides *how* each department will contribute, and lower management makes sure the work is actually *done*. Weakness at any one level breaks the chain.

@s 13
**Scientific management** was developed by **Frederick Winslow Taylor** (USA), known as the father of scientific management, and later extended by Henry Gantt, the Gilbreths and others.

**Four limitations of scientific management.**
1. **Mechanical view of workers.** It treats workers as machines driven only by pay, ignoring their social and psychological needs, so it can cause monotony and dissatisfaction.
2. **Opposition from labour unions.** Workers and unions felt that higher output meant exploitation and fear of unemployment, so many resisted it.
3. **Heavy cost and time to install.** Time and motion studies, new tools, training and planning departments require large investment that small firms cannot bear.
4. **Narrow, shop-floor focus.** It concentrates on operational efficiency and ignores the problems of finance, marketing and top management; functional foremanship also breaks the unity of command.
5. **Other limits.** Standard times may be unrealistic, it kills individual creativity and initiative, and it assumes that one best method exists for every job.

Despite these limitations, the ideas of work study, standardisation and incentive wages are still widely used in factories and service firms.

@s 14
Decision making is the process of choosing the best alternative from the available alternatives. It plays a significant role in business for the following reasons.
1. **Basis of all functions.** Planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling all need decisions; without them no function can proceed.
2. **Achievement of objectives.** Sound decisions choose the course of action that leads most directly to the objectives of the business.
3. **Use of resources.** It decides how money, materials, machines and people will be allocated, so it avoids waste and raises efficiency.
4. **Facing change and uncertainty.** Managers decide how to react to competitors, technology, price changes and customer tastes.
5. **Motivation and morale.** When employees take part in decisions, they feel valued and are more committed to carry them out.
6. **Coordination.** Decisions on policies and procedures bring uniform action across departments.
7. **Growth and survival.** Decisions on expansion, diversification and investment decide whether the firm grows or fails.

As the saying goes, "management is decision making". A wrong decision at a key moment can cost a business years of progress.

**Or (Q14). Four types of plan with examples.**
1. **Objectives.** The end results the firm wants, e.g. to earn a net profit of Rs 5 million this year.
2. **Policies.** General guidelines for decisions, e.g. "the company will promote from within wherever possible".
3. **Procedures.** Step-by-step methods of carrying out work, e.g. the steps for recruiting a clerk, from advertisement to appointment letter.
4. **Rules.** Specific, compulsory dos and don'ts, e.g. "smoking is prohibited in the factory".
5. **Budgets and programmes** are further types: a sales budget or an annual training programme.

Employees report to **two bosses**: a functional manager (for their specialist discipline) and a project manager (for the work on the project). It is used by construction, consulting and software firms that run several projects at once.

**Benefits (2 marks).**
1. **Efficient use of specialists.** Experts are shared between projects, so skills are not duplicated and idle time falls.
2. **Better coordination and flexibility.** The project manager focuses on completing the project on time and the structure adapts quickly to changes in project needs.
3. **Development of staff.** Employees gain wider experience in both specialist and project work, which prepares them for senior roles.

@s 16
**Suitable style (1 mark).** A **democratic (participative) leadership style**, and in the extreme case a **free-rein (laissez-faire)** style, suits an organisation with highly skilled, qualified and experienced employees.

**Why (2 marks).**
1. Skilled and experienced employees know their jobs well and need little supervision, so close control (autocratic style) would only irritate them.
2. They can offer valuable ideas, so consulting them improves the quality of decisions, raises their motivation and commitment, and brings creativity and innovation.
3. Such employees want autonomy and recognition; freedom to decide how to do their work satisfies their higher-order needs.

**Demerits (2 marks).**
1. **Slow decision making.** Consultation and discussion take time, which is a problem in an emergency.
2. **Lack of control and coordination.** With too much freedom, employees may drift in different directions, and under free-rein style the leader may lose control over results.
3. **Diffusion of responsibility.** When everyone takes part, it is difficult to fix accountability for a wrong decision.
4. **Dependence on the group.** The style works only if members are mature and responsible; a few careless members can reduce performance and cause conflicts of opinion.

@s 17
**Controlling (1 mark)** is the managerial function of measuring actual performance, comparing it with the planned standards, finding deviations and taking corrective action so that objectives are achieved.

**Importance of controlling (4 marks).**
1. **Accomplishing objectives.** It keeps the work on the track of plans and reveals problems early.
2. **Judging accuracy of standards.** Regular comparison shows whether the standards set in planning were realistic and need revision.
3. **Efficient use of resources.** It checks waste, pilferage and idle capacity, and makes sure that resources go to where they give best results.
4. **Employee motivation.** Employees who know their work will be measured on clear standards work harder; fair appraisal and reward follow.
5. **Order and discipline.** It creates an atmosphere of discipline and reduces dishonesty, delay and negligence.
6. **Coordination.** Control ensures that departments move toward the same goals in step.
7. **Facing change.** It helps the firm to notice changes in the environment and adjust plans.

Without control, even the best plans fail, because nobody knows whether actual work matches what was planned.

@s 18
**Talent management** is the planned effort to attract, develop, motivate and retain the people who have high skills and potential. It is important for business because of the following reasons.
1. **Competitive advantage.** Skilled and creative people produce better products and services than rivals, which is the strongest source of advantage.
2. **Higher productivity.** Right people in the right jobs with continuous training produce more output at lower cost and with fewer errors.
3. **Lower turnover.** Career paths, fair pay and recognition keep key employees, saving the cost of recruiting and training replacements.
4. **Leadership succession.** It builds a pool of future managers, so that the business does not suffer when a senior person leaves.
5. **Employer brand.** A company known for developing talent attracts the best applicants easily.
6. **Innovation and change.** Talented people bring new ideas and adapt quickly to technology and market changes.
7. **Employee engagement.** Challenging work and growth opportunities raise morale and commitment.

In a service economy like Nepal's banking, hotel and IT sectors, where the best people often migrate abroad, talent management is a condition of survival.

**Or (Q18). Conflict management (1 mark)** means identifying the causes of disagreement between individuals or groups and taking steps to reduce its harmful effects and use its useful side, so that cooperation and productivity are maintained.

**Four sources of conflict (4 marks).**
1. **Limited resources.** Departments compete for the same budget, staff or equipment.
2. **Differences in goals and perception.** Production wants long runs and low cost, while marketing wants variety and fast delivery.
3. **Poor communication.** Misunderstanding, rumours and lack of information create disputes.
4. **Interdependence and role ambiguity.** Unclear authority and overlapping duties cause blame and friction. Personality and cultural differences and unfair rewards are other sources.

@s 20
**Why decentralisation of authority is appropriate (4 marks).** Decentralisation means systematically pushing decision-making authority down to middle and lower levels instead of keeping it at the top. It is appropriate because:
1. **Relief to top management.** Top managers are freed from routine decisions and can concentrate on strategy, policy and long-term growth.
2. **Quick and better decisions.** Decisions are taken near the point of action by people who know the local facts, so delay and the need for approval at every step are removed.
3. **Development of managers.** Subordinates practise decision making and accept responsibility, creating a pool of future leaders and a second line of management.
4. **Motivation and morale.** Authority gives employees a sense of importance, trust and commitment, and increases job satisfaction.
5. **Suits large and diversified firms.** Branches, divisions and regional offices can respond to local customers and competitors, and performance of each unit can be measured separately.
6. **Better communication and coordination within units.** Fewer levels are crossed for approval, so distortion and delay of information fall.

**Argument against centralisation of authority (4 marks).** Centralisation keeps decision-making power at the top. It is open to criticism on these grounds:
1. **Overload and delay.** Top managers are burdened with minor matters, and every file waits for their signature, so response to problems is slow, especially in branches far from the head office.
2. **Poor decisions.** Top managers are distant from the field and decide without full knowledge of local conditions, customer needs and operational facts.
3. **Demotivation.** Subordinates become order-takers with no initiative; their talent is wasted, and bored, frustrated employees leave or become passive.
4. **No training of successors.** Without practice, middle managers cannot take over when top managers leave, causing a leadership gap.
5. **Rigidity in a changing environment.** A large organisation controlled from one point cannot adapt quickly to competition, technology or local markets.
6. **Risk of autocracy.** Concentrated power may lead to favouritism and abuse, and creates dependence on one or two persons.

Centralisation is useful only for small firms, for finance and policy, and in a crisis. For growing organisations in Nepal, such as banks, hotel chains and manufacturers with several branches, a balanced and well-defined decentralisation is the more suitable choice.

@s 21
The statement is correct: both financial (monetary) and non-financial (non-monetary) incentives satisfy an employee, the first through pay and the second through status and feelings. Motivation techniques are therefore grouped as follows.

**A. Monetary (financial) techniques**
1. **Pay and allowances.** A fair basic salary, dearness and house-rent allowances; e.g. a bank's regular monthly salary.
2. **Bonus and profit sharing.** Under the Labour Act of Nepal, 10 per cent of annual profit is distributed as bonus; a yearly festival (Dashain) bonus is common.
3. **Performance-based pay.** Commission to sales staff, piece-rate wages and incentive for exceeding targets.
4. **Stock options and employee share ownership.** The employee becomes a co-owner, e.g. bank staff share schemes.
5. **Retirement and welfare benefits.** Provident fund, gratuity, insurance, medical and education allowance and paid leave.

**B. Non-monetary (non-financial) techniques**
1. **Status and recognition.** Promotion, a better designation or an "Employee of the Month" award shows respect for the person.
2. **Job enrichment and enlargement.** Giving more challenging tasks, authority and responsibility, e.g. a clerk handling customer loans.
3. **Participation.** Involving employees in decisions through suggestion schemes and quality circles makes them feel valued.
4. **Job security.** Permanent appointment and protection from arbitrary dismissal give psychological security.
5. **Good working conditions and job rotation.** Clean and safe workplace, flexible hours, training and rotation to avoid monotony.
6. **Praise, feedback and a friendly climate.** Words of appreciation, a letter of thanks, and supportive leadership build emotional satisfaction.
7. **Career development.** Scholarships, training and study leave offer growth.

**Conclusion.** Money meets basic and security needs, while non-monetary incentives meet social, esteem and self-actualisation needs. A wise manager uses a **combination** of both, depending on the person, the job and the situation.

**Or (Q21). Factors affecting effective supervision (8 marks).** Supervision is overseeing and guiding workers' daily work. Its effectiveness depends on the following factors:
1. **Ability of the supervisor.** Technical knowledge of the job, human skills and communication skill decide whether workers respect and follow him or her.
2. **Leadership style.** An overly autocratic style produces fear and resistance; a participative, supportive style gets cooperation.
3. **Clarity of plans, targets and instructions.** Workers cannot be guided properly if the objectives, standards and procedures are unclear.
4. **Authority and responsibility of the supervisor.** Authority must match responsibility; a supervisor without power to reward, correct or report cannot enforce discipline.
5. **Span of supervision.** Too many subordinates dilute attention; a suitable number allows close contact and feedback.
6. **Nature and complexity of work.** Routine work can be supervised with fewer visits; complex or risky work needs close attention.
7. **Quality and attitude of workers.** Skilled, motivated workers need less supervision; careless or untrained workers need more.
8. **Communication system and feedback.** Open two-way channels let problems and suggestions reach management quickly.
9. **Working conditions, tools and incentives.** Good equipment, safe surroundings and fair incentives make the supervisor's task easier.
10. **Support from top management and union relations.** Backing from higher levels and good industrial relations reduce conflict.

When these factors are favourable, supervision raises productivity, discipline and morale; when they are weak, it becomes mere fault-finding.
