### Section I: Botany — Group B

@s 6
**Definition.** Parenchyma is a simple permanent tissue made of living, thin-walled, generally isodiametric cells that retain the power of division. It is the most abundant and the most primitive ground tissue of the plant body (cortex, pith, mesophyll, medullary rays and the packing cells of xylem and phloem).

**Structure.**
- The cells are living and have a prominent nucleus, a large central vacuole with cell sap, and a thin primary cell wall of cellulose and pectin.
- The shape is round, oval, polygonal or elongated, and the cells are loosely packed with small *intercellular spaces* between them.
- Types by contents and function: *chlorenchyma* (with chloroplasts), *aerenchyma* (large air spaces, in hydrophytes), *storage parenchyma* (starch, proteins, oils, in potato tuber) and *prosenchyma/collenchyma-like* transfer cells.

**Function.**
- Storage of food (starch, fat, protein) and water (succulents).
- Photosynthesis in chlorenchyma of leaves and young stems.
- Buoyancy and gas exchange in aerenchyma of aquatic plants.
- Healing of wounds and regeneration (callus formation), and secretion.

@s 7
**(i)** Pollen grains are produced in the pollen sacs (microsporangia) of the anther during microsporogenesis. The diploid pollen mother cells undergo meiosis to form tetrads of haploid microspores, which mature into pollen grains while the flower is still a bud, before the flower opens (anthesis).
**(ii)** **A** = *exine*, the thick outer wall of sporopollenin, with spines and germ pores. **C** = the *vegetative cell* (its cytoplasm and the large vegetative nucleus), which forms the pollen tube. (The labels in the figure are B = intine, the thin inner wall of cellulose and pectin, and D = the small lens-shaped *generative cell*, which divides to give the two male gametes.)
**(iii)** The two male gametes carried by the pollen tube are needed for *double fertilization*, which is unique to angiosperms:
- One male gamete (n) fuses with the egg cell (n) to form the diploid zygote (2n), which develops into the embryo (*syngamy*).
- The second male gamete (n) fuses with the two polar nuclei (or the diploid secondary nucleus) of the central cell to form the triploid (3n) primary endosperm nucleus (*triple fusion*), which develops into the nutritive endosperm.
If only one gamete were carried, there would be no endosperm to nourish the embryo, and the plant would waste energy forming food for an unfertilized ovule. Double fertilization therefore makes sure food is stored only after fertilization has taken place.

@s 8
**Incomplete dominance** is the inheritance in which neither allele is completely dominant over the other, so the heterozygote shows a phenotype *intermediate* between the two homozygous parents (Correns, 1900).

*Example: flower colour in* Mirabilis jalapa *(four o'clock plant) / snapdragon.*
- *Parents (P):* red-flowered plant (RR) × white-flowered plant (rr).
- *Gametes:* all R from the red parent and all r from the white parent.
- *F₁:* all Rr, with **pink** flowers (a blend, neither red nor white).
- *F₁ selfing* (Rr × Rr): gametes R and r from each parent; Punnett square gives RR : Rr : Rr : rr.

| | R | r |
|---|---|---|
| **R** | RR (red) | Rr (pink) |
| **r** | Rr (pink) | rr (white) |

*F₂ result:* 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white. The phenotypic ratio and the genotypic ratio are the same, **1 : 2 : 1**. The result shows that the factors do not blend permanently, because the red and white types reappear in F₂.

| Complete dominance | Incomplete dominance |
|---|---|
| One allele (dominant) masks the other completely. | Neither allele masks the other; the hybrid is intermediate. |
| F₁ looks like the dominant parent. | F₁ differs from both parents. |
| F₂ phenotypic ratio 3 : 1. | F₂ phenotypic ratio 1 : 2 : 1. |
| Phenotypic ratio ≠ genotypic ratio (3:1 vs 1:2:1). | Phenotypic ratio = genotypic ratio (1:2:1). |
| Example: tall × dwarf pea. | Example: red × white *Mirabilis*. |

**Or.** *Practical importance of seed dormancy:* dormancy is a period in which a viable seed does not germinate even under favourable conditions. Its importance is:
1. **Survival of the species:** it prevents germination in unfavourable seasons (extreme cold, drought), so seedlings do not die; the seed waits for the right season.
2. **Storage and transport:** dormant seeds can be stored for months and years in seed banks with little loss of viability; this ensures seed supply for the next season.
3. **Dispersal in space and time:** seeds stay dormant while they are carried to new places and germinate after they reach a suitable place.
4. **Prevention of pre-harvest germination (vivipary):** seeds do not sprout on the ear or in the fruit in humid weather (wheat, rice, tomato), which avoids crop loss.
5. **Horticulture/agriculture:** knowledge of dormancy breaking (scarification, stratification, soaking in GA₃, KNO₃ treatment) allows uniform and timely germination in nurseries and fields.

@s 9
**Micropropagation** is the *in vitro* clonal multiplication of a plant from a very small part of it (explant: shoot tip, axillary bud, leaf piece) on a sterile, nutrient medium under controlled conditions. It rests on **totipotency**: every living plant cell can regenerate a whole plant.
*Steps:*
1. **Selection and sterilisation of the explant** from a healthy mother plant (shoot tip or axillary bud, usually disease-free), surface-sterilised with ethanol and sodium hypochlorite.
2. **Initiation/inoculation** on a sterile Murashige–Skoog (MS) medium (sucrose, mineral salts, vitamins, agar) in culture tubes in a laminar-flow chamber.
3. **Multiplication:** a medium with a high cytokinin : auxin ratio produces many shoots from each explant (or callus is formed and organs are induced); the shoots are subcultured repeatedly, which can give thousands of plantlets.
4. **Rooting:** transfer to a medium with auxin (IBA/NAA) so that roots form.
5. **Hardening and transfer:** plantlets are slowly acclimatised (high humidity → lower humidity) in the greenhouse and then planted in the field.
*Use in the conservation of endangered plants:*
- Very few seeds or cuttings of a rare species can give a large number of identical plants without damaging the wild population (e.g. orchids, *Rauvolfia*, some Himalayan medicinal herbs).
- The plants are disease-free (meristem culture) and the clones can be reintroduced into the wild habitat to restore the population.
- Shoot tips/tissues can be kept in an *in vitro* gene bank, slowly growing at low temperature or stored by cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen (–196 °C), which preserves germplasm in little space.
- The seeds of species that are difficult to germinate (orchids) can be germinated in culture (asymbiotic germination).

@s 10
The light-independent reactions are the **Calvin cycle (C₃ cycle)**. They take place in the *stroma* of the chloroplast, use the ATP and NADPH formed in the thylakoid membranes (light-dependent reactions) and do not need light directly. The cycle has three phases.

**Steps of the Calvin cycle**
1. **Carboxylation (carbon fixation).** CO₂ combines with the five-carbon acceptor *ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP)*, catalysed by the enzyme **RuBisCO**, to form an unstable six-carbon intermediate that splits at once into **two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA)**, the first stable product (a three-carbon compound, hence "C₃").
2. **Reduction.** Each 3-PGA is phosphorylated by **ATP** to 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate and then reduced by **NADPH** to **glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P/PGAL)**. ATP → ADP + Pi and NADPH → NADP⁺ are formed in this step. G3P is the first carbohydrate of photosynthesis.
3. **Regeneration of RuBP.** Of every six G3P molecules formed, only one is the net gain (used to make glucose, sucrose, starch, amino acids and fatty acids); the other five (15 carbons) are rearranged through a series of reactions and, using **ATP**, are converted into three RuBP (15 carbon) so that the cycle can continue.

**Summary equation:** 6CO₂ + 18ATP + 12NADPH + 12H⁺ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 18ADP + 18Pi + 12NADP⁺ (six turns of the cycle).
For **one CO₂ fixed** the cycle needs 3 ATP and 2 NADPH; for each glucose, 6 turns are needed.

**Use of the products of the light-dependent reactions**
- **ATP** is the *energy source*: it phosphorylates 3-PGA (reduction phase) and phosphorylates ribulose-5-phosphate to RuBP (regeneration phase): 2 ATP + 1 ATP = 3 ATP for each CO₂ fixed.
- **NADPH** is the *reducing power*: its hydrogen and electrons reduce 1,3-bisphosphoglycerate to G3P (2 NADPH per CO₂).
- Without the continuous supply of ATP and NADPH from the light reactions the Calvin cycle stops, and the NADP⁺ and ADP formed return to the thylakoids to be recharged; so the two sets of reactions are linked.
- The G3P formed leaves the chloroplast to make sucrose (transport), or stays to make starch (storage), and is also used to make amino acids and lipids.

@s 11
**1. DNA as the genetic material** (1 mark). DNA qualifies as the genetic material because it:
- *stores information* in the sequence of its bases (the genetic code);
- *replicates* accurately (semi-conservative), so a complete copy is passed to every daughter cell and every offspring;
- *expresses* its information by producing RNA and then proteins (transcription and translation), controlling the traits of the organism;
- is *stable* chemically, but can change by *mutation*, which gives the variation needed for evolution.
Experimental proofs: Griffith's transformation of *Streptococcus pneumoniae* (1928), Avery–MacLeod–McCarty (1944) showed the transforming principle was DNA, and the Hershey–Chase experiment (1952) with ³²P-labelled DNA and ³⁵S-labelled protein of the T₂ bacteriophage proved that only DNA enters the bacterium and directs new virus formation.

**2. Double helical structure (Watson and Crick, 1953)** (4 marks, with diagram).
- DNA is a right-handed double helix of **two polynucleotide chains** wound round a common axis.
- Each nucleotide = a deoxyribose sugar + phosphate + a nitrogenous base (purines A, G; pyrimidines C, T). The sugar and phosphate make the *backbone* on the outside (3′–5′ phosphodiester bonds) and the bases lie inside.
- The two chains are **antiparallel** (one runs 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′).
- The bases are paired by hydrogen bonds: **A = T (two H-bonds)** and **G ≡ C (three H-bonds)**, a purine always with a pyrimidine; so the chains are *complementary* and Chargaff's rule (A = T, G = C) holds.
- Dimensions: diameter 2 nm; distance between adjacent base pairs 0.34 nm; one complete turn (pitch) 3.4 nm, with 10 base pairs per turn; there are a major groove and a minor groove.

**3. Similarities and differences between DNA and RNA** (2 marks).
Similarities: both are nucleic acids made of nucleotides joined by 3′–5′ phosphodiester bonds; both have a sugar, phosphate and four nitrogenous bases; both carry genetic information and take part in protein synthesis; both have A, G and C.

| DNA | RNA |
|---|---|
| Sugar is deoxyribose. | Sugar is ribose. |
| Bases A, T, G, C. | Bases A, U, G, C (uracil in place of thymine). |
| Double-stranded helix (usually). | Single-stranded (mostly). |
| Found mainly in the nucleus (also mitochondria, chloroplasts). | Formed in the nucleus, works in the cytoplasm. |
| One kind. | Three main kinds: mRNA, tRNA, rRNA. |
| Stable; stores and transmits genetic information. | Less stable; carries and translates the information. |
| Replicates itself. | Is made from DNA (transcription). |

**4. Significance** (1 mark). DNA stores the hereditary information and transfers it through replication (cell division and reproduction), while RNA transfers it into proteins: mRNA carries the code from DNA (transcription), tRNA brings the amino acids and rRNA forms the ribosomes where proteins are made (translation). Together they control all traits and life processes, and changes in them (mutation) give the variation on which natural selection acts.

**Or. Chromosomal mutations** are changes in the *number* or *structure* of chromosomes (1 mark), usually visible under the microscope (karyotype).

**A. Numerical changes (change in number)**
1. **Euploidy**: change by whole sets of chromosomes. *Monoploidy* (n), and *polyploidy*: triploid (3n), tetraploid (4n); common in plants (wheat is hexaploid 6n); caused by failure of spindle formation (non-disjunction of the whole set), e.g. colchicine treatment.
2. **Aneuploidy**: gain or loss of one or a few chromosomes, due to **non-disjunction** at meiosis. *Monosomy* (2n – 1): Turner syndrome (44 + XO = 45); *trisomy* (2n + 1): Down syndrome (trisomy 21, 47), Edwards (trisomy 18), Patau (trisomy 13), Klinefelter (44 + XXY = 47).

**B. Structural changes (change in structure)**
1. **Deletion** (deficiency): loss of a chromosome segment, e.g. *cri-du-chat* syndrome (loss of part of chromosome 5).
2. **Duplication**: a segment is repeated (present twice), which can increase gene dose (e.g. Bar eye in *Drosophila*).
3. **Inversion**: a segment is broken, turned through 180° and rejoined, so that the gene order is reversed (paracentric: without the centromere; pericentric: with the centromere).
4. **Translocation**: transfer of a segment to a *non-homologous* chromosome (reciprocal translocation); e.g. the Philadelphia chromosome (9;22) in chronic myeloid leukaemia.

**C. Significance**
- Harmful in most animals: abnormal syndromes, infertility, spontaneous abortion, cancer.
- Polyploidy in plants gives bigger, more vigorous, seedless varieties (seedless watermelon, triticale) and *new species* quickly.
- Structural changes provide raw material for evolution (new gene combinations and gene families), and chromosome studies help in diagnosis (karyotyping, genetic counselling).

@s 19
**Coelom** is the fluid-filled body cavity that lies between the body wall and the digestive tube, and is completely lined by a mesodermal epithelium (peritoneum). It holds and protects the visceral organs, allows them to grow and move independently of the body wall, and its fluid acts as a hydrostatic skeleton and a transport medium. Animals having it are *coelomates* (frog, man).

**Formation of coelom in the frog.**

**Mode of transmission (2):** typhoid spreads by the **faeco-oral route**.
- By food and water contaminated with the faeces or urine of a patient or a *carrier* (a person who is healthy but keeps the bacteria in the gall bladder).
- By houseflies that carry the bacteria from faeces to food; by uncooked vegetables washed in polluted water, street food and unclean hands of food handlers.
- Poor sanitation and overcrowding help it to spread.

**Symptoms (2):** the incubation period is 1–3 weeks.
- A fever that rises step by step for a week to 39–40 °C, with severe headache and weakness.
- Abdominal pain, loss of appetite, constipation (or diarrhoea in children).
- Rose-coloured spots on the chest and abdomen, enlarged spleen and a coated tongue.
- Diagnosed by the *Widal test* and blood or stool culture; treated with antibiotics (e.g. ciprofloxacin, azithromycin, ceftriaxone).

**Preventive measures (2):**
- Drink boiled or chlorinated/filtered water; eat freshly cooked, covered food; wash fruits and vegetables.

- Control flies and keep food covered; keep the surroundings clean.
- Typhoid (Vi polysaccharide or conjugate) vaccination; find and treat carriers, who should not handle food.

1. **Testes (a pair):** oval organs, about 4–5 cm long, in the scrotum. Each has about 250 lobules containing 1–3 coiled **seminiferous tubules**; the germ cells of the tubules undergo spermatogenesis, and the Sertoli cells nourish them. The **interstitial (Leydig) cells** between the tubules secrete the male hormone **testosterone**.
2. **Scrotum:** a pouch of skin and muscle (dartos and cremaster) that holds the testes outside the abdomen.
3. **Duct system:** seminiferous tubules → *rete testis* → *vasa efferentia* → **epididymis** (a coiled tube on the testis where sperms mature and are stored, about 6 m long) → **vas deferens** (passes through the inguinal canal into the abdomen and over the bladder) → **ejaculatory duct** (joined with the duct of the seminal vesicle) → **urethra**, which passes through the penis and carries both semen and urine.
4. **Accessory glands:** (a) a pair of **seminal vesicles** secrete an alkaline, fructose-rich fluid (about 60% of semen); (b) the **prostate gland** secretes an alkaline milky fluid that activates the sperm; (c) a pair of **bulbourethral (Cowper's) glands** secrete a clear mucus that lubricates the urethra and neutralises acidic urine. Their secretions together with sperms form the **semen**.
5. **Penis:** the copulatory organ with erectile tissue (two *corpora cavernosa* and one *corpus spongiosum*) and a head called the *glans penis* covered by the foreskin (prepuce).

**Why the testes lie outside the abdominal cavity (2 marks).** Spermatogenesis needs a temperature about **2–3 °C lower** than normal body temperature (about 34–35 °C instead of 37 °C). The scrotum outside the body gives this lower temperature, and it is regulated by the cremaster muscle (draws the testes closer to the body in cold) and the dartos muscle, and a countercurrent heat exchange in the pampiniform plexus of veins. If the testes stay in the abdomen (cryptorchidism), the sperm production fails and the man becomes sterile.

**Structure of the eye (5 marks).** The eyeball is a spherical organ of about 2.5 cm diameter in the orbit, with three coats and transparent media.
1. **Outer fibrous coat (sclera and cornea):** the *sclera* is the tough white opaque layer that protects the eye and gives it shape; in front it becomes the transparent *cornea*, which makes most of the refraction (about two-thirds) of the incoming light. The cornea is covered by the *conjunctiva*.
2. **Middle vascular coat (uvea):** the *choroid* is rich in blood vessels and in pigment, which nourishes the retina and absorbs the extra light, preventing internal reflection; in front it forms the **ciliary body** (ciliary muscles and suspensory ligaments that hold the lens and change its shape) and the **iris**, a coloured muscular diaphragm with a central opening, the **pupil**, whose size regulates the light entering (the radial muscles dilate it in dim light and the circular muscles constrict it in bright light).
3. **Inner nervous coat (retina):** it is the light-sensitive layer, containing about 125 million **rods** (dim-light, black-and-white vision; rhodopsin) and about 6 million **cones** (bright-light and colour vision; three kinds, red, green and blue), the bipolar cells and ganglion cells whose axons form the **optic nerve**. The **yellow spot (macula) with the fovea** at the centre has only cones and gives the sharpest vision.
4. **Lens and humours:** the transparent, elastic, biconvex **lens** lies behind the iris and focuses the image on the retina (accommodation by changing its curvature). The *aqueous humour* (between cornea and lens) maintains the pressure and nourishes the cornea and lens; the *vitreous humour* (the jelly behind the lens) keeps the eyeball spherical and holds the retina in position.
*Working:* light → cornea → aqueous humour → pupil → lens → vitreous humour → retina; a real, inverted, smaller image forms on the retina, the photoreceptors convert it to electrical impulses, which pass by the optic nerve to the occipital lobe of the brain, which interprets the image as upright.

**Blind spot (1 mark).** The point where the optic nerve leaves the eyeball has **no rods and cones** (the photoreceptors) because the nerve fibres and blood vessels pass through the retina there. A light image falling on it cannot produce any nerve impulse, so there is no vision at that point; hence it is called the blind spot. We do not notice it normally because the image of the same object falls on a different part of the other eye's retina and the brain fills the gap.
