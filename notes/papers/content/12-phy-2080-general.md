@s 12
**(a)** *Angular momentum* is the moment of linear momentum about an axis: $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$, so $L = mvr = I\omega$ for a rotating rigid body. Its SI unit is kg m² s⁻¹ (equivalently J s).

**(b)** $\omega_1 = 900$ rev/min $= 15$ rev/s $= 30\pi$ rad/s; $\omega_2 = 600$ rev/min $= 10$ rev/s $= 20\pi$ rad/s; $t = 10$ s.

(i) Angular acceleration
$$\alpha = \frac{\omega_2-\omega_1}{t} = \frac{20\pi - 30\pi}{10} = -\pi\ \text{rad s}^{-2} \approx -3.14\ \text{rad s}^{-2}$$
The negative sign shows a retardation of $\pi$ rad/s².

(ii) Number of revolutions: $\theta = \dfrac{\omega_1+\omega_2}{2}\,t = \dfrac{30\pi + 20\pi}{2}\times10 = 250\pi$ rad. Revolutions $= \dfrac{250\pi}{2\pi} = 125$.

(iii) From $\omega_2 = 20\pi$ rad/s to rest at the same retardation: $0 = \omega_2 + \alpha t'$, so $t' = \dfrac{20\pi}{\pi} = 20$ s. The motor needs 20 more seconds to stop.

**Or (a)** Let a simple pendulum of length $l$ and bob mass $m$ be displaced by a small angle $\theta$, so the bob is displaced $x$ along the arc from the mean position. Along the arc the restoring component of the weight is
$$F = -mg\sin\theta \approx -mg\theta \quad(\theta\ \text{small})$$
Since $\theta = \dfrac{x}{l}$, $F = -\dfrac{mg}{l}\,x$. By Newton's second law $ma = -\dfrac{mg}{l}x$, so
$$a = -\frac{g}{l}\,x \;\Rightarrow\; a\propto -x$$
The acceleration is directly proportional to the displacement and directed towards the mean position, which is the condition of simple harmonic motion, with $\omega^2 = g/l$.

**Or (b)** The restoring force of 60 N is taken at the extreme position (displacement = amplitude $A = 0.30$ m), so $k = \dfrac{F}{A} = \dfrac{60}{0.30} = 200$ N/m.
(i) $T = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac mk} = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{8}{200}} = 2\pi(0.2) = 1.26$ s.
(ii) $a_{max} = \dfrac{F}{m} = \dfrac{60}{8} = 7.5\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ (check: $\omega^2A = 25\times0.3 = 7.5$).
(iii) At $x = 0.12$ m: $K = \tfrac12k(A^2 - x^2) = \tfrac12(200)(0.09 - 0.0144) = 100\times0.0756 = 7.56$ J.

**(c)** When the ball attains the constant (terminal) velocity $v_t$ the acceleration is zero, so the net force is zero:
$$\text{weight} = \text{upthrust} + \text{viscous drag}$$
$$\tfrac43\pi r^3\rho g = \tfrac43\pi r^3\sigma g + 6\pi\eta r v_t$$
$$6\pi\eta r v_t = \tfrac43\pi r^3(\rho - \sigma)g$$
$$\boxed{v_t = \frac{2r^2(\rho - \sigma)g}{9\eta}}$$
The terminal velocity is proportional to the square of the radius and to the density difference, and inversely proportional to the viscosity $\eta$ of the glycerin.

@s 14
**(a)** *Entropy* is a thermodynamic state function that measures the degree of disorder (randomness) of a system. For a reversible change at temperature $T$, $dS = \dfrac{dQ}{T}$; its SI unit is J K⁻¹. In terms of entropy, since $dQ = T\,dS$, the first law $dQ = dU + dW$ becomes
$$T\,dS = dU + P\,dV$$

**(b)** No. A refrigerator takes heat $Q_2$ from the inside and rejects $Q_1 = Q_2 + W$ into the room through its back coils. With the door open the room is both the source and the sink, so the net effect is that the electrical work $W$ is converted to heat in the room: the room becomes slightly warmer, not cooler.

**(c)** Sudden compression is adiabatic, so $TV^{\gamma-1} = \text{constant}$.
$T_1 = 30 + 273 = 303$ K, $V_2 = \dfrac{8}{27}V_1$, $\gamma - 1 = \dfrac23$.
$$T_2 = T_1\left(\frac{V_1}{V_2}\right)^{\gamma-1} = 303\left(\frac{27}{8}\right)^{2/3} = 303\left(\frac32\right)^{2} = 303\times2.25 = 681.75\ \text{K}$$
Change in temperature $\Delta T = 681.75 - 303 = 378.75$ K $\approx 379$ K (a rise; the final temperature is about $409^{\circ}$C).

@s 15
**(a)** Fringe width $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda D}{d}$.
(i) In water the wavelength becomes $\lambda' = \lambda/\mu$ ($\mu = 1.33$) while $D$ and $d$ are unchanged, so $\beta' = \dfrac{\beta}{\mu}$: the fringe width decreases (to about 75 %), and the fringes become closer.
(ii) The fringe width depends on the *separation* $d$ of the slits, not on their individual widths. If the separation $d$ is increased, $\beta\propto\dfrac1d$ decreases. If only the width $a$ of each slit is increased (separation fixed), the fringe width stays the same, the fringes become brighter, but the diffraction envelope narrows so fewer bright fringes are visible.

**(b)** (i) *Diffraction* is the bending of light around the edges of an obstacle or a narrow aperture (of size comparable to the wavelength) into the geometrical shadow, producing a pattern of bright and dark regions.
(ii) Width of the central maximum $= \dfrac{2\lambda D}{a} = \dfrac{2\times600\times10^{-9}\times4}{0.01} = 4.8\times10^{-4}$ m $= 0.48$ mm.

At balance the galvanometer shows no deflection, $I_g = 0$, so B and D are at the same potential.
Kirchhoff's voltage law for loop ABDA (no current through G): $I_1P - I_2R = 0 \Rightarrow I_1P = I_2R$ …(1)
For loop BCDB: $I_1Q - I_2S = 0 \Rightarrow I_1Q = I_2S$ …(2)
(The current through $P$ also flows through $Q$, and that through $R$ also through $S$, by Kirchhoff's current law at B and D, as $I_g = 0$.)
Dividing (1) by (2):
$$\boxed{\frac{P}{Q} = \frac{R}{S}}$$
This is the balanced condition of the Wheatstone bridge.

**(b)** This is a potentiometer: the driving cell $E_0$ in series with $R$ sends a steady current through the wire AB, which gives a uniform potential gradient $k$ along it. At balance $E = k\,l$, where $l$ is the length AX.
(i) When $R$ is *increased*, the current in the wire falls, so the potential gradient $k$ falls. To balance the same emf $E$ a longer length is needed ($l = E/k$ increases), so the balance point shifts **towards B**.
(ii) $S$ lies in the secondary (galvanometer) circuit. At the balance point no current flows in that circuit, so there is no potential drop across $S$ and $E = k\,l$ is unchanged. Hence decreasing $S$ **does not shift** the balance point; it only changes the sensitivity (the galvanometer deflection away from balance), not the position of balance.

In one second the source emits $f$ waves. The first wave travels a distance $v$ in that second, while the source has moved away by $v_s$. So the $f$ waves occupy a length $v + v_s$ and the apparent wavelength is
