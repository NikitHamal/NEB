Cell notation: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(1 M) ‖ H⁺(1 M) | H₂(g, 1 atm) | Pt.

**(b)** Zinc has the lower reduction potential, so it is oxidised and H⁺ is reduced.

At cathode (reduction): 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → H₂(g)

**(c)** $E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{cathode} - E^\circ_{anode} = 0.00 - (-0.76) = +0.76$ V. The positive emf shows that the reaction is spontaneous.

**Or.** *Hess's law:* the total enthalpy change of a chemical reaction is the same whether it takes place in one step or in several steps, provided the initial and final conditions are the same; it depends only on the initial and final states and not on the path.

Required: 2C(s) + 3H₂(g) + ½O₂(g) → C₂H₅OH(l), ΔH_f = ?
Take equation (1) H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O, ΔH₁ = −286.2; (2) C + O₂ → CO₂, ΔH₂ = −393.2; (3) C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O, ΔH₃ = −1368.
Multiply (1) by 3 and (2) by 2 and reverse (3): 3(1) + 2(2) − (3) gives the required equation. Therefore
$$\Delta H_f = 3(-286.2) + 2(-393.2) - (-1368) = -858.6 - 786.4 + 1368 = -277.0\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}.$$
The heat of formation of ethanol is −277 kJ mol⁻¹.

@s 13
**(a)(i) Lewis base:** a species (molecule or ion) that can donate a pair of electrons to form a coordinate covalent bond, e.g. NH₃, H₂O, OH⁻, Cl⁻.
**(ii) Basic buffer:** a solution that resists change in pH on adding small amounts of acid or base and has pH greater than 7. It is a mixture of a weak base and its salt with a strong acid, e.g. NH₄OH + NH₄Cl. Added H⁺ is consumed by NH₄OH and added OH⁻ by NH₄⁺.

**(b)** Let each solution have volume V.
pH = 3 gives [H⁺] = 10⁻³ M; pH = 5 gives [H⁺] = 10⁻⁵ M.
Total moles of H⁺ = 10⁻³V + 10⁻⁵V = 1.01 × 10⁻³ V; total volume = 2V.
$$[H^+] = \frac{1.01\times10^{-3}V}{2V} = 5.05\times10^{-4}\ \text{M}$$
$$pH = -\log(5.05\times10^{-4}) = 4 - \log 5.05 = 4 - 0.703 = 3.30.$$
(Averaging the pH values to get 4 is wrong, because pH is logarithmic and the stronger acid dominates.)

@s 15
Corrosive sublimate is mercuric chloride, HgCl₂.
*Methods of preparation:*
1. **From mercuric sulphate (sublimation method):** HgSO₄ + 2NaCl → HgCl₂ + Na₂SO₄. An intimate mixture of HgSO₄ and NaCl (with a little MnO₂ to oxidise any mercurous salt) is heated in a glass retort; HgCl₂ sublimes and condenses on the cooler upper part.

HgCl₂ + 2KI → HgI₂↓ (red) + 2KCl
HgI₂ + 2KI → K₂[HgI₄] (soluble, potassium tetraiodomercurate(II)).
(b) **NH₃:** a white precipitate of mercuric amido-chloride (infusible white precipitate) is formed.
HgCl₂ + 2NH₃ → Hg(NH₂)Cl↓ + NH₄Cl

@s 16
**(i)** Nitrobenzene is reduced to aniline by Sn/conc. HCl (or Fe/HCl, or H₂/Ni), followed by NaOH to liberate the free base:
C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] —(Sn/HCl)→ C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O
(a) *Phenyl isocyanide* (carbylamine reaction): aniline is heated with chloroform and alcoholic KOH,
C₆H₅NH₂ + CHCl₃ + 3KOH → C₆H₅NC + 3KCl + 3H₂O (foul smell).
(b) *Azo-dye:* aniline is diazotised at 0–5 °C and coupled with phenol (or β-naphthol) in alkaline medium.

**(ii)** The –NO₂ group is strongly electron-withdrawing by both the –I and –R effects, so it deactivates the benzene ring towards electrophilic attack. Resonance puts a partial positive charge at the ortho and para positions (C₂, C₄, C₆), so these positions are the most electron-deficient and the most strongly deactivated. The meta positions keep a comparatively higher electron density, so the electrophile attacks there. In the arenium ion formed by ortho or para attack, one resonance structure has the positive charge on the carbon bearing –NO₂, which is very unstable. The intermediate from meta attack avoids this structure and is the least destabilised, so the meta-product is formed. For example, nitration of nitrobenzene gives 1,3-dinitrobenzene (about 93% meta).

**Or (a)** *Normality equation.* Normality is the number of gram equivalents of solute per litre of solution, $N = \dfrac{\text{gram equivalents}}{V(\text{L})}$, so the number of milli-equivalents is $N \times V(\text{mL})$. In a titration, substances react in the ratio of their equivalents; at the end point the equivalents of the acid are exactly equal to those of the base (or oxidant and reductant). Let the acid have normality N₁ and volume V₁, and the base N₂ and V₂:
$$\text{meq of acid} = N_1V_1,\qquad \text{meq of base} = N_2V_2,\qquad N_1V_1 = N_2V_2.$$
This is the normality equation. For an acid of molarity M₁ and basicity n₁, and a base of M₂ and acidity n₂, it becomes $n_1M_1V_1 = n_2M_2V_2$.
