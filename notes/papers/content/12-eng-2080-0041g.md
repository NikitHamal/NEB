It's not cool to be fat, but that hasn't stopped an obesity epidemic from occurring among young Americans. Childhood obesity increased from 5% in 1964 to about 13% in 1994. Today it is about 20%, and rising. Children, on average, spend five to six hours a day involved in **sedentary** activities like watching TV, using the computer, and playing video games, which is partly to blame for this rising rate. Maybe it would not matter if they were active enough at other times, but most of them are not.

To make matters worse, children are bombarded with television advertisements for fast food chains and other providers of high-fat, high-sugar meals and snacks. These highly **effective** advertising campaigns, combined with a physically inactive lifestyle, have produced a generation of children who are at high risk for medical conditions associated with **obesity**.

The main health threat is the early development of type 2 diabetes (adult onset), particularly in children with a family history of the disease. Doctors report an increase in the number of young adolescents developing type 2 diabetes, which can lead to heart disease, high blood pressure, kidney disease, stroke, limb amputations, and blindness. People who develop diabetes in adolescence face a decreased quality of life and a shorter life expectancy, particularly if the disease progresses without treatment. It is a **scary** prospect for our children, but in many cases, obesity and diabetes are preventable.

When kids spend most of their free time sitting in front of TVs and computers, they are not outside running, jumping, or participating in team sports that would keep their weight down. Parents should set limits on the time their children spend in passive activities. Parent involvement remains the most important key to healthy diets for our children. Programs to educate parents about nutrition are essential. Fast foods should be eaten only in moderation. Caregivers, who are often busy and in a hurry, must avoid the **temptation** to take their children to fast food restaurants or buy fast food for dinner at home. Changing eating habits and lifestyles is not easy, but the benefit to our children's health is a wonderful reward for parents willing to take on the task.

@s 2
**(a) Neighbours.** The young couple felt like sojourners (temporary visitors) because the neighbourhood in which they bought their house was full of migrant families whose language, food, dress, habits and loud, cheerful way of life were completely different from their own. Their street seemed a foreign land to them, though it lay in their own country. They felt isolated and uncomfortable among such unfamiliar neighbours, and they did not yet belong to the community. Only later did the neighbours' kindness change this feeling.

**(b) My Old Home.** Lu Xun returns to his old home after more than twenty years in the bitter cold of winter, under a gloomy sky, with a cold wind whistling through the boat's awning. The village he sees, with its desolate, tumbledown houses, is nothing like the beautiful place he remembers, and he feels a deep sadness and a pang of disappointment. He realises that the old home and the old life are lost for ever. He has come only to sell the house and move his family away, so his feeling is one of heavy melancholy.

**Or — A Very Old Man with Enormous Wings.** After a long rainy spell Pelayo and his wife Elisenda find a very old man lying face down in the mud of their courtyard, unable to get up because his huge wings are tangled and weighed down. He is dressed like a ragpicker, almost bald, with few hairs and almost no teeth, and he looks so pitiful and soaked that the couple are frightened. He speaks in an incomprehensible dialect. They think he is a sailor, but a wise neighbour woman says he is an angel who has come for their sick child, and they lock him up in the chicken coop.

**(c) Every Morning I Wake.** The poet describes God as great, merciful, almighty and all-knowing, the protector of every helpless person, and I agree with this impression to a large extent. The world is full of beauty, order and love that seem to point to a creator, and in times of fear and sorrow faith gives people comfort and hope, as it does to the people of Milk Wood. In my opinion, God's existence is a matter of faith: believers feel His presence in goodness, compassion and nature, while others rely on reason and science. I respect both views, but I think faith in a loving God leads people to good conduct.

**(d) Marriage as a Social Institution.** Yes, marriage is a social institution. As Stephen L. Nock explains, it is not only a private bond of two persons, but is governed by legal, moral and customary rules which come from outside the couple. It is linked with other institutions such as the family, education, the economy and politics, and it brings both families and the community into a network of trust (social capital). Society recognises it publicly through ceremonies and laws, and it shapes people's roles, responsibilities and ambitions. For this reason, marriage is much more than a personal relationship.

**Or — Human Rights and the Age of Inequality.** The Universal Declaration of Human Rights, adopted by the United Nations on 10 December 1948, is important because it sets a common standard of basic rights for every human being: freedom, equality, dignity, justice and respect, whatever one's race, sex, religion or wealth. It protects people from discrimination and the abuse of power, guides the laws of many nations and supports peace in the world. Samuel Moyn notes, however, that it did not tackle the gap between the rich and the poor, so its importance is real but incomplete.

**(e) A Matter of Husbands.** The Famous Actress is a clever, confident and skilful woman. Her profession has given her a talent for acting, quick thinking and persuasion. When the Earnest Woman, the wife of Alfred, comes to see her, she weaves a smooth, false story so well that the wife believes it, even though Alfred is hiding in her closet. She remains calm, charming and in control of the situation, and she manages the woman's feelings with great skill. Though she lives in a world of illusion and show, she is also practical and quick to save a difficult situation.

@s 3
**(a) 'The Awakening Age'.** *Reference to the context.* These lines come from the poem 'The Awakening Age', which speaks about the new age in which people begin to see themselves and the poor with new eyes, and which calls for a change in the way we think about poverty.

*Explanation.* The speaker says that when our perceptions change, society gains a great deal: instead of the pain of ignorance and injustice, truth flowers like a blossom. The poet insists that people should not be judged by their poverty. A poor people have much more to offer: their hard work, their wisdom and their creativity. If we look at the poor with respect and see their strength and not only their lack, we shall help them to grow and find dignity.

*Significance.* The lines express the poem's hope for a changed attitude, respect for labour and a more just and equal society.

**Or — 'The Treasure in the Forest' as a mystery story.** H. G. Wells' story has many features of a mystery story. Two men, Evans and Hooker, paddle to a lonely coral island with a map in Chinese writing that Hooker has stolen from Chang-hi, whom they killed; we learn about this only gradually. The map carries a puzzling pattern of little dashes pointing in all directions, whose meaning is not explained. In the forest they find a dead man lying with a purple, swollen neck, hands and ankles, but they do not stop to ask what killed him. The eerie forest, the hidden treasure and the unexplained clues build suspense. As they carry the gold, Evans collapses and dies, and Hooker is pricked by a thorn and suddenly understands that the dashes meant poisoned thorns, like those the Dyaks use in blowing-tubes. The solution to the mystery comes only at the very end, too late to save them.

**(b) 'Facing Death' as a modern tragedy.** Yes, I think 'Facing Death' is a modern tragedy. A classical tragedy deals with kings and heroes, but August Strindberg writes about an ordinary man, Mr. Durand, a poor man in exile weighed down by debts, creditors and starvation. The setting is a small ordinary house and the language is the ordinary talk of a family, which is typical of modern realistic drama. The conflict is internal and social: his daughters are angry and blame him for the family's poverty, and there is no help from outside. He makes a desperate plan, with the house insured and ready to be burned, and the play ends with his death. His downfall is caused by poverty, pride and hopelessness and not by fate or the gods, so it arouses pity and fear in the audience, as a tragedy should.

**Model story.**

**The Shout in the Street**

One evening Himesh was on the terrace of his house, watching the last rays of the Sun turn the hills orange. The street below was quiet, and he felt peaceful after a tiring day.

Suddenly he heard a loud shout from the street, followed by the screech of tyres. He listened for a moment and then ran down the stairs, two at a time, to see what had happened.

At the gate he stopped in horror. A man was lying in the middle of the street, motionless, and the floor around him was full of blood. A motorbike lay on its side a few yards away, and the rider who had hit him had already sped away.

Himesh's hands were shaking, but he did not panic. He shouted for help, and neighbours rushed out. Together they lifted the injured man into a taxi, and Himesh phoned the police on the way to the hospital. The doctors treated the man in time, and he survived.

**Moral:** Be ready to help in an emergency; one brave act can save a life.

**PRESS RELEASE**
For immediate release · 12 Baisakh 2080

**Dairy Development Corporation to revise the prices of milk and dairy products**

Kathmandu: The Dairy Development Corporation (DDC) announces that, with effect from 1 Jestha 2080, the prices of its milk and dairy products will increase by a small amount: the price of a litre of pasteurised milk will rise by Rs. 5, and the prices of curd, butter, ghee, cheese and other dairy products will be adjusted in proportion.

The Corporation has taken this decision with great reluctance and only after careful study. The main reasons for the increase are as follows.

1. **Rising cost of raw milk.** The price paid to the dairy farmers who supply us has risen because the cost of fodder, cattle feed, medicine and veterinary services has increased sharply. If farmers are not paid a fair price, they will abandon dairy farming and the supply of milk will fall.
2. **Higher cost of production and transport.** The prices of fuel, electricity, packaging materials and transport have all gone up, and this has increased the Corporation's operating expenses.
3. **Wages and maintenance.** The Corporation must meet its staff costs and the cost of keeping its plants and cold chain in good working order, so that consumers receive safe and fresh products.

The Corporation has tried to absorb part of these costs by improving efficiency, and the new prices are still lower than those of many private brands. We appeal to our valued consumers for their understanding and continued support.

Issued by:
General Manager
Dairy Development Corporation

| Q | (a) | (b) | (c) | (d) | (e) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ans | (iii) | (i) | (iv) | (iv) | (iii) |

**(a) should.** *Shoot, to* and *food* have the long vowel /uː/; *should* has the short vowel /ʊ/.
**(b) noun.** In "have a go", *go* follows the article *a* and means an attempt, so it is a noun.
**(c) to experience.** *Undergo* means to go through or suffer something, here extreme poverty.
**(d) medicaid/meeting.** In dictionary order *medicaid* (m-e-d-i-c-a) comes before *medicine* (m-e-d-i-c-i) and *meeting* (m-e-e) comes after it.
**(e) penalized.** To bring someone to book means to punish them or hold them to account for wrongdoing.
