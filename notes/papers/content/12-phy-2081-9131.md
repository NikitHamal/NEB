@s 12
**(a)** The *angular momentum* of a particle about a point is the moment of its linear momentum about that point, $\vec L = \vec r\times\vec p$, with magnitude $L = rp\sin\theta$. For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis, $L = I\omega$, where $I$ is the moment of inertia and $\omega$ the angular velocity. Its rate of change equals the external torque, $\tau = dL/dt$.
SI unit: $\text{kg m}^2\,\text{s}^{-1}$ (equivalently J s). Dimension: $[ML^2T^{-1}]$.

**(b)** No external torque acts on the child and turntable, so angular momentum is conserved:
$$I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2,\qquad I_2 = \tfrac23 I_1$$
$$\omega_2 = \frac{I_1}{I_2}\,\omega_1 = \frac32\times40 = 60\ \text{rpm}$$
The child (and turntable) spin faster, at 60 rpm, because the moment of inertia decreased.

**(c)** The analogue of mass is the moment of inertia $I$; the analogue of force is torque $\tau$ (so $\tau = I\alpha$ corresponds to $F = ma$).

**Or (a)** A *mass spring system* is a body of mass $m$ attached to the free end of a light helical spring whose other end is fixed to a rigid support. When the mass is pulled slightly and released it oscillates up and down (or to and fro on a smooth horizontal surface) about its equilibrium position.

**Or (b)** Let a light spring of force constant $k$ hang from a support. A mass $m$ attached to it stretches the spring by $e$ in equilibrium, so
$$mg = ke$$
Pull the mass down through a further small displacement $y$ and release it. The spring force is now $k(e + y)$ upwards, and the weight $mg$ acts downwards, so the net restoring force is
$$F = mg - k(e + y) = ke - ke - ky = -ky$$
By Newton's second law $m\,\dfrac{d^2y}{dt^2} = -ky$, giving
$$a = \frac{d^2y}{dt^2} = -\frac{k}{m}\,y = -\omega^2 y,\qquad \omega^2 = \frac{k}{m}$$
The acceleration is directly proportional to the displacement and always directed towards the mean position, which is the defining condition of simple harmonic motion. The time period is
$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$

**Or (c)** There is no change. $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$ contains only $m$ and $k$; the weight merely shifts the equilibrium position (the extension $e = mg/k$ is smaller on the moon) but the period does not depend on $g$.

@s 13
**(a)** The *coefficient of viscosity* $\eta$ of a liquid is the tangential viscous force per unit area needed to maintain a unit velocity gradient between two layers:
$$\eta = \frac{F}{A\,(dv/dx)}$$
Equivalently it is the viscous force on a layer of unit area when the velocity gradient is unity.
SI unit: $\text{N s m}^{-2} = \text{Pa s}$ (decapoise); CGS unit: poise (1 poise = 0.1 Pa s). Dimension: $[ML^{-1}T^{-1}]$.

**(b)** A rising bubble reaches terminal velocity when the viscous drag balances the net upthrust. With the density of air neglected, the terminal velocity of a sphere of radius $r$ in a liquid of density $\sigma$ is
$$v = \frac{2r^2\sigma g}{9\eta}$$
Given $r = 2\ \text{mm} = 0.2\ \text{cm}$, $\sigma = 0.82\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$, $\eta = 349$ poise, $g = 980\ \text{cm s}^{-2}$:
$$v = \frac{2\times(0.2)^2\times0.82\times980}{9\times349} = \frac{64.29}{3141} = 2.05\times10^{-2}\ \text{cm s}^{-1}$$
The bubble rises at about $2.05\times10^{-4}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. (In SI: $\eta = 34.9$ Pa s, $\sigma = 820$ kg m$^{-3}$, $r = 2\times10^{-3}$ m gives the same value.)

@s 14
**(a)** *First law of thermodynamics:* the heat supplied to a system is equal to the increase in its internal energy plus the external work done by the system,
$$\Delta Q = \Delta U + \Delta W$$
It is the law of conservation of energy applied to heat and work.

**(b)** The heat needed to raise the temperature of a gas depends on the conditions under which it is heated. If the gas is heated at constant volume no work is done and all the heat raises the internal energy, giving $C_v$. If it is heated at constant pressure it expands, so extra heat is needed for the work of expansion as well, giving a larger $C_p$. A gas can be made to expand or not by a large amount, so it can have any number of specific heats between $0$ and $\infty$ in principle; the two defined ones are $C_v$ and $C_p$. (For solids and liquids the expansion is tiny, so they have practically one specific heat.)

**(c)** Take one mole of an ideal gas.
At constant volume, $dW = 0$, so the heat for a rise $dT$ is $dQ = C_v\,dT = dU$.
At constant pressure, the same rise $dT$ requires
$$C_p\,dT = dU + P\,dV$$
The internal energy of an ideal gas depends only on temperature, so $dU = C_v\,dT$ in both processes. Hence
$$C_p\,dT = C_v\,dT + P\,dV$$
From the gas equation $PV = RT$ at constant pressure, $P\,dV = R\,dT$. Therefore
$$C_p\,dT = C_v\,dT + R\,dT\;\Rightarrow\; C_p - C_v = R$$
This is Mayer's relation; it also shows $C_p > C_v$ (the extra heat does the work $R\,dT$).

@s 15
**(a)** A *rectifier* is a device (a circuit using one or more p-n junction diodes) that converts an alternating current into a unidirectional (dc) current.

**(b)** *Construction.* The ac supply is connected across the primary of a step-down transformer whose secondary has a centre tap. The two ends of the secondary are joined to the p-sides (anodes) of two identical diodes $D_1$ and $D_2$. The n-sides (cathodes) are joined together, and the load resistance $R_L$ is connected between this common point and the centre tap of the secondary.

*Working.*
- *Positive half cycle:* the upper end of the secondary is positive with respect to the centre tap, so $D_1$ is forward biased and conducts, while $D_2$ is reverse biased. Current flows through $R_L$ from the common cathode point to the centre tap.
- *Negative half cycle:* the lower end becomes positive, so $D_2$ is forward biased and conducts, while $D_1$ is reverse biased. Current again flows through $R_L$ in the **same direction**.

Both half cycles therefore produce current in the load in one direction, so the output is a pulsating dc whose frequency is $2f$, twice that of the input. The average (dc) output is $V_{dc} = 2V_m/\pi \approx 0.637\,V_m$ (each diode must withstand a peak inverse voltage $2V_m$) and the ripple is smoothed by a capacitor filter across the load.

@s 17
**(a)** No. Two independent (separate) sources cannot produce sustained interference. Light is emitted by atoms in bursts lasting about $10^{-8}$ s, and the phase of the wave from one source changes randomly and independently of the other, so the phase difference at a point changes continuously. The sources are not coherent, and the interference pattern shifts too fast to be seen; the screen appears uniformly illuminated.

**(b)** Conditions for sustained interference:
1. The two sources must be coherent: they must emit waves of the same frequency with a constant phase difference (usually obtained from one source by splitting its wave).
2. The amplitudes should be equal or nearly equal so that the minima are dark and the contrast is good.
3. The light should be monochromatic and the sources narrow (point or slit), and the sources must be close to each other ($d \ll D$) with the screen far away so that the fringes are wide enough to be seen.

**(a)** *Kirchhoff's current law (junction law):* the algebraic sum of the currents meeting at a junction is zero, $\sum I = 0$; the current entering a junction equals the current leaving it. It expresses conservation of charge, since charge cannot accumulate at a junction in a steady circuit.
*Kirchhoff's voltage law (loop law):* around any closed loop of a circuit the algebraic sum of the emfs equals the algebraic sum of the products $IR$,
$$\sum E = \sum IR$$
It expresses conservation of energy: a charge taken once round a closed loop returns to the same potential. Sign convention: an emf is positive if the loop passes through the cell from the negative to the positive terminal; an $IR$ term is positive if the loop runs in the direction of the current.

**(b)** Let $L$ be the left wire and $J$ the junction on the right where the middle branch joins the right-hand wire. $$i_3 = 1 + 0.5 = 1.5\ \text{A}$$

**Or (a)** *Principle of potentiometer:* when a constant current flows through a wire of uniform cross-section, the potential difference between its two points is directly proportional to the length of the wire between them, $V\propto l$ (the potential gradient $k = V/l$ is constant).

**Or (b)** A cell of emf $E$ and internal resistance $r$ is connected to the potentiometer wire through a driver cell and rheostat. A resistance box $R$ is connected across the cell through a key $K$. Let $k$ be the potential gradient along the wire.
1. With $K$ open, the cell delivers no current and its terminal voltage equals its emf. The balance point is at length $l_1$:
$$E = k\,l_1$$
2. Close $K$ and take out a resistance $R$ from the box. The cell now sends a current $I = \dfrac{E}{R + r}$ through $R$ and its terminal voltage is $V = IR$. The balance point shifts to $l_2$ ($l_2 < l_1$):
$$V = k\,l_2$$
Dividing,
$$\frac{E}{V} = \frac{l_1}{l_2}$$
But $E = I(R + r)$ and $V = IR$, so $\dfrac{E}{V} = \dfrac{R + r}{R}$. Therefore
$$\frac{R + r}{R} = \frac{l_1}{l_2}\;\Rightarrow\; r = R\left(\frac{l_1 - l_2}{l_2}\right)$$
Measuring $l_1$, $l_2$ and knowing $R$ gives the internal resistance $r$ of the cell.

**(a)** *Biot-Savart law:* the magnetic field $d\vec B$ produced at a point P by a small current element $I\,d\vec l$ is
- directly proportional to the current $I$ and to the length $dl$ of the element,
- directly proportional to $\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $d\vec l$ and the line $\vec r$ joining the element to P,
- inversely proportional to the square of the distance $r$ of P from the element.
$$dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,dl\sin\theta}{r^2},\qquad d\vec B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\vec r}{r^3}$$
Here $\mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{T m A}^{-1}$. The direction of $d\vec B$ is perpendicular to the plane containing $d\vec l$ and $\vec r$ (right-hand screw rule). On the line of the element ($\theta = 0$) the field is zero, and it is maximum at $\theta = 90^{\circ}$.

**(b)** Let a straight conductor carry a current $I$ and let P be at a perpendicular distance $d$ from it. The wire subtends angles $\phi_1$ and $\phi_2$ at P, measured from the perpendicular PO to the two ends. Take an element $dl$ at a distance $l$ from O, at distance $r$ from P, with the line $r$ making angle $\phi$ with PO.

From the geometry $l = d\tan\phi$, so $dl = d\sec^2\phi\,d\phi$, and $r = d\sec\phi$. The angle between $d\vec l$ and $\vec r$ is $\theta = 90^{\circ} - \phi$, so $\sin\theta = \cos\phi$. The Biot-Savart law gives
$$dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,dl\sin\theta}{r^2} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\cdot\frac{d\sec^2\phi\,d\phi\cos\phi}{d^2\sec^2\phi} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\cos\phi\,d\phi$$
All elements give a field in the same direction (perpendicular to the plane of the paper), so integrating from $-\phi_1$ to $+\phi_2$:
$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\int_{-\phi_1}^{\phi_2}\cos\phi\,d\phi = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\left(\sin\phi_1 + \sin\phi_2\right)$$
For a very long conductor $\phi_1 = \phi_2 = 90^{\circ}$, therefore
$$B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi d}$$
The field lines are circles around the wire; the direction is given by the right-hand grip rule.

**(a)** A *stationary (standing) wave* is formed when two progressive waves of the same amplitude, wavelength and frequency travel along the same line in opposite directions and superpose. Usually the second wave is the reflection of the first from a boundary (a fixed end, a closed end of a pipe, or the end of a string). At some points the two waves always cancel (nodes, zero displacement) and at others they always add (antinodes, maximum amplitude $2a$). The energy does not move along the medium; the wave pattern stays fixed in space and every particle between two nodes vibrates in phase.

**(b)** Let the incident wave be $y_1 = a\sin(\omega t - kx)$ and the reflected wave $y_2 = a\sin(\omega t + kx)$, with $k = 2\pi/\lambda$. By superposition
$$y = y_1 + y_2 = 2a\cos kx\,\sin\omega t$$
The amplitude at position $x$ is $2a\cos kx$. At a *node* the amplitude is zero:
$$\cos kx = 0\;\Rightarrow\; kx = (2n + 1)\frac{\pi}{2}\;\Rightarrow\; x = (2n+1)\frac{\lambda}{4},\quad n = 0, 1, 2,\ldots$$
The consecutive nodes are therefore at $\dfrac{\lambda}{4},\ \dfrac{3\lambda}{4},\ \dfrac{5\lambda}{4},\ldots$ and the distance between two consecutive nodes is
$$\frac{3\lambda}{4} - \frac{\lambda}{4} = \frac{\lambda}{2}$$
(Similarly, antinodes where $|\cos kx| = 1$ are also $\lambda/2$ apart, and a node lies midway between two antinodes, $\lambda/4$ from each.)

**(b)** Let an electron of charge $-e$ and mass $m$ enter a region of uniform electric field $E$ (between two parallel plates) with velocity $u$ along the $x$ direction, perpendicular to the field (along $y$). - Along $y$ (starting from rest in this direction): $y = \tfrac12 a t^2$.

**(a)** The *r.m.s. (root mean square) value* of an alternating current is the value of the steady direct current which produces the same heating effect in a given resistor in the same time as the alternating current does. $I_{rms} = \dfrac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}} = 0.707\,I_0$, where $I_0$ is the peak value.

**(b)** The mains "220 V" ac is the r.m.s. value. Its peak value is $220\sqrt{2}\approx311$ V, so the instantaneous voltage swings between $+311$ V and $-311$ V, 100 times per second at 50 Hz, whereas 220 V dc stays at 220 V. Hence ac of the same quoted value can drive larger instantaneous currents through the body. Moreover, the alternating current repeatedly contracts the muscles (the hand clenches on the conductor so the victim cannot let go) and, with a frequency of 50 Hz, it interferes with the rhythm of the heart and can cause ventricular fibrillation. A dc shock usually gives a single jerk that throws the person away from the source. Thus 220 V ac is more dangerous than 220 V dc.

**(c)** Let the applied emf be $V = V_0\sin\omega t$ across a capacitor $C$. The charge on the capacitor is
$$q = CV = CV_0\sin\omega t$$

$$I = \frac{dq}{dt} = \omega CV_0\cos\omega t = \frac{V_0}{1/\omega C}\sin\!\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right) = I_0\sin\!\left(\omega t + \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$
