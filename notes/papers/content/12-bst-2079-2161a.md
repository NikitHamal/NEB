### Group A

@s 1
Management is generally divided into three levels: (i) **top level** (board of directors, chief executive), which sets objectives and policy; (ii) **middle level** (departmental or functional managers), which turns policy into departmental plans; and (iii) **lower or first-line level** (supervisors, foremen), which directs the day-to-day work of operatives.

@s 2
**Pre-control** (feedforward or steering control) is exercised before the work starts. It anticipates problems by checking inputs such as raw materials, money, manpower and policies, so that the standard is met in advance, for example inspecting raw material before production begins.

@s 3
**Supervision** is the function of overseeing and directing the day-to-day work of operative employees to see that work is done according to plan and instructions. It is performed by the first-line manager, called the supervisor, who links management with workers.

@s 4
Vertical communication flows along the chain of command, **downward** from superiors to subordinates (orders, policies) and **upward** from subordinates to superiors (reports, complaints, suggestions).

@s 5
**Talent management** is the process of attracting, selecting, developing, motivating and retaining highly skilled and high-potential people so that the organisation always has the right talent to achieve its goals. It covers talent planning, recruitment, development and retention.

@s 6
The components of quality management are (i) **quality planning**, setting quality objectives and the processes to meet them; (ii) **quality control**, checking products against standards and correcting defects; (iii) **quality assurance**, preventing defects through systems and procedures; and (iv) **quality improvement**, continuously raising quality (kaizen, TQM).

@s 7
A **complaint letter** is a letter written by a customer or buyer to a seller or service provider to express dissatisfaction with goods or services (late delivery, damage, wrong quantity, poor service) and to demand a remedy such as replacement, refund or compensation. It should be polite, factual and specific, with order number and date.

@s 9
Benefits of planning: it gives direction and clear objectives; reduces uncertainty and risk; avoids waste and duplication; helps coordination; provides a basis for control; and encourages innovation and better use of resources.

@s 10
The drivers of organizing are the forces that create the need for and guide an organisation: **objectives and goals**, **work specialisation (division of labour)**, **authority and responsibility**, **coordination**, and the **environment and technology** (also size and strategy).

@s 11
Sources of conflict: differences in goals and interests, scarcity of resources, unclear authority and responsibility, poor communication and misunderstanding, differences in values and personalities, interdependence of tasks, and unfair rewards or treatment.

### Group B

@s 12
A **science** is a systematised body of knowledge built on observation and experiment, with principles that explain cause and effect and can be tested. Management can be examined against these features.

| Feature of a science | Position of management |
|---|---|
| Systematic body of knowledge | Management has well-organised concepts, theories and principles (Fayol, Taylor, Weber) grouped under planning, organising, leading and controlling |
| Principles based on experiment | Principles were drawn from observation and studies such as Taylor's time and motion studies and the Hawthorne experiments |
| Universal validity | Principles such as unity of command and division of work apply in all organisations, but they are **less exact** than the laws of physics |
| Cause and effect relationship | Better motivation raises productivity, but human behaviour makes the result uncertain |
| Testability and prediction | Principles can be tested and used to forecast, though only with probability, not certainty |

**Conclusion.** Management has a scientific base, but because it deals with unpredictable human behaviour and its principles are flexible, it is called an **inexact or social science**, and it is also an art in application.

@s 13
**Scientific management** was developed by F. W. Taylor, who tried to raise efficiency by applying scientific methods to work. Its principles are:
1. **Science, not rule of thumb.** Replace guesswork with methods found by time and motion study of each task.
2. **Harmony, not discord.** Management and workers should cooperate; fair wages and fair profits remove conflict.
3. **Cooperation, not individualism.** Management and labour work together, and group effort is encouraged.
4. **Maximum output, not restricted output.** Output should be raised to the maximum, which brings higher prosperity to both owners and workers.
5. **Development of every worker.** Select workers scientifically and train them to the highest efficiency.

Taylor also stressed **functional foremanship**, the **differential piece-rate wage**, standardisation of tools and **mental revolution** in the attitude of managers and workers. The approach increased productivity but was criticised for treating workers as machines and ignoring social needs.

@s 14
**Conditions of decision making** are the situations, defined by how much is known about outcomes, in which a manager must choose.
1. **Certainty.** The manager knows the alternatives and the exact result of each, so the best result is chosen directly. Example: placing money in a fixed deposit with a known interest rate.
2. **Risk.** The alternatives are known, but each outcome has only a known **probability**, estimated from past data. Example: launching a product when past data show a 60 per cent chance of success. The manager uses the expected value.
3. **Uncertainty.** Neither the outcomes nor their probabilities are known, because the environment is new or changing. Example: entering an unknown foreign market. The manager relies on judgement, intuition and flexible plans.

Most managerial decisions are taken under risk or uncertainty, so the quality of information and the manager's experience decide how good the decision is.

**Or (Q14). Types of plan.** Plans are classified as follows.
- **By time:** *long-term* (strategic, 5 years or more), *medium-term* and *short-term* (operational, up to one year).
- **By use:** *single-use plans* such as programmes, projects and budgets, made for a one-time situation; and *standing plans* such as policies, procedures, rules and methods, used repeatedly.
- **By level:** *strategic* plans by top management, *tactical* plans by middle management and *operational* plans by lower management.
- **By scope:** *corporate*, *functional* and *departmental* plans.
- **Contingency plans** are standby plans used if the main plan fails.

A **policy** is a general guide to thought and action, a **procedure** is a sequence of steps, a **rule** is a specific compulsory action, and a **budget** is a plan stated in numbers.

@s 15
**Delegation** is passing authority to subordinates to do a job, while **decentralisation** is the systematic spreading of decision-making authority throughout the organisation.

| Basis | Delegation | Decentralisation |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Assigning work and authority to a subordinate | Dispersing authority to all levels as policy |
| Nature | An act of a superior toward a particular subordinate | An organisation-wide policy |
| Scope | Narrow, between two persons | Wide, covers the whole organisation |
| Withdrawal | Authority can be taken back easily | Reversing is difficult, because the structure is built on it |
| Necessity | Essential in every organisation | Optional, a matter of management choice |
| Accountability | The superior remains accountable for results | Accountability is shared at the decentralised units |
| Control | The superior keeps close control | Control is by standards, results and reports |

Delegation is the process and decentralisation is the result, so decentralisation is possible only where delegation is practised widely.

@s 16
Ways for effective communication:
1. **Clarify the idea first.** The sender should know exactly what is to be said and why.
2. **Use clear, simple language** and avoid jargon, ambiguity and technical terms the receiver may not know.
3. **Choose the right medium and time**, written for records and instructions, oral for urgent or personal matters.
4. **Be aware of the receiver.** Consider the receiver's background, needs and attitude.
5. **Provide feedback** and encourage two-way communication to confirm that the message was understood.
6. **Listen actively** with attention and without prejudice.
7. **Remove physical and psychological barriers**, such as noise, status differences and mistrust.
8. **Follow up** and make words and actions consistent so that the message is credible.

Effective communication reduces misunderstanding, improves coordination and builds trust between managers and employees.

@s 17
**Meaning.** Leading is the managerial function of influencing, guiding and motivating people so that they work willingly toward organisational goals. It includes leadership, motivation, communication and supervision.

**Importance:**
- It **initiates action**, because plans stay on paper until people are led to carry them out.
- It **motivates employees** with incentives, recognition and a good climate, which raises their morale and productivity.
- It **builds confidence** and cooperation through guidance and support.
- It **creates a link** between the organisation and its employees, and **coordinates** individual and group efforts.
- It **manages change** by helping people accept and adapt to new methods.
- It develops **teamwork** and so improves **organisational effectiveness** and growth.

Good leading turns the other functions into results and brings the best out of the employees.

**Or (Q17). Factors affecting supervision.** (i) **Quality of the supervisor**: knowledge, skill and leadership ability; (ii) **nature of the work**: routine work needs less supervision than complex work; (iii) **ability and attitude of subordinates**: trained and motivated staff need little supervision; (iv) **span of control**: a smaller span allows closer supervision; (v) **organisational culture and policies** and the support of top management; (vi) **communication system** and feedback; (vii) **technology** and the physical working conditions; (viii) **group size and cohesiveness**.

@s 18
**Meaning.** Supply chain management (SCM) is the planning, coordination and control of all activities that move goods from suppliers of raw material through manufacturers, wholesalers and retailers to the final customer, including information and money flows.

**Importance:**
- It **reduces cost** through better purchasing, transport, storage and inventory control.
- It **improves customer service**, with the right product at the right place and time.
- It **raises efficiency and productivity** by removing delay and duplication.
- It **lowers inventory** and wastage, which frees working capital.
- It builds **long-term relationships** with suppliers and partners.
- It **manages risk** and gives flexibility when demand or supply changes.
- It gives a **competitive advantage** through speed, quality and lower price.

@s 19
A **business letter** is a formal written message used for business purposes. A full-block or modified block layout has these parts, in order:

```figure caption="Structure of a business letter."
from matplotlib.patches import Rectangle
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4.2,3.0)); ax.axis('off'); ax.set_xlim(0,10); ax.set_ylim(0,10)
ax.add_patch(Rectangle((1.3,0.2),7.4,9.6,fc='white',ec=INK,lw=1))
parts=[('Letterhead (name, address, contact)',9.1),('Date',8.2),('Inside address (receiver)',7.3),('Salutation: Dear Sir/Madam',6.4),('Subject line',5.5),('Body: opening, details, closing',4.2),('Complimentary close: Yours faithfully',2.9),('Signature, name, designation',2.0),('Enclosures / cc',1.1)]
for t,y in parts:
    ax.add_patch(Rectangle((1.8,y-0.35),6.4,0.7,fc='#d9e9fa' if 'Body' not in t else '#fbe3c8',ec=INK,lw=0.6)); ax.text(5,y,t,ha='center',va='center',fontsize=7)
```

1. **Letterhead** with the firm's name, address, phone and email. 2. **Date.** 3. **Inside address** of the receiver. 4. **Salutation** such as "Dear Sir/Madam". 5. **Subject line**, a short statement of the matter. 6. **Body**, with an opening that states the purpose, the details in short paragraphs and a closing that asks for action. 7. **Complimentary close**, "Yours faithfully" when the name is unknown and "Yours sincerely" when it is known. 8. **Signature**, printed name and designation. 9. **Enclosures and copy (cc)** notation. A good letter uses the **7 Cs**: clear, concise, correct, courteous, complete, concrete and considerate.
