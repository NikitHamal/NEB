**(i)** An electrochemical cell is a device that converts the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy (a galvanic or voltaic cell). Its two half-cells are joined by a wire and a salt bridge, so electrons pass through the wire while ions pass through the bridge.

**(ii) Cell representation.** The electrode of lower reduction potential (Al, −1.67 V) is the anode and is written on the left; silver (+0.80 V) is the cathode on the right.
Al(s) | Al³⁺(aq) ‖ Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s)

**(iii) Cell reactions.** To balance the electrons, the silver half-reaction is multiplied by 3.
Anode (oxidation): Al(s) → Al³⁺(aq) + 3e⁻
Cathode (reduction): 3Ag⁺(aq) + 3e⁻ → 3Ag(s)
Overall: Al(s) + 3Ag⁺(aq) → Al³⁺(aq) + 3Ag(s)

**(iv) emf.** The coefficient 3 is not applied to the potential, because E° is an intensive property.
$$E^\circ_{cell} = E^\circ_{cathode} - E^\circ_{anode} = 0.80 - (-1.67) = +2.47\ \text{V}$$
The positive value shows that the cell reaction is spontaneous.

**Or (i)** Hess's law of constant heat summation states that the total enthalpy change of a chemical reaction is the same whether it takes place in one step or in several steps, provided the initial and final states are the same. It follows from enthalpy being a state function.

**(ii)** The enthalpy of reaction (ΔHᵣ) is the heat change at constant pressure when the number of moles of reactants shown in the balanced equation react completely to form the products, at the stated temperature and pressure. It is negative for exothermic and positive for endothermic reactions.

**(iii)** The printed enthalpy of H₂O is read as −286 kJ/mol (the sign is lost in the print); the formation of water is exothermic.
$$\Delta H_r = \sum \Delta H_f(\text{products}) - \sum \Delta H_f(\text{reactants})$$
Products: 2 × (−286) + 0 (O₂ is an element) = −572 kJ
Reactants: 2 × (−188) = −376 kJ
$$\Delta H_r = -572 - (-376) = -196\ \text{kJ}$$
The decomposition of two moles of H₂O₂ releases **196 kJ** (−98 kJ per mole of H₂O₂), so it is exothermic and spontaneous, which is why hydrogen peroxide must be stored with a stabiliser.

@s 13
**(i)** The ionic product of water is the product of the molar concentrations of H⁺ and OH⁻ ions in pure water (or in any dilute aqueous solution) at a given temperature. At 25 °C it is 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² L⁻².

**(ii) Deduction.** Water ionises very slightly:
H₂O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻
Applying the law of mass action,
$$K = \frac{[H^+][OH^-]}{[H_2O]}$$
Water is present in huge excess (55.5 mol L⁻¹) and only about 10⁻⁷ mol L⁻¹ ionises, so [H₂O] is practically constant and can be combined with K:
$$K[H_2O] = [H^+][OH^-] = K_w$$
Therefore **Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻]**, which is 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C because in pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁷ M.

**(iii)** HCl is a strong acid and ionises completely, so [H⁺] = 0.01 = 10⁻² M.
$$[OH^-] = \frac{K_w}{[H^+]} = \frac{1\times10^{-14}}{10^{-2}} = 1\times10^{-12}\ \text{M}$$
(The solution has pH 2 and pOH 12; pH + pOH = 14.)

**(iv)** The ionisation of water is endothermic (ΔH = +57.3 kJ/mol), so by Le Chatelier's principle a rise in temperature shifts the equilibrium to the right and **Kw increases**. For example Kw is 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C and about 5.5 × 10⁻¹³ at 100 °C. Because [H⁺] = [OH⁻], pure water stays neutral but its pH falls (about 6.13 at 100 °C).

@s 17
**Hoffmann's method** separates a mixture of 1°, 2° and 3° amines using **diethyl oxalate**, (COOC₂H₅)₂, which reacts with the N–H bonds. A primary amine has two N–H, a secondary has one, a tertiary has none.

**Step 1 — treat with diethyl oxalate.** The mixture is warmed with diethyl oxalate:
- A **primary** amine (e.g. ethylamine) replaces both ethoxy groups to give a solid, **N,N′-diethyloxamide** (dialkyl oxamide, a solid):
2C₂H₅NH₂ + (COOC₂H₅)₂ → (CONHC₂H₅)₂ + 2C₂H₅OH
- A **secondary** amine replaces only one ethoxy group, because after that it has no N–H left to continue. The product is a liquid, **ethyl N,N-diethyloxamate** (dialkyl oxamic ester):
(C₂H₅)₂NH + (COOC₂H₅)₂ → C₂H₅OOC–CON(C₂H₅)₂ + C₂H₅OH
- A **tertiary** amine has no replaceable hydrogen and does **not react**.

**Step 2 — fractional distillation.** The mixture is distilled. The unreacted **tertiary amine** (lower boiling point) distils over first and is collected.

**Step 3 — separate the solid from the liquid.** The residue is cooled; the solid dialkyl oxamide (1° derivative) is filtered off from the liquid dialkyl oxamic ester (2° derivative), which passes through as the filtrate (it can also be distilled out).

**Step 4 — hydrolysis.** Each derivative is boiled with aqueous KOH to regenerate its amine:
(CONHC₂H₅)₂ + 2KOH → (COOK)₂ + 2C₂H₅NH₂ (primary amine, distilled out)
C₂H₅OOC–CON(C₂H₅)₂ + 2KOH → (COOK)₂ + C₂H₅OH + (C₂H₅)₂NH (secondary amine, distilled out)

All three amines are thus recovered separately.

**(a)** *(The printed rate law is rate = k[P]ᵐ[Q]ⁿ with overall order m + n.)*

**(i) Rate law.** The rate law (rate equation) is the expression that relates the rate of a reaction to the molar concentrations of the reactants, each raised to a power determined by experiment: rate = k[P]ᵐ[Q]ⁿ. The sum of the powers is the overall order.

**(ii) Why experimental.** The powers m and n cannot be read from the coefficients of the balanced equation, because most reactions take place in several elementary steps and the overall rate is governed by the slowest (rate-determining) step; the species in that step, and the powers, are found only by measuring how the initial rate changes when concentrations are changed (for example, 2N₂O₅ → 4NO₂ + O₂ has the coefficient 2 but is first order in N₂O₅, and the decomposition of H₂O₂ is first order although two molecules appear in the equation). Hence the rate law is an experimental result.

**(iii) Rate constant.** The rate constant (specific reaction rate), k, is the rate of the reaction when the concentration of every reactant is unity (1 mol L⁻¹). It depends on temperature and catalyst but not on concentration, and its unit depends on the order: (mol L⁻¹)¹⁻ⁿ s⁻¹.

**(iv) Order and molecularity.**

| Order | Molecularity |
|---|---|
| sum of powers of concentration terms in the experimental rate law | number of molecules colliding in an elementary step |
| determined experimentally | determined theoretically from the mechanism |
| may be zero, fractional or integer | always a whole number (1, 2, 3), never zero |
| applies to overall and complex reactions | applies to a single elementary step |

**(b)** Orders: first order in P and zero order in Q, so rate = k[P]¹[Q]⁰ = k[P], independent of [Q].

**(i)** From Experiment I: k = rate/[P] = (2 × 10⁻²)/0.1 = 0.2 s⁻¹.
- **A:** Experiment II has rate 4 × 10⁻² M s⁻¹, so [P] = A = (4 × 10⁻²)/0.2 = **0.2 M** (doubling [P] doubles the rate; the change of [Q] 0.1 → 0.2 has no effect).
- **C:** Experiment III has [P] = 0.4 M, so rate = 0.2 × 0.4 = **8 × 10⁻² M s⁻¹** (the change of [Q] to 0.4 does not matter).
- **B:** Experiment IV has rate 2 × 10⁻² M s⁻¹, equal to Experiment I, so [P] = B = **0.1 M**.

**(ii) Rate constant.**
$$k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[P]} = \frac{2\times10^{-2}\ \text{M s}^{-1}}{0.1\ \text{M}} = 0.2\ \text{s}^{-1}$$
The unit s⁻¹ is that of a first-order reaction overall; check with Experiment III: 0.2 × 0.4 = 0.08 = 8 × 10⁻² ✓.

**Or (a)(i) Primary standard solution.** A primary standard solution is a solution of accurately known concentration prepared by dissolving an accurately weighed amount of a primary standard substance in a definite volume of water. A primary standard is a pure, stable, non-hygroscopic substance of high molar mass and known composition (e.g. oxalic acid dihydrate, anhydrous Na₂CO₃).

**(ii) Indicator.** No external indicator is used. KMnO₄ is a **self-indicator**: its purple colour disappears while Mn²⁺ forms (the solution is colourless), and the first drop of excess KMnO₄ gives a permanent light-pink colour at the end point.

**(a)(i) Portland cement.** Portland cement is a fine, grey powder of calcium silicates and calcium aluminates which, when mixed with water, sets into a hard, stone-like mass. It was named by Joseph Aspdin (1824) because the set cement looks like the building stone from Portland in England.
