@s 12
**(i)** De Moivre's theorem.

**(ii)** Replacing $n$ by $-n$ the statement becomes $[\,r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)\,]^{-n} = r^{-n}[\cos n\theta - i\sin n\theta]$. This holds because $(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)^{-n} = \dfrac{1}{\cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta} = \cos n\theta - i\sin n\theta$, so the theorem is true for every integer $n$.

**(iii)** $r$ is the **modulus** of the complex number $z = r(\cos\theta + i\sin\theta)$, that is $r = |z| = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$, the distance of the point $z$ from the origin in the Argand plane.

**(iv)** $\theta$ is the **argument** (amplitude) of $z$: the angle which the line from the origin to the point $z$ makes with the positive real axis, $\theta = \tan^{-1}\dfrac{y}{x}$ (adjusted for the quadrant).

**(v)** Application: finding powers and roots of complex numbers. For example the $n$th roots of unity are $\cos\dfrac{2k\pi}{n} + i\sin\dfrac{2k\pi}{n}$, $k = 0, 1, \dots, n-1$. It is also used to write $\cos n\theta$ and $\sin n\theta$ in powers of $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$, e.g. $\cos3\theta = 4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta$.

@s 19
**(a)** Let $AB = d$. Take the like parallel forces $X$ at $A$ and $Y$ at $B$ ($X > Y$). Their resultant $R = X + Y$ acts at a point $C$ on $AB$ which divides $AB$ internally in the inverse ratio of the forces (the moments about $C$ balance):
$$X\cdot AC = Y\cdot CB.$$
With $AC + CB = d$ we get $X\cdot AC = Y(d - AC)$, so
$$AC = \frac{Y}{X + Y}\,d.$$
Now let the forces interchange their positions: $Y$ acts at $A$ and $X$ at $B$. The resultant is still $X + Y$ and it acts at a new point $C'$ with
$$Y\cdot AC' = X\cdot C'B,\qquad\text{so}\qquad AC' = \frac{X}{X + Y}\,d.$$
Both $C$ and $C'$ lie on $AB$, measured from $A$. The displacement of the point of application of the resultant is
$$CC' = AC' - AC = \frac{X}{X + Y}d - \frac{Y}{X + Y}d = \frac{X - Y}{X + Y}\,AB.$$
Since $X > Y$, $C'$ lies nearer to $B$ than $C$ does: the point moves towards $B$ through $\dfrac{X-Y}{X+Y}AB$. Hence shown.

Special checks: if $X = Y$ the displacement is 0 (the resultant is at the mid-point both times); if $Y\to0$ the displacement tends to $AB$ (the resultant moves from $A$ to $B$).

**(a)** For $f$ to obey the mean value theorem (MVT) on $[0, 1]$ it must be (1) continuous on $[0, 1]$ and (2) differentiable on $(0, 1)$.
*Continuity at $x = \tfrac12$:* left limit $= \dfrac12 - \dfrac12 = 0$; right limit $= \left(\dfrac12 - \dfrac12\right)^2 = 0$; $f\!\left(\tfrac12\right) = 0$. So $f$ is continuous (the pieces are polynomials elsewhere).
*Differentiability at $x = \tfrac12$:* for $x < \tfrac12$, $f'(x) = -1$, so the left derivative is $-1$. For $x > \tfrac12$, $f'(x) = 2\left(\tfrac12 - x\right)(-1) = 2x - 1$, which tends to $0$ at $x = \tfrac12$. The left and right derivatives differ ($-1\neq0$), so $f$ is **not differentiable** at $x = \tfrac12\in(0, 1)$.
Condition (2) fails. We can also check the conclusion directly: $f(0) = \tfrac12$, $f(1) = \tfrac14$, so $\dfrac{f(1) - f(0)}{1 - 0} = -\dfrac14$. For $x < \tfrac12$, $f' = -1\neq-\tfrac14$. For $x > \tfrac12$, $2x - 1 = -\tfrac14$ gives $x = \tfrac38$, which is not greater than $\tfrac12$. No point satisfies it.
**So $f(x)$ does not obey the mean value theorem in $[0, 1]$**, because it is not differentiable at $x = \tfrac12$.
