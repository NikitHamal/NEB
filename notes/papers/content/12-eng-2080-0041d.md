For centuries, time was measured by the position of the Sun with use of sundials. Noon was recognised when the Sun was the highest in the sky, and cities would set their clock by this **apparent** solar time, even though some cities would often be on a slightly different time. Daylight saving Time (DST), sometimes called summer time, was instituted to make better use of daylight. Thus, clocks are set forward one hour in the spring to move an hour of daylight from the morning to the evening and then set back one hour in the fall to return to normal daylight.

Benjamin Franklin first **conceived** the idea of daylight saving during his tenure as an American delegate in Paris in 1984 and wrote about it extensively in his essay, “An Economical Project.” It is said that Franklin awoke early one morning and was surprised to see the sunlight at such an hour. Always the economist, Franklin believed the practice of moving the time could save on the use of candlelight, as candles were expensive at the time.

In England, builder William Willett (1957-1915) became a strong supporter for Daylight Saving Time upon noticing blinds of many houses were closed on an early sunny morning. Willett believed everyone, including himself, would appreciate longer hours of light in the evenings. In 1909, Sir Robert Pearce introduced a bill in the House of Commons to make it **obligatory** to adjust the clocks. A bill was drafted and introduced into Parliament several times but met with great opposition, mostly from farmers. Eventually, in 1925, it was decided that summer time should begin on the day following the third Saturday in April and close after the first Saturday in October.

The U.S. congress passed the standard Time Act of 1918 to establish standard time and **preserve** and set Daylight saving Time across the continent. This act also devised five time Zones throughout United States : Eastern, central, Mountain, Pacific, and Alaska. The first time zone was set on “the mean astronomical time of the seventy-fifth degree of longitude west from Greenwich” (England). In 1919, this act was cancelled.

President Roosevelt established year-round Daylight saving Time (also called War Time) from 1942-1945. However, after this period, each state **adopted** its own DST, which proved to be **disconcerting** to television and radio broadcasting and transportation. In 1996, President Lyndon Johnson **created** the Department of Transportation and signed the Uniform Time Act. As a result, the Department of transportation was given the responsibility for the time laws. During the oil embargo and energy crisis of the 1970s, President Richard Nixon extended DST through the Daylight Saving Time Energy Act of 1973 to conserve energy further. This law was modified in 1986, and Daylight Saving Time was reset to begin on the first Sunday in April (to spring ahead) and end on the last Sunday in October (to fall back).

@s 2
**(a) My Old Home.** The narrator went back to his native village after more than twenty years for a practical reason: the old house, shared by the clan, had been sold and had to be handed over to its new owners by a fixed date. He came to pack up, say goodbye to the home of his childhood, and take his mother and his little nephew Hung-erh to the town where he now lives and works. It was a farewell visit, made in cold, gloomy weather and a sad mood.

**(b) The Half-Closed Eyes of the Buddha and the Slowly Sinking Sun.** The tourist declares that the East has always given and the West has taken. Nepal, as part of the East, has given the West religion and the Puranas, images made of brass, ornaments of ivory, manuscripts written on palm leaves and inscriptions on copperplate. Above all it has given civilisation and wisdom, and the Buddha's message of peace. The tourist boasts of this long list, while the old man and the villagers simply live it.

**Or. On Libraries.** Oliver Sacks says that as a boy he was "biased towards the sciences". He was fascinated by chemistry, metals, the periodic table and the working of living things, and he wanted books that explained them. His home library and the local library could not satisfy this hunger, so he turned to the library of the Science Museum in London, where he devoured many volumes, and later to the science libraries at Oxford. For him science was a living adventure, not a school subject.

**(c) Every Morning I Wake.** The poet, Dylan Thomas, calls "all poor creatures born to die" poor creatures. He means every living being, human beings included, because all are mortal and powerless against death. They are poor not for lack of money but because life is short and uncertain, "whether we last the night or no". The poet prays to God to keep a loving eye on them, to bless the town and let everyone see another day, showing his compassion and humility.

**(d) Humility.** The author concludes this because every human society that has ever existed has had moral codes, art, religion or spiritual practice and creative expression. Since all groups share these abilities, they cannot belong to one race or one culture. They must be inherited from the common ancestors of humankind who lived in Stone Age Africa, so they are part of our DNA. In my opinion the argument is sound: it removes the pride of any group claiming to have "invented" civilisation and teaches humility.

**Or. Knowledge and Wisdom.** Yes, I agree with Bertrand Russell that wisdom is more important. Knowledge is the collection of facts and skills; wisdom is the right use of them, which needs a sense of proportion, the habit of looking at things in a wide perspective and freedom from fanaticism. Knowledge without wisdom can destroy, as the atomic bomb shows, while wisdom decides whether knowledge is used for human good. Knowledge increases our power; wisdom guides that power towards kindness and peace.

**(e) A Matter of Husbands.** The letter is the main piece of "evidence" in the play. The Earnest Young Woman, the wife, brings a letter found on her husband's desk which seems to be from an actress who is meeting him ("My darling..."), to prove that the Famous Actress has taken her husband. The actress turns the letter around by arguing that no guilty husband would leave such a letter on his desk, so he left it for his wife to find, wanting to make her jealous. The letter thus drives the plot, creates the conflict and leads to the clever twist.

@s 3
**(a) I Was My Own Route.** Julia de Burgos felt confined by a society that wanted women to be quiet, obedient wives and ornamental figures; she refused to live as a "puppet" and chose to walk her own road. As the head of a local government unit I would first listen to such women through a women's forum and a complaint desk in every ward, since no policy works unless the people concerned are heard. I would then take practical steps:
- allot part of the municipal budget to women's skill training, small loans and cooperatives so that they can earn and be independent;
- guarantee women's seats and speaking time in ward committees and user groups;
- run awareness campaigns, with schools and local media, against domestic violence, child marriage and discrimination, and set up a safe shelter and free legal aid;
- give scholarships to girls and make roads, toilets and street lighting safe for women.
In this way women like Julia would not have to leave their community to be free: they could be their own route within it.

**(b) Facing Death.** Yes, I think Durand's step was right in his circumstances, though a painful one. He is a poor, widowed innkeeper crushed by debt; the baker, butcher and grocer are pressing for payment, and his daughters Adèle, Thérèse and Annette will be left penniless and homeless. To save them he burns down the insured house and poisons himself, so that the death looks like an accident and the fire-insurance money clears the debts and gives his daughters a new start. Before he dies he forgives Thérèse, blesses Annette and Antonio, and tells Adèle the plan. It is an act of sacrificial love and shows his courage and responsibility as a father. But I also think the deceit and the destruction of property are wrong, and he should have found help from relatives or the law. Under the pressure he faced, however, his sacrifice is a moving and justifiable choice for a father.

**Or. My Old Home.** *Runtu in Chinese society.* The boy Runtu, a bright, lively friend of the narrator, becomes a worn, silent, poor peasant. Famine, heavy taxes, soldiers, bandits, officials and landlords, along with many children to feed, crushed him; the rigid class system makes him call his boyhood friend "Master" and he stands "like a lifeless wooden figure". His case shows the sufferings of the Chinese peasantry in the early twentieth century.

*Nepal today.* In my opinion the living conditions of the average Nepali are improving slowly but unevenly. Roads, electricity, mobile phones and the internet, schools, and health posts have reached villages; remittance and migration have raised incomes. Still, inflation, unemployment, youth migration, corruption and poor public services make daily life hard for many families. So I conclude that it is getting better in facilities but is still difficult in livelihood. As the narrator hopes, hope "is like roads": they are made by many people walking together.

**Rewritten sentences.**

| Item | Answer | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | This year the whole winter season was dry. There was very **little** rain. | 'rain' is uncountable, so *little* (meaning almost none) is used, not *few/many* |
| (b) | The Principal along with other experts **was** present at the meeting. | the subject is the singular 'Principal'; 'along with' does not make it plural |
| (c) | We got wet in the rain **although/though** we had an umbrella. | contrast between getting wet and having an umbrella |
| (d) | People **might** think it is interesting but I think it is pathetic. | expresses possibility; *could* is also acceptable |
| (e) | The experts **often** go for a walk on Saturdays. | adverbs of frequency come before the main verb |
| (f) | Health care in Nepal is not as **expensive** as it is abroad. | as + adjective + as |
| (g) | The class teacher **told the student not to ignore his/her study**. | an order or advice in the negative changes to *told ... not to* |
| (h) | A bar of chocolate **was given to children by parents**. (or: Children were given a bar of chocolate by parents.) | passive: object + was/were + past participle + by + agent |
| (i) | These leaders have great respect **for** the academicians of the country. | the noun *respect* takes the preposition *for* |
| (j) | This is the house **which** belongs to my uncle. | a relative pronoun for a thing is *which* (or *that*) |
