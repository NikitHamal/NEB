@s 11
**Dimensional formula (4 marks).** The dimensional formula of a physical quantity expresses it in terms of the powers of the fundamental dimensions $M$, $L$ and $T$. For example, speed has the formula $[M^{0}LT^{-1}]$.

Let the time period depend on the mass $m$ of the bob, the length $l$ and $g$:

$$T = k\, m^{a}\, l^{b}\, g^{c}$$

where $k$ is a dimensionless constant. Writing the dimensions of each quantity ($[T]$, $[M]$, $[L]$ and $g$ as $[LT^{-2}]$):

$$[M^{0}L^{0}T^{1}] = [M]^{a}[L]^{b}[LT^{-2}]^{c} = [M^{a}L^{b+c}T^{-2c}]$$

Comparing powers: $a = 0$, $b + c = 0$ and $-2c = 1$. So $c = -\tfrac12$ and $b = +\tfrac12$.

$$T = k\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$$

The period does not depend on the mass. Dimensional analysis cannot give $k$; experiment and theory give $k = 2\pi$, so $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$.

@s 12
**Resolution of a vector (4 marks).** Splitting a single vector into two or more vectors, called components, which together produce the same effect as the original vector is called resolution. Rectangular components are the two components along perpendicular axes.

For a force $F$ at angle $\theta$ with the x-axis, the components are $F_x = F\cos\theta$ and $F_y = F\sin\theta$.

Here $F = 20$ N and $\theta = 60^\circ$:

$$F_x = 20\cos 60^\circ = 20\times0.5 = 10\ \text{N}$$
$$F_y = 20\sin 60^\circ = 20\times0.866 = 17.32\ \text{N}$$

Check: $\sqrt{10^{2}+17.32^{2}} = \sqrt{100+300} = 20$ N, so the components rebuild the original force.

@s 13
**Second equation of motion (4 marks).** Take a body with initial velocity $u$ and uniform acceleration $a$; after time $t$ its velocity is $v$. The velocity-time graph is a straight line $AB$ (figure). The displacement $s$ equals the area under the graph, which is a trapezium made of a rectangle (height $u$, width $t$) and a triangle (base $t$, height $v-u$).


$$s = \text{area of rectangle} + \text{area of triangle} = ut + \tfrac12 (v-u)\,t$$

From the definition of acceleration, $v - u = at$. Substituting,

$$s = ut + \tfrac12\,(at)\,t = ut + \tfrac12 at^{2}$$

This is the required equation. If the body starts from rest ($u=0$) it reduces to $s = \tfrac12at^{2}$.

@s 14
**Conservation of momentum (4 marks).** *Principle:* if no external force acts on a system, the total linear momentum of the system remains constant. (In a collision, the total momentum before equals the total momentum after.)

*Numerical.* Take the direction of the initial motion as positive. $m = 0.15$ kg, $u = +20$ m/s, $v = -15$ m/s (reversed), $\Delta t = 0.02$ s.

$$\text{Impulse} = \Delta p = m(v-u) = 0.15\,(-15-20) = -5.25\ \text{N s}$$

The magnitude of the impulse is $5.25$ N s, directed away from the wall.

$$F_{avg} = \frac{\text{Impulse}}{\Delta t} = \frac{-5.25}{0.02} = -262.5\ \text{N}$$

The wall exerts an average force of about $262.5$ N on the ball, opposite to its original motion.

@s 15
**Work-energy theorem (4 marks).** *Statement:* the net work done on a body by all the forces acting on it equals the change in its kinetic energy.

*Proof.* A constant net force $F$ acts on a body of mass $m$ and changes its speed from $u$ to $v$ over a displacement $s$ along the force. The acceleration is $a = F/m$ and, from the third equation of motion, $v^{2} - u^{2} = 2as$, so $s = \dfrac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{2a}$.

$$W = Fs = ma\cdot\frac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{2a}$$
$$W = \tfrac12 mv^{2} - \tfrac12 mu^{2} = K_f - K_i = \Delta K$$

Hence the work done equals the gain in kinetic energy. If $W$ is negative (a retarding force such as friction) the kinetic energy decreases by that amount.

@s 16
**Centripetal acceleration (4 marks).** The acceleration of a body in circular motion that is directed towards the centre of the circle and changes only the direction of the velocity is called centripetal acceleration.

*Derivation.* Let a particle move from $A$ to $B$ on a circle of centre $O$ and radius $r$ with uniform speed $v$, through a small angle $\theta$ in time $\Delta t$. The velocity vectors $\vec v_A$ and $\vec v_B$ have equal magnitude $v$ and differ in direction by $\theta$. The triangle of velocities $(\vec v_A,\ \vec v_B,\ \Delta\vec v)$ and the triangle $OAB$ are both isosceles with the same apex angle, so they are similar:

$$\frac{|\Delta \vec v|}{v} = \frac{AB}{r}$$

For a very small angle the chord $AB$ equals the arc, $AB = v\,\Delta t$. Hence $|\Delta\vec v| = v\cdot v\Delta t/r$, and

$$a = \frac{|\Delta\vec v|}{\Delta t} = \frac{v^{2}}{r} = \omega^{2}r$$

As $\theta\to0$, $\Delta\vec v$ points along $AO$, towards the centre.
