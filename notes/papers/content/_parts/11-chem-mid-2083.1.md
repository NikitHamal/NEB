**1. A.** $n = m/M = 11/44 = 0.25$ mol.

**2. B.** 18 g of water is 1 mol (molar mass 18 g/mol), and 1 mol holds $6.022\times10^{23}$ molecules (Avogadro's number).

**3. B.** The azimuthal (subsidiary) quantum number $l$ gives the shape ($l = 0$ is spherical $s$, $l = 1$ is dumb-bell $p$). $n$ gives size and energy, $m$ the orientation, $s$ the spin.

**4. B.** A shell holds $2n^{2}$ electrons: $2\times3^{2} = 18$.

**5. B.** Chromium is an exception: a half-filled $3d^{5}$ sub-shell with one electron in $4s$ is more stable than $3d^{4}4s^{2}$.

**6. C.** Fluorine, at the top right of the table (excluding noble gases), has the highest electronegativity, 4.0 on the Pauling scale.

**7. B.** Argon has 18 electrons. $K^{+}$ ($19-1$) has 18. $Na^{+}$ and $Mg^{2+}$ have 10 and $F^{-}$ has 10.

### Group B

@s 11
**Limiting reagent and stoichiometry (4 marks).** The reactant that is completely consumed first in a reaction, and so limits the amount of product formed, is the limiting reagent.

Molar mass of $CaCO_3 = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100$ g/mol. Moles of $CaCO_3 = 25/100 = 0.25$ mol. From the equation 1 mol $CaCO_3$ gives 1 mol $CO_2$, so 0.25 mol $CO_2$ forms.

$$m(CO_2) = 0.25\times44 = 11\ \text{g}$$
$$V(CO_2) = 0.25\times22.4 = 5.6\ \text{L at STP}$$

@s 12
**Empirical and molecular formula (4 marks).** Take 100 g of the compound.

| Element | Mass (g) | Moles | Ratio (÷ 3.333) |
|---|---|---|---|
| C | 40.00 | 40.00/12 = 3.333 | 1 |
| H | 6.67 | 6.67/1.008 ≈ 6.62 | 2 |
| O | 53.33 | 53.33/16 = 3.333 | 1 |

The empirical formula is $CH_2O$, with empirical mass $12 + 2 + 16 = 30$.

$$n = \frac{\text{molar mass}}{\text{empirical mass}} = \frac{180}{30} = 6$$

Molecular formula $= (CH_2O)_6 = C_6H_{12}O_6$ (glucose).

@s 13
**Uncertainty principle and de Broglie wavelength (4 marks).** *Principle:* it is impossible to measure the exact position and the exact momentum of a moving microscopic particle at the same time: $\Delta x\cdot\Delta p \ge h/4\pi$.

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{mv} = \frac{6.626\times10^{-34}}{9.109\times10^{-31}\times1\times10^{6}}$$
$$\lambda = 7.27\times10^{-10}\ \text{m} \approx 0.73\ \text{nm}$$

This is comparable to atomic dimensions, which is why electrons show wave behaviour in atoms.

@s 14
**Filling rules (4 marks).**
- **Aufbau principle:** electrons fill the orbitals in the order of increasing energy ($n + l$ rule): 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, ...
- **Pauli's exclusion principle:** no two electrons in an atom can have the same four quantum numbers, so an orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spins.
- **Hund's rule:** electrons occupy degenerate orbitals singly with parallel spins before any pairing starts.

Iron ($Z = 26$): $1s^{2}2s^{2}2p^{6}3s^{2}3p^{6}3d^{6}4s^{2}$, i.e. $[Ar]\,3d^{6}4s^{2}$. The six $3d$ electrons fill five orbitals as $\uparrow\downarrow, \uparrow, \uparrow, \uparrow, \uparrow$, so there are **4 unpaired electrons**.

@s 15
**Ionization energy (4 marks).** The minimum energy needed to remove the outermost electron from an isolated gaseous atom in its ground state is its first ionization energy.

Nitrogen is $1s^{2}2s^{2}2p^{3}$ and oxygen is $1s^{2}2s^{2}2p^{4}$. The $2p^{3}$ half-filled sub-shell of nitrogen is extra stable because of symmetry and maximum exchange energy, so more energy is needed to remove its electron. In oxygen one $2p$ orbital holds a pair, and the electron–electron repulsion in that orbital makes removal easier. Hence IE(N) = 1402 kJ/mol is greater than IE(O) = 1314 kJ/mol, even though the general trend across a period is an increase.

@s 16
**VSEPR and water (4 marks).** The central oxygen atom has six valence electrons, forming two bond pairs with H and carrying **two lone pairs**: four electron pairs in total. They repel each other and take a tetrahedral arrangement, but only two positions hold atoms, so the molecule is **bent (V-shaped)**.

Lone pair–lone pair repulsion is greater than lone pair–bond pair repulsion, which in turn is greater than bond pair–bond pair repulsion. The two lone pairs push the O–H bonds closer, squeezing the angle from the ideal 109.5° to **104.5°**. For comparison, $NH_3$ (one lone pair) has 107° and $CH_4$ (none) has 109.5°.

### Group C

@s 17
**(a) Bohr's postulates (4 marks).**
1. An electron revolves round the nucleus in certain fixed circular orbits (stationary states) without radiating energy.
2. Only those orbits are allowed in which the angular momentum is an integral multiple of $h/2\pi$: $mvr = nh/2\pi$ ($n = 1, 2, 3, \dots$).
3. Each orbit has a definite energy. Energy is absorbed or emitted only when an electron jumps between orbits, and the photon has energy $\Delta E = E_{2} - E_{1} = h\nu$.
4. The energy of the electron in the $n$th orbit of hydrogen is $E_n = -13.6/n^{2}$ eV, and the radius is $r_n = 0.529\,n^{2}$ Å.

**(b) Numerical (4 marks).** Energy in the second orbit:

$$E_2 = -\frac{13.6}{2^{2}} = -3.4\ \text{eV}$$

For the transition $n=3 \to n=2$ (Balmer series):

$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H\left(\frac{1}{2^{2}}-\frac{1}{3^{2}}\right) = 1.097\times10^{7}\times\frac{5}{36}$$
$$\frac{1}{\lambda} = 1.524\times10^{6}\ \text{m}^{-1}$$
$$\lambda = 6.56\times10^{-7}\ \text{m} = 656\ \text{nm}$$

This is the red H$_\alpha$ line, in the visible region.

@s 18
**(a) Ionic bond in NaCl (4 marks).** Sodium ($2,8,1$) loses its single valence electron to attain the neon configuration and becomes $Na^{+}$. Chlorine ($2,8,7$) gains that electron to attain the argon configuration and becomes $Cl^{-}$.

$$Na \rightarrow Na^{+} + e^{-}$$
$$Cl + e^{-} \rightarrow Cl^{-}$$

The oppositely charged ions attract each other by a strong electrostatic force: this is the ionic bond, and the ions pack in a crystal lattice in which each ion is surrounded by six of the opposite charge.

*Properties:* (i) high melting and boiling points, as the lattice is strong ($NaCl$ melts at 801 °C); (ii) hard, brittle crystalline solids; (iii) conduct electricity in the molten state or in aqueous solution but not as solids, because ions are free to move only then. (They are also generally soluble in water.)

**(b) $sp^{3}$ hybridization in methane (4 marks).** Carbon in the ground state is $1s^{2}2s^{2}2p_x^{1}2p_y^{1}$. One $2s$ electron is promoted to the empty $2p_z$ orbital, giving four unpaired electrons in $2s^{1}2p^{3}$. The one $2s$ and three $2p$ orbitals mix and form **four equivalent $sp^{3}$ hybrid orbitals** of equal energy and shape, directed to the corners of a regular tetrahedron at 109.5°.

Each $sp^{3}$ orbital overlaps end-on with the $1s$ orbital of a hydrogen atom to form four identical C–H σ bonds. So $CH_4$ is tetrahedral, with bond length 109 pm and bond angle 109.5°. The same hybridization appears in ethane ($C_2H_6$) and, with lone pairs, in $NH_3$ and $H_2O$.
