**Allocation of resources** is the process of distributing scarce resources among their competing uses, that is, deciding what to produce, how to produce and for whom to produce, so that human wants are satisfied as fully as possible.

@s 4
Two arguments for free trade: (i) it permits international **specialisation** according to comparative advantage, which raises efficiency and world output; (ii) it gives consumers a **wider choice of goods at lower prices**, and competition from imports improves quality.

Its value lies between 0 and 1.

### Group B

@s 12
**Division of labour** means dividing the total work of production into small, separate operations so that each worker performs only one or a few operations in which he or she is skilled. A pin factory, where one worker draws the wire, another cuts it and a third sharpens it, is the classical example (Adam Smith).

**Advantages:**
1. **Increase in skill and efficiency.** Repetition of one task makes the worker an expert, so output per worker rises.
2. **Saving of time.** No time is lost in changing tools and moving from one job to another.
3. **Use of machinery.** Simple operations can be mechanised, which raises productivity.
4. **Large-scale production and low cost.** More output at lower average cost, so prices fall.
5. **Better quality.** Each task is done by the person best suited to it.
6. **Use of natural talent.** Workers are placed in the jobs for which they have aptitude.
7. **Innovation and employment.** Specialisation encourages invention of tools and creates jobs for people with varied skills.

The main drawbacks, such as monotony and dependence on others, do not remove these benefits.

**Or (Q12). Capitalist economy.** A capitalist economy is one in which the means of production are privately owned and decisions on production and distribution are taken by private individuals through the price mechanism for profit. Its **features** are: (i) **private property** and the right to inherit; (ii) **freedom of enterprise and choice** for producers, workers and consumers; (iii) **profit motive** as the driving force; (iv) **price mechanism**, in which demand and supply fix prices and allocate resources; (v) **competition** among firms; (vi) **consumer sovereignty**, since production follows consumer demand; (vii) **limited role of the government**, confined to law, defence and public goods; (viii) **inequality of income and wealth** as a result of the unequal ownership of property. The USA is the typical example.

@s 13
The theory of rent was given by **David Ricardo**. **Rent** is the payment made to the owner of land for the use of the **original and indestructible powers of the soil**. It arises because lands differ in fertility and location, while land is limited in supply.

**Assumptions:** land differs in fertility; the law of diminishing returns operates; there is perfect competition; the most fertile land is cultivated first; and the marginal (no-rent) land only just covers its cost of cultivation.

**Explanation.** As population grows, cultivation spreads to poorer land. The poorest land in use, the **marginal land**, earns no rent because its produce only pays the cost. Better lands earn a surplus over the marginal land, and that surplus is the rent.

| Grade of land | Output (quintals) | Rent (quintals) |
|---|---:|---:|
| A (best) | 40 | 40 - 20 = 20 |
| B | 30 | 30 - 20 = 10 |
| C (marginal) | 20 | 0 |

Hence **rent = produce of the land - produce of the marginal land**. The same principle holds for **intensive cultivation**, where successive doses of labour and capital on the same land give diminishing returns, and the surplus over the last (marginal) dose is rent.

**Conclusion.** Ricardo stated that rent does not enter the price of produce; price is fixed by the cost on marginal land, and rent is the result of that price. The theory is criticised because land has some alternative uses, and not all rent is due to fertility, but it explains differential rent well.

@s 15
A monopolist is the sole seller of a product with no close substitute, so it faces a **downward sloping AR (demand) curve** and the **MR curve lies below AR**. It is a price maker and its aim is to maximise profit.

**Conditions of equilibrium:** (i) **MC = MR**, and (ii) the **MC curve cuts the MR curve from below**, that is MC rises faster than MR beyond the point. At any output where MR > MC an extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost, so output should be expanded; where MR < MC it should be reduced.

**Illustration.** Let AR: P = 10 - 0.5Q, so MR = 10 - Q, and MC = 2 + Q. Setting MR = MC gives 10 - Q = 2 + Q, so **Q = 4**, MR = MC = 6. The price is read from the AR curve: P = 10 - 0.5(4) = **Rs. 8**.

@s 16
A **commercial bank** is a financial institution that accepts deposits from the public and gives loans for profit. Its main functions are:
1. **Accepting deposits** in current, saving, fixed and call accounts, which gives safety and interest to depositors.
2. **Advancing loans:** loans, overdrafts, cash credit and discounting of bills to individuals and businesses; this is the main source of income.
3. **Credit creation.** Banks create deposits by lending a part of their deposits, which increases the money supply.
4. **Agency functions:** collecting cheques and bills, paying insurance premiums and taxes, buying and selling shares and securities for customers.
5. **Remittance and transfer of funds** by draft, mobile and internet banking, and receipt of foreign remittance.
6. **Foreign exchange** dealings and issue of letters of credit and guarantees for trade.
7. **General utility services:** lockers, debit and credit cards, ATM, and advice on investment.

These functions mobilise savings into investment and so promote economic development.

@s 17
Poverty reduction has been a goal of every periodic plan in Nepal since the Fifth Plan, and the Constitution of Nepal gives a right to social security. The main efforts are:
- **Special programmes:** the **Poverty Alleviation Fund** (2003) for the poor and excluded groups, the Karnali Employment Programme, **Prime Minister Employment Programme** and the Youth and Small Entrepreneur Self-employment Fund.
- **Social security allowances** for senior citizens, single women, the disabled and endangered groups.
- **Microfinance** and cooperative loans to the poor, particularly women.
- **Land reform**, free basic education and the expansion of health services to rural areas.
- **Infrastructure** such as rural roads and electrification, which open markets and jobs, and a pro-poor budget with targeted grants to local governments.

**Evaluation.** The incidence of poverty has fallen from about 42 per cent in 1995/96 to about 25 per cent in 2010/11 and to roughly 20 per cent in the latest living standards survey, which shows progress. However, much of the fall came from **remittance** and not from productive jobs, poverty remains high in Karnali and Madhesh, and weak implementation, leakage and duplication reduce the effect. The efforts are therefore helpful but not fully effective.

@s 18
Nepal follows **periodic planning** since 1956, and plan formulation is led by the **National Planning Commission (NPC)** chaired by the Prime Minister. The process is:
1. **Approach paper and objectives.** The NPC prepares the long-term vision and the approach paper that states the goals, strategies and the macro targets for growth and employment.
2. **Sector committees and working groups.** Experts, ministries and the private sector study each sector and propose programmes and projects.
3. **Resource estimation.** Domestic revenue, borrowing and foreign aid are estimated to see how much can be spent, using the medium term expenditure framework.
4. **Consultation.** The draft is discussed with provincial and local governments, civil society, the private sector and development partners.
5. **Approval.** The **National Development Council** and the Council of Ministers approve the plan, and it is presented to the Federal Parliament.
6. **Implementation, monitoring and evaluation.** The ministries and the three tiers of government carry out the programmes through annual budgets, and the NPC monitors progress and evaluates results.

This gives plans that are linked to resources and to the Sustainable Development Goals.

Mean = 1505 / 27 = 55.74. Variance = 985.2 / 27 = 36.49, so **SD = sqrt(36.49) = 6.04 marks**.

**Why SD is superior to mean deviation:** (i) mean deviation **ignores the signs** of the deviations, which is mathematically unsound, while SD removes the signs by **squaring**; (ii) SD is **algebraically workable**, and is used in correlation, regression, sampling and tests of significance; and (iii) SD is **less affected by sampling fluctuations** and gives more stable results.

**Or (Q19). Paasche's index.** Paasche index = (Sum p1q1 / Sum p0q1) x 100. Sum p1q1 = 150 + 80 + 60 + 91 = 381 and Sum p0q1 = 90 + 50 + 36 + 104 = 280, so the index is 381 / 280 x 100 = **136.07**; prices have risen by about 36 per cent from 2070 to 2078.

**Difference.** Paasche uses the **current year quantities** as weights, while Laspeyres uses the **base year quantities**. Paasche needs new quantity data every year, so it is costly, and it tends to **understate** the price rise; Laspeyres is simpler and tends to **overstate** it.

@s 21
**Capital expenditure** is government spending that creates or improves **physical and human assets** which last for more than a year, such as roads, bridges, airports, hydropower, irrigation, schools, hospitals and industrial estates. It differs from **recurrent expenditure** (salary, administration, subsidies), which only keeps the government running.

**Its vital role in economic development:**
1. **Builds infrastructure.** Roads, power, communications and irrigation are the base for every other activity; without them private investment does not come.
2. **Raises production and productivity.** Irrigation and power increase agricultural and industrial output, and better transport lowers the cost of goods.
3. **Creates employment and income.** Construction projects employ workers directly and create demand for materials, which has a **multiplier effect** on income.
4. **Attracts private investment (crowding in).** Public capital spending makes private projects profitable and gives confidence to domestic and foreign investors.
5. **Develops human capital.** Spending on schools, training and health raises the skill and health of the labour force, which is the most important source of long-term growth.
6. **Reduces regional inequality.** Investment in remote and backward areas, for example Karnali and Madhesh, spreads the benefits of development.
7. **Promotes structural change and exports.** Industrial and energy projects shift the economy from agriculture toward industry and services and can lower the import bill, for example through hydropower replacing imported fuel.
8. **Builds national assets for the future.** Capital assets give returns for decades and improve the capacity of the economy to grow.

**Nepal's case.** Nepal's capital expenditure is a small share of the budget and is often spent below the target because of weak project preparation, delays in procurement and construction, and the pile-up of spending at the end of the fiscal year. This has slowed growth. Raising the share of capital spending and the quality of implementation is therefore essential to move the economy to a higher growth path.

@s 22
**Foreign trade** is the exchange of goods and services between Nepal and other countries. Nepal's trade is characterised by a narrow base and a very wide gap between imports and exports.

**Direction.**
- **Exports** go mostly to **India**, which takes roughly two-thirds of Nepal's exports, followed by the USA, Germany, the United Kingdom, Bangladesh and other countries. The main exports are palm oil, soybean oil, jute goods, cardamom, tea, carpet, garments and pulses.
- **Imports** come mainly from **India** (more than half of the total) and **China**, and also from the UAE, Indonesia, Argentina and others. The main imports are petroleum products, machinery, vehicles, electronic goods, iron and steel, gold and fertilizer.
- Trade with third countries passes through Indian ports, such as Kolkata, and the **transit** is under the treaty with India.

**Problems.**
1. **Large and growing trade deficit.** Imports are several times exports, and the gap is financed by remittance and aid.
2. **Narrow export base.** A few primary and semi-processed goods make most of the exports, and many are re-exports of imported raw materials.
3. **Dependence on India** for both markets and routes, which exposes the country to policy changes and border delays.
4. **Low competitiveness.** High cost of production, low productivity and weak quality standards reduce the ability to compete.
5. **Landlocked position.** High transport and transit costs and delays at the border.
6. **Non-tariff barriers.** Strict sanitary and quality requirements block exports of agricultural goods.
7. **Weak infrastructure and institutions.** Poor energy supply, testing laboratories and trade facilitation.

**Suggestions.** Diversify exports, promote import-substituting industries and use of hydropower, improve trade facilitation and transit, ensure quality standards, and sign market access agreements to reduce the deficit.

**Or (Q22). Foreign employment and the Nepalese economy.** Foreign employment, in which Nepali workers go mainly to the Gulf, Malaysia, Korea and Japan, now contributes to the economy in several ways.
1. **Remittance.** The inflow is around a quarter of GDP, one of the highest shares in the world, and is the largest source of foreign exchange.
2. **Balance of payments.** It covers the large trade deficit and has built up foreign exchange reserves, which keep the balance of payments in surplus.
3. **Poverty reduction.** The money raises the income of households, raises consumption and has reduced the poverty rate.
4. **Education, health and housing.** Families invest in the children's schooling and in housing, which raises human capital.
5. **Employment.** It gives jobs to a large number of young people who could not find work at home, and so relieves the pressure of unemployment.
6. **Skills and savings.** Workers gain skills, technology and work discipline, and the savings can be invested in small businesses on return.
7. **Financial sector.** Remittance raises bank deposits and the growth of banks and money transfer companies.

**Limitations.** Remittance mostly goes to consumption and land and housing instead of productive investment; the departure of the young causes a shortage of labour in agriculture and a loss of skilled people (brain drain); the economy becomes dependent on conditions abroad; and workers face risks of exploitation. With policies to channel remittance into productive investment, to train workers and to protect them, foreign employment can contribute even more.
