The Maginot Line was one of the largest military structures ever built, second only to the Great Wall of China. It was a series of bunkers, forts, turrets, and obstacles that spanned more than 450 miles of France's border with Germany. Built between 1930 and 1940, it was one of the world's most **impressive** forts, yet it proved to be almost useless.

The Maginot Line was named after the man who argued for its construction, French Minister of War Andre Maginot. Andre Maginot had fought with the French against the Germans in the First World War. Much of this **conflict** took place along the Western Front, which was a line of trenches across which the two sides faced one another. Both sides dug in deep and each lost many men over little ground. Conditions were **horrid** for all and there was a stalemate for many years as neither side was able to move the other.

Maginot never forgot these awful conditions. He wanted to build a line of defenses that would give the French an advantage in a similar conflict. He feared, rightfully so, that the Germans would attack France again. Germany's population nearly doubled France's. The line of defenses that Maginot pictured would allow a smaller French army to hold off a large German force. In 1929, Maginot convinced the French Parliament to fund his **vision**.

Though calling it a line makes it seem thin, the Maginot Line was in fact quite deep. It was fifteen miles wide at some points and varied in structure. There were outposts disguised as houses. These were manned by troops and rigged with explosives. There were antitank rails and **obstacles**. These were planted in the ground to prevent tanks and trucks from passing. There were bunkers armed with mounted machine guns and anti-tank guns. These were for pushing back attackers. And there were many large and small **fortresses** along the line. Each had mess halls, lots of supplies, and air conditioning. The Maginot line would give the French a supreme edge in the case of a head-on **invasion** by the Germans.

Unfortunately for the French, the Germans did not attack head-on. They positioned a decoy army in front of the line to distract the French. While the French waited, the Germans snuck a larger force through Belgium. Belgium is France's northeastern neighbor. The French did have some defenses along their border with Belgium, but this part of the Maginot line was weak. The Germans made quick work of these defenses. Within five days of their initial attack they were well into France.

Once they were in France, the Germans attempted to seize the main forts along the Maginot Line. They were not successful. The forts had proved to be strong, but they failed to hold back the Germans. The Germans had taken Paris, France's capital city. Soon after the French commander ordered his men to stand down. He commanded the French defenders to leave their bases along the Maginot Line. These soldiers were then taken to Prisoner of War (POW) camps.

**C. Answers.**
(a) The Maginot Line was built to defend France against another German attack. André Maginot had seen the terrible stalemate of trench warfare in the First World War and feared that Germany would attack again. A chain of forts and bunkers would let a smaller French army hold off a larger German force.
(b) Germany's population was almost double that of France. This is why Maginot wanted strong fixed defences: France could not match Germany in number of soldiers.
(c) Antitank obstacles (rails and similar barriers) were planted in the ground to stop tanks and trucks from passing, so that attackers could not drive through the line and could be pushed back by the machine guns and anti-tank guns in the bunkers.
(d) The Germans put a decoy army in front of the Maginot Line to distract the French. While the French waited for a head-on attack there, the Germans sent a much larger force secretly through Belgium, where the line was weak, and were well inside France within five days.
(e) No, I do not think so. The Maginot Line was strong, with bunkers, guns, fortresses, supplies and air conditioning, and it was designed to give the French "a supreme edge" against a head-on invasion. The Germans could not even take its main forts later. Their success came only because they avoided the strong part and went round it through weak Belgium.

**(a) A Respectable Woman.** Mrs. Baroda had never met Gouvernail because he had never visited the Baroda plantation, although her husband Gaston, his old college friend, often talked about him and invited him. Gouvernail was a busy newspaperman and always found a reason not to come. **(b) My Old Home.** Runtu had become like a lifeless wooden figure because of a life of poverty and suffering. He had many children to feed, and famine, heavy taxes, soldiers, bandits, officials and landlords all pressed on him until he was worn out. Lack of money and education, and the rigid class system, made him hopeless and humble, and he called the narrator "Master". The lively, bright boy of the narrator's memory had been crushed by hardship.
**Or — A Very Old Man with Enormous Wings.** When the old man landed in the courtyard, the weather was miserable. It had been raining for three days, the sea and sky had turned ash-grey, and the sand of the beach had become a soft, stinking mush. The house was full of dead crabs that Pelayo had to throw into the sea, and the newborn child had a fever all night. In this gloomy, rainy and sickening weather Pelayo found the winged old man lying face down in the mud.

**(c) A Day.** The poet knows the sunrise well because she watches it: the poem describes the sun rising "a ribbon at a time", with the hills and the sky changing colour and the world waking, like a child's day starting. Sunrise belongs to the day of childhood and is seen and enjoyed. The sunset, however, she cannot describe clearly. It stands for the end of the day and of life and is a mystery; at that time a child is led away like a flock by "a dominie in gray", and nothing more is known.

**(d) Knowledge and Wisdom.** Bertrand Russell says that wisdom needs more than knowledge. A sense of proportion is the ability to see things in their right size and place, to weigh all the important factors and not to give too much importance to one narrow interest or to one's own small concerns. A wise person therefore judges calmly, is not carried away by fanaticism or personal feeling, and decides what is good for human life as a whole. Without this sense of proportion, knowledge can be misused.

**(e) A Matter of Husbands.** The Earnest Young Woman blames the Famous Actress. She comes to the actress's drawing room and demands her husband back, saying that her husband Alfred is in love with the actress. She bases the blame on evidence: he sends the actress flowers twice a week and she has found an emotional letter of apology and kisses on his desk. She therefore believes the glamorous actress has stolen her husband.
**Or — The Bull.** The signs that Male, the king's bull, is dead are: the bull does not breathe, does not eat even when the king offers bananas, and does not move or wake up. Its ears have drooped and its tail has become loose, and its eyes are fixed and still. The cowherds and the bull doctor Laxminarayan can see these signs but are afraid to say it; finally the king himself notices them and declares, "He has died."

@s 3
**(a) On Libraries.** Oliver Sacks writes of two kinds of libraries: the traditional library full of printed books, and the digital library in which books are replaced by screens. The traditional library impresses me much more. As a boy Sacks was always found in the oak-panelled library of his family home, the quietest and loveliest room, and later in public libraries, where he could handle books, wander among shelves and find a surprising volume by chance. Such a library has an atmosphere, with a smell of paper, silence and companionship of other readers, that helps one to concentrate and to think deeply. It also keeps old books, notes and editions that a screen cannot reproduce. He was shocked when he found a public library with its shelves almost empty and its books thrown out after digitising, and few people protested. I agree with him: digital libraries are useful for quick searching, but a library of real books preserves memory, builds community and makes reading a pleasure.
**Or — Marriage as a Social Institution.** Stephen L. Nock distinguishes a married couple from a cohabiting couple by saying that marriage is a social institution, while cohabitation is only a private arrangement. A married couple is recognised by law, religion and custom; its rules and expectations come from outside the pair, and it has "soft boundaries" linking husband and wife with kin, friends and community, so it produces social capital, a network of trust and help. Marriage also exercises social control, for the society expects faithfulness, maturity and responsibility, and it is normally seen as lasting and leading to parenthood. A cohabiting couple lives together without this public recognition. Their relationship rests mainly on personal choice, has weaker legal rights and duties, has fewer expectations from relatives and society, and can end more easily. Therefore the married couple is bound by institutional norms and the cohabiting couple by private agreement only.

**(b) The Half-closed Eyes of the Buddha and the Slowly Sinking Sun.** The story presents two perspectives: the view of the foreign tourist, who looks at Nepal from outside and admires it, and the view of the Nepali guide, who knows the country from inside and shows its real life. Both reflect good qualities of Nepal. The tourist notices its natural and cultural beauty, the peaceful and sacred atmosphere of Swayambhu with the Buddha's half-closed eyes, and the warm hospitality of its people. The guide's perspective, the visit to the poor house of the paralysed child, shows other qualities: the family is poor, yet generous, kind and grateful, and the guide's small lie that the visitor is a doctor shows compassion and a wish to bring hope. The child's gaze, like the Buddha's calm eyes, suggests patience and gentleness. Together the two views show that Nepal's real greatness lies in its kind people, its spirituality and its humanity even in poverty, though it also reminds the reader of the need to care for the weak.

Dear Sujata,

I hope you are in the best of health and spirit. I am writing this letter to share a small concern. Last week I noticed that you bought several things without looking at them properly, and I felt I should give you a few friendly suggestions about shopping.

First, always check the packaging. Torn, leaking or swollen packets can mean that the goods are unsafe. Second, read the manufacturing and expiry dates carefully, for expired food and medicine can harm health. Third, go through the list of ingredients, especially if you are allergic to something, and avoid things with too many artificial colours and preservatives. Fourth, check the price label and compare it with the maximum retail price, and ask for a bill. Fifth, prefer shops and brands that are reliable and certified by the standards office. Finally, do not be carried away by big discounts and advertisements; buy only what you need.

These small habits will protect your health and your money. Please think about them next time you go out to shop.

With best wishes,

Your friend,
Anil

**Essay (about 300 words).**

**The Journey That Changed Me**

Every journey leaves some footprints, but one journey changed the way I look at life. It was a four-day trek to Ghandruk in Kaski, which our school organised after my SEE examination.

We started from Pokhara early in the morning, and by noon the bus left us at Nayapul. From there we walked through rice terraces, rhododendron forests and small villages. At first I was only excited by the photographs and jokes, and I carried a bag that was far too heavy. Within two hours my legs were aching and I wanted to go back. The steep stone steps seemed endless. Then an old woman carrying a huge load of firewood in a basket overtook me with a smile and said, "Slowly, daughter, but do not stop." Those words stayed with me.

In the evening we stayed in a village home. The family had little, but they gave us hot dal bhat, a warm place by the fire and endless stories. I learned about Gurung culture, their songs and their hospitality, and I realised how simple, happy lives can be without luxuries. Early on the third morning, when I stood on a hill and saw the sunrise turn the Annapurna range gold, I forgot all my pain. I felt small in front of the mountains, but also proud that I had reached there with my own feet.

On the way back I thought about the old woman and about my own complaints at home. The journey taught me patience, humility, teamwork and gratitude. It also taught me that a challenge looks smaller once we begin. Since then, whenever I face a difficult exam or problem, I remember the steep steps of Ghandruk and keep walking, slowly but without stopping.

| Item | Answer | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| (a) | Neither my father nor my mother **comes** in today's meeting. | With *neither ... nor* the verb agrees with the nearer subject, "mother", which is singular. (*came* is also acceptable if the meeting is over, but the agreement rule is what is tested.) |
| (b) | Dipti loved Dipesh but she married **to** Rahul. | "To" is the choice that fits the structure "be married to"; strictly, in standard English *married Rahul* needs no preposition. |
| (c) | I like **listening** to music. | The gerund follows *like* for something one enjoys in general; *to* is needed after *listening*. (*to listen to music* is also grammatical.) |
| (d) | He couldn't score good GPA in the exam **despite** his brilliance. | "Despite" (or *in spite of*) is a connective of contrast followed by a noun. |
| (e) | The hotel **where** we stayed was not very comfortable. (or: The hotel in which we stayed was not very comfortable.) | A relative adverb/pronoun joins the two sentences; "where" stands for "in which". |
| (f) | By the end of this decade, we **will have used** more electric vehicles than the fuel vehicles. | "By the end of ..." marks a point in the future before which the action will be completed: future perfect. |
| (g) | I think the problem is **far more complicated** than it seems. | The comparative of a long adjective is formed with *more*, and *far* strengthens it. |
| (h) | "Who is the stranger standing there?" "I don't know." He **may** be a tourist. | *May* expresses a guess or possibility when the speaker is not sure; *must* would show strong certainty. |
| (i) | The teacher asked the students **if (whether) they had submitted their assignment**. | A yes/no question becomes *asked ... if/whether*, with statement word order and the past perfect (*had submitted*); "your" changes to "their" and the question mark goes. |
| (j) | **A bar of chocolate was given to the children by Father.** (or: Children were given a bar of chocolate by Father.) | The object becomes the subject, with *was given*, and the agent follows *by*. |
