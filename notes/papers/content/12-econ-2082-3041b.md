**Differences between capitalist and socialist economy.**

| Basis | Capitalist economy | Socialist economy |
|---|---|---|
| Ownership of resources | Private ownership of land, capital and firms | Collective or state ownership of the main means of production |
| Motive of production | Maximum private profit | Social welfare and the needs of society |

| Examples | USA, UK | Former USSR, Cuba |

**Or: Division of labour (1 + 4).** Division of labour means dividing the production of a good into separate processes and assigning each process to a worker or group who does only that task. Adam Smith's pin factory is the standard example.

*Four advantages.*
1. **Higher productivity:** a worker repeats one task and becomes quick and skilled, so total output rises.
2. **Saving of time:** no time is lost moving from one job to another.
3. **Use of machines:** simple repeated tasks can be handed to machinery, which cuts the cost per unit.
4. **Right person in the right job:** each worker is placed where his or her ability is greatest, so skill and talent are used fully.

(Other advantages: lower cost and cheaper goods, better quality, and growth of large-scale production.) Its limits are monotony, loss of all-round skill and dependence of one stage on the others.

@s 13
**Classical theory of interest (saving-investment theory).** Developed by Marshall, Pigou, Taussig and others. Interest is the **price paid for the use of capital**, and it is determined by the **demand for and supply of loanable capital**, that is, by investment and saving.

*Supply of capital (saving).* People save because they must wait and abstain from consumption; interest is the reward for this waiting. Saving is an **increasing function of the rate of interest**: S = f(r). A higher rate induces more saving, so the supply curve slopes upwards.

*Demand for capital (investment).* Firms borrow to invest because capital is productive. A business invests only while the marginal productivity of capital exceeds the interest rate. Investment is a **decreasing function of the interest rate**: I = f(r), so the demand curve slopes downwards.

*Equilibrium.* Interest settles where saving equals investment (S = I). If the rate is above this level, saving exceeds investment and the rate falls; if below, investment exceeds saving and the rate rises.

@s 15
Equilibrium of a firm is where **MR = MC**, with MC cutting MR from below (second-order condition).

TR = 120Q − 5Q², so MR = dTR/dQ = 120 − 10Q.
TC = 60 + 7Q², so MC = dTC/dQ = 14Q.

Setting MR = MC:
$$120 - 10Q = 14Q$$
$$24Q = 120$$
$$Q = 5$$

Profit at Q = 5:
$$TR = 120(5) - 5(25) = 600 - 125 = 475$$
$$TC = 60 + 7(25) = 60 + 175 = 235$$
$$\text{Profit} = TR - TC = 475 - 235 = 240$$

**Check.** The slope of MC (14) is greater than the slope of MR (−10), so the second-order condition holds and profit is the maximum. Price at this output is TR ÷ Q = 475 ÷ 5 = Rs. 95. Equilibrium output is **5 units** and profit is **Rs. 240**.

@s 16
**Features of direct tax.** A direct tax is one whose burden is borne by the person on whom it is legally imposed. Income tax, property tax and corporate tax are examples.

1. **Impact and incidence on the same person:** the taxpayer cannot shift the burden to another person.
2. **Progressive in nature:** the rate rises as income or wealth rises, so the rich pay a larger share of income.
3. **Equitable:** because it follows ability to pay, it narrows income inequality and satisfies the canon of equity.
4. **Certain for the government:** the amount, time and manner of payment are known in advance, and revenue grows with national income, so it is elastic.
5. **Causes a feeling of pain:** since it is paid directly out of income, taxpayers feel the burden strongly and may try to evade it.
6. **Costly to administer:** assessment and collection need records, accounts and a large staff.

(Write any five.) Direct taxes are less inflationary than indirect taxes, because they reduce disposable income, and they raise civic awareness since taxpayers see what they pay.

@s 18
**Usefulness of foreign employment for Nepal.** Large numbers of Nepali youth work abroad (Gulf countries, Malaysia, India, Korea and others), and it helps Nepal in these ways:

1. **Remittance income:** workers send billions of rupees home every year. Remittance finances imports, raises foreign exchange reserves and supports the balance of payments.
2. **Reduces unemployment:** the domestic economy cannot absorb all job seekers. Foreign jobs relieve pressure on the labour market.
3. **Raises living standards and reduces poverty:** remittance is spent on food, health, education and housing, so household income and consumption rise and poverty falls.
4. **Skill, knowledge and technology transfer:** returnees bring new skills, work discipline and savings, which they can use to start businesses.
5. **Capital formation and financial inclusion:** remittance is channelled into land, housing, small enterprises and bank deposits, and it raises demand in local markets and for banking services.

The benefits are real but must be balanced against brain drain, shortage of labour in agriculture, dependence on remittance and the social cost of family separation.

**(III) Market structure (1 mark).** The firm can sell more only by cutting the price (AR falls from 12 to 5), and MR is below AR. This is the mark of **imperfect competition**. With a straight-line demand and MR falling twice as fast as AR, the structure is that of a **monopoly** (a single seller facing the whole market demand). Under perfect competition AR = MR would be a horizontal line. @s 21
**Free trade (1 mark).** Free trade is the international trade in which goods and services move between countries without tariffs, quotas or other government restrictions, so that comparative advantage and market forces decide what is traded.

**Why developing countries do not always gain (7 marks).** In theory free trade raises output and welfare, but in practice developing countries often gain less than developed ones, for these reasons.

1. **Unequal competition:** infant industries of developing countries cannot compete with large, efficient foreign firms, so local industry may collapse and jobs are lost.
2. **Dependence on primary goods:** exports are mostly agricultural products and raw materials whose prices are low and unstable, while imports are costly manufactured goods. The **terms of trade** turn against them.
3. **Trade deficit and drain of foreign exchange:** open markets raise imports faster than exports, so the deficit widens and reserves are drained.
4. **Protectionism by rich countries:** developed countries keep subsidies, tariffs and quality standards on farm and textile goods, so developing countries cannot enjoy equal market access.
5. **Dominance of multinationals:** foreign companies take profit and technology rents out of the country and may displace local firms.
6. **Narrow production base and low productivity:** weak infrastructure, small markets and shortage of capital and technology prevent exporters from using their comparative advantage.
7. **Loss of revenue and policy space:** tariff cuts reduce customs revenue, which is a major source of government income in countries like Nepal, and WTO rules limit protection and support policies.

**Conclusion.** Free trade benefits developing countries only when it is supported by industrial policy, export diversification, gradual (not sudden) opening, and fair rules in the WTO.

@s 22
**Causes of the increasing trade deficit in Nepal.** Trade deficit means imports exceed exports. Nepal's deficit is large and persistent: imports are several times larger than exports, and the gap is financed mainly by remittance. The causes are the following.

1. **Narrow export base and weak competitiveness:** exports are few (e.g. cardamom, tea, carpets, pashmina, ginger, jute and soybean products) and mostly low-value. Small output, low productivity and poor quality limit sales abroad.
2. **High dependence on imports:** Nepal imports petroleum, vehicles, machinery, electronics, gold, medicine, fertiliser and even food grains and vegetables that it could grow.
3. **Weak industrial and agricultural base:** industrial growth is slow and agriculture is still traditional, so import substitution has not occurred.
4. **Remittance-led consumption:** remittance raises household income and demand for imported consumer goods, and the high exchange value of the rupee (pegged to the Indian rupee) makes imports cheap and exports dear.
5. **Dependence on one trading partner:** India accounts for the largest share of trade, and the open border and informal trade weaken control.
6. **Geographical and infrastructure problems:** as a landlocked country with poor roads, high transport costs, power shortages and delays at borders, Nepal's exports are costly.
7. **Policy and trade-agreement factors:** liberal tariff reduction, WTO commitments, and limited use of preferential access for LDC exports; lack of quality certification.
8. **Political instability and weak implementation** of export promotion policies discourage investment.

**Remedies.** Diversify and add value to exports, promote import substitution in food and energy (hydropower and electric vehicles), improve trade facilitation and transit, bargain for better market access, and channel remittance into production.

**Or: Achievement of the Fifteenth Plan (2076/77 to 2080/81).** The plan carried the vision "Prosperous Nepal, Happy Nepali" and aimed at high growth, poverty reduction, infrastructure and social development. The figures below are given in general terms from the plan reviews; no printed table was provided in the question.

*Achievements.*
1. **Infrastructure and energy:** electricity generation capacity rose sharply (from about 1,100 MW to well over 2,500 MW), many roads, bridges and airports were extended, and electrification reached almost all households.
2. **Social sector:** school enrolment, immunisation and access to drinking water improved, and Nepal moved up the Human Development Index.
3. **Poverty and inclusion:** absolute poverty and multidimensional poverty declined, and social security allowances were extended.
4. **Federal structure:** the three-tier governments became operational and public services moved closer to citizens; digital payments and e-governance expanded.
5. **Graduation preparation:** Nepal met the criteria for graduation from LDC status, set for 2026.

*Shortfalls.* The COVID-19 pandemic and the 2022 external-sector crisis meant that economic growth stayed far below the target (about 9 per cent a year in the plan), investment and the share of industry did not reach targets, many mega-projects were delayed, and employment creation and a reduction of the trade deficit were weak.

**Conclusion.** The plan was successful in infrastructure, energy and social indicators but fell short in growth, investment, productive employment and trade balance, which the Sixteenth Plan seeks to correct.
