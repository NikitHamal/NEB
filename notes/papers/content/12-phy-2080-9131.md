@s 12
**(a)** *Simple harmonic motion* is the to-and-fro (oscillatory) motion of a body about a mean position in which the acceleration is directly proportional to the displacement from the mean position and is always directed towards it, $a = -\omega^{2}x$.

**(b)** Let a bob of mass $m$ hang from a light inextensible string of length $l$ and be displaced through a small angle $\theta$ from the vertical, so that its displacement along the arc is $y = l\theta$. Two forces act on the bob: the weight $mg$ and the tension $T$ in the string. Resolve the weight into $mg\cos\theta$ along the string (balanced by the tension) and $mg\sin\theta$ perpendicular to the string. The component $mg\sin\theta$ always acts towards the mean position, so it is the restoring force:
$$F = -mg\sin\theta$$
For small amplitude $\sin\theta\approx\theta = \dfrac{y}{l}$, therefore
$$F = -\frac{mg}{l}\,y$$
The restoring force is proportional to the displacement and opposite to it. Writing $F = ma$,
$$a = -\frac{g}{l}\,y = -\omega^{2}y,\qquad \omega^{2} = \frac{g}{l}$$
Thus the motion is simple harmonic and the time period is
$$T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{l}{g}}$$

**(c)** No. The time period $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$ contains only the length $l$ of the pendulum and the acceleration due to gravity $g$. The restoring force $mg\sin\theta$ is proportional to the mass, but so is the inertia $m$ that resists acceleration, and the mass cancels in $a = -\dfrac{g}{l}y$. A heavy and a light bob on strings of the same length therefore swing together with the same period.

@s 13
**(a)** *Viscosity* is the property of a fluid (liquid or gas) by which it opposes the relative motion between its adjacent layers. The tangential viscous force on a layer of area $A$ with velocity gradient $dv/dx$ is $F = \eta A\,\dfrac{dv}{dx}$, where $\eta$ is the coefficient of viscosity (SI unit Pa s).

**(b)** Consider a sphere of radius $r$ and density $\rho$ falling through a liquid of density $\sigma$ and coefficient of viscosity $\eta$. Three forces act on it:
- weight (downwards): $W = \dfrac43\pi r^{3}\rho g$,
- upthrust (upwards): $U = \dfrac43\pi r^{3}\sigma g$,
- viscous drag (upwards, Stokes' law): $F = 6\pi\eta r v$, which increases with the speed $v$.

At first the sphere accelerates, the drag grows with speed and finally the net force becomes zero. From then on the sphere moves with a constant speed, the *terminal velocity* $v_t$:
$$W = U + F \;\Rightarrow\; \frac43\pi r^{3}(\rho-\sigma)g = 6\pi\eta r v_t$$
$$v_t = \frac{2r^{2}(\rho-\sigma)g}{9\eta}$$
The terminal velocity is proportional to the square of the radius and to the difference in the densities and inversely proportional to the viscosity.

**(c)** The velocity increases from zero, the slope (acceleration) decreases steadily and the curve flattens at the terminal velocity.

@s 14
**(a)** Newton assumed that the pressure changes in a sound wave take place slowly, so that the temperature of the air stays constant (an isothermal process). Then the velocity of sound in a gas of pressure $P$ and density $\rho$ is
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{P}{\rho}}$$

**(b)** For air at N.T.P. Newton's formula gives $v\approx280\ \text{m s}^{-1}$, but the measured value is about $332\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Laplace pointed out that compressions and rarefactions follow each other so rapidly, and that air is a poor conductor of heat, that there is no time for heat to flow between the compressed and rarefied parts. The changes are therefore *adiabatic*, not isothermal, and the pressure $P$ must be replaced by the adiabatic elasticity $\gamma P$.

**(c)** The velocity of a longitudinal wave in a medium of bulk modulus $K$ and density $\rho$ is $v = \sqrt{K/\rho}$. For an adiabatic change of a gas
$$PV^{\gamma} = \text{constant}$$
Differentiating, $V^{\gamma}\,dP + \gamma PV^{\gamma-1}\,dV = 0$, so
$$K_{ad} = -\frac{dP}{dV/V} = \gamma P$$
Substituting $K = \gamma P$ in $v = \sqrt{K/\rho}$ gives Laplace's formula
$$v = \sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}}$$
where $\gamma = C_p/C_v$ (1.4 for air). Check at N.T.P. ($P = 1.013\times10^{5}$ Pa, $\rho = 1.293\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$): Newton $v=\sqrt{78\,350}=280$ m/s; Laplace $v=\sqrt{1.4\times78\,350}=331$ m/s, in agreement with the experiment.

@s 15
**(a)** An *organ pipe* is a cylindrical pipe, open at both ends (open pipe) or closed at one end (closed pipe), in which an air column is set into vibration by blowing air across or into one end to produce a musical sound. Stationary waves form in the air column.

**(b)** In a closed pipe of length $L$ the closed end is always a displacement *node* (the air cannot move) and the open end is an *antinode*. The air column can therefore vibrate only in modes in which a node is at the closed end and an antinode at the open end.
- *Fundamental mode:* one node and one antinode, $L = \dfrac{\lambda_1}{4}$, so $\lambda_1 = 4L$ and $f_1 = \dfrac{v}{4L}$.
- *First overtone (third harmonic):* $L = \dfrac{3\lambda_2}{4}$, $f_2 = \dfrac{3v}{4L} = 3f_1$.
- *Second overtone (fifth harmonic):* $L = \dfrac{5\lambda_3}{4}$, $f_3 = \dfrac{5v}{4L} = 5f_1$.

In general $L = (2n+1)\dfrac{\lambda}{4}$ and $f_n = (2n+1)\dfrac{v}{4L}$, $n = 0,1,2,\dots$ The frequencies are in the ratio $1:3:5:\dots$, so a closed pipe produces only the odd harmonics.

**(c)** The antinode at the open end of an organ pipe does not form exactly at the open end but slightly outside it, because the air just outside the mouth also takes part in the vibration. The small length $e$ by which the effective length of the pipe exceeds its actual length is the *end correction*; $e\approx0.6\,r$ (about $0.3\,d$) for a pipe of radius $r$. So the effective length is $L+e$ for a closed pipe, $f = \dfrac{v}{4(L+e)}$, and $L+2e$ for an open pipe.

@s 16
**(a)** *Interference of light* is the phenomenon of the redistribution of light energy in space when two coherent light waves of the same frequency, travelling in nearly the same direction, superpose. At some points the waves meet in phase and give maximum intensity (bright fringes, constructive interference) and at others they meet in opposite phase and give minimum intensity (dark fringes, destructive interference).

**(b)** *Brewster's law:* when unpolarized light is incident on the surface of a transparent medium at a particular angle of incidence $\theta_p$, called the *polarizing angle* (Brewster angle), the reflected light is completely plane polarized, with its vibrations perpendicular to the plane of incidence. At this angle the reflected and refracted rays are at $90^{\circ}$ to each other. The tangent of the polarizing angle is equal to the refractive index $\mu$ of the medium:
$$\mu = \tan\theta_p$$
Proof: Snell's law gives $\mu = \dfrac{\sin\theta_p}{\sin r}$ and with $\theta_p + r = 90^{\circ}$, $\sin r = \cos\theta_p$, so $\mu = \tan\theta_p$. The refractive index depends on wavelength, so the polarizing angle is different for different colours.

**(c)** From Brewster's law
$$\mu = \tan\theta_p = \tan30^{\circ} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} = 0.577$$
*Remark:* a refractive index below 1 is not possible for ordinary transparent materials (it would mean light travelling faster in the material than in air). The printed angle may be a misprint for $60^{\circ}$, which gives the usual value $\mu = \tan60^{\circ} = 1.73$. The question as printed has the answer 0.577.

@s 17
**(a)** The meter bridge is based on the principle of the *Wheatstone bridge*: when the bridge is balanced, no current flows through the galvanometer and the resistances in the four arms satisfy
$$\frac{P}{Q} = \frac{R}{S}$$
In the meter bridge the ratio $P:Q$ is replaced by the ratio of the lengths of a uniform wire, $\dfrac{l_1}{l_2}$.

**(b)** A meter bridge consists of a uniform wire $AC$ of length 1 m stretched along a metre scale and fixed to thick copper strips that make two gaps. A known resistance $R$ (from a resistance box) is put in the left gap and the wire of unknown resistance $S$ is put in the right gap. A cell with a key and a rheostat is connected across $A$ and $C$ and a galvanometer $G$ with a protective high resistance is connected between the middle point $B$ of the strips and a sliding contact (jockey) $J$.

*Procedure.* Take out a suitable resistance $R$, close the key and slide the jockey along the wire until the galvanometer shows zero deflection. Let $AJ = l_1$ cm and $JC = (100 - l_1)$ cm. The resistance of the wire is proportional to its length, so
$$\frac{R}{S} = \frac{l_1\rho/A}{(100-l_1)\rho/A}\;\Rightarrow\; S = R\,\frac{100-l_1}{l_1}$$
Repeat for several values of $R$ and take the mean. To remove end errors the box and the unknown resistance are interchanged and the mean is taken; the balance point should be near the middle of the wire ($l_1\approx50$ cm), where the sensitivity is greatest.

**(c)** The *balanced condition* is the condition of the bridge in which the potentials at the two junctions between which the galvanometer is connected are equal, so no current flows through the galvanometer and it shows zero deflection. Then the product of the resistances in opposite arms are equal: $\dfrac{P}{Q} = \dfrac{R}{S}$, i.e. $\dfrac{R}{S} = \dfrac{l_1}{l_2}$. The value of the bridge does not depend on the emf of the cell or the resistance of the galvanometer.

**Or (a)** The *Lorentz force* is the total electromagnetic force on a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ in a region having both an electric field $\vec E$ and a magnetic field $\vec B$:
$$\vec F = q\left(\vec E + \vec v\times\vec B\right)$$
The magnetic part has magnitude $qvB\sin\theta$ and is perpendicular to both $\vec v$ and $\vec B$, so it does no work; the electric part $q\vec E$ acts along the field.

**Or (b)** *Biot-Savart law:* the magnetic field $d\vec B$ at a point $P$ due to a small current element $I\,d\vec l$ at distance $r$ is proportional to the current $I$, the length $dl$ and $\sin\theta$ ($\theta$ = angle between $d\vec l$ and the line joining the element to $P$) and inversely proportional to $r^{2}$:
$$dB = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,dl\sin\theta}{r^{2}}$$
The direction is given by the right-hand screw rule.

*Field at the centre of a circular coil.* Let the coil have radius $a$ and $N$ turns and carry current $I$. Every element $d\vec l$ is perpendicular to the radius joining it to the centre, so $\theta = 90^{\circ}$ and $r = a$. Each element contributes $dB = \dfrac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\dfrac{I\,dl}{a^{2}}$ in the same direction (along the axis). Summing over the circumference $\oint dl = 2\pi a$:
$$B = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I}{a^{2}}(2\pi a)\times N = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2a}$$
The direction is along the axis, given by the right-hand rule (fingers along the current, thumb along $\vec B$).

**(a)** In a series LCR circuit the inductive reactance $X_L = \omega L$ rises with the frequency while the capacitive reactance $X_C = \dfrac{1}{\omega C}$ falls. **(a)** The *photoelectric effect* is the emission of electrons from the surface of a metal when light (or other electromagnetic radiation) of frequency greater than a certain minimum (the threshold frequency $f_0$) falls on it. The emitted electrons are called photoelectrons.

**(b)** The photoelectrons leave the metal with kinetic energies up to a maximum $K_{max}$. When the anode is made negative with respect to the cathode the current falls; at the *stopping potential* $V_0$ even the fastest photoelectrons are just stopped and the photocurrent becomes zero. The work done by the retarding field equals the maximum kinetic energy:
$$K_{max} = eV_0 = \tfrac12 mv_{max}^{2}$$
With Einstein's equation $K_{max} = hf - \phi$ this gives $V_0 = \dfrac{h}{e}f - \dfrac{\phi}{e}$, so the stopping potential is directly proportional to the maximum kinetic energy and grows linearly with the frequency; it does not depend on the intensity.

**(c)** *Millikan's experiment (1916).* A clean surface of an alkali metal (for example sodium or lithium) is the photocathode in an evacuated tube. Its surface is cleaned by a knife inside the vacuum so that it is free from oxide. Monochromatic light of a known frequency $f$ falls on it, and the retarding (negative) voltage on the collector is raised until the photocurrent just becomes zero. This reading is the stopping potential $V_0$. The measurement is repeated for several frequencies (obtained with a monochromator) and $V_0$ is plotted against $f$.

By Einstein's equation $eV_0 = hf - \phi$ the graph is a straight line which cuts the frequency axis at the threshold frequency $f_0$. Its slope is
$$\text{slope} = \frac{V_0}{f - f_0} = \frac{h}{e}\;\Rightarrow\; h = e\times\text{slope}$$
Using the known charge $e$ Millikan obtained $h = 6.57\times10^{-34}$ J s, close to the value found from black-body radiation. The experiment confirmed Einstein's photoelectric equation, the quantum nature of light and the existence of a threshold frequency, and gave the work function from the intercept ($\phi = hf_0$).

**Or (c)** The quality of X-rays means their penetrating power (hardness or softness).
- *Hardness (quality)* is controlled by the *accelerating voltage* between the filament and the target. A higher voltage gives faster electrons and a shorter $\lambda_{min} = hc/eV$, so harder X-rays that penetrate more.
- *Intensity (quantity)* is controlled by the *filament current*. A larger heating current raises the filament temperature, releases more electrons per second and so more X-ray photons per second, without changing the hardness.
Soft X-rays (low voltage) are used for photographing hands or thin objects, hard X-rays for bones, industrial radiography and cancer therapy.

**Or (d)** $f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda}$, so for the same speed $c$ the frequency is inversely proportional to the wavelength. With $\lambda_1 = 10^{-10}$ m and $\lambda_2 = 10\times10^{-10} = 10^{-9}$ m:
$$\frac{f_1}{f_2} = \frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_1} = \frac{10^{-9}}{10^{-10}} = 10$$
So $f_1 : f_2 = 10 : 1$ ($f_1 = 3\times10^{18}$ Hz and $f_2 = 3\times10^{17}$ Hz). The shorter wavelength X-rays have ten times the frequency, and therefore ten times the photon energy.
