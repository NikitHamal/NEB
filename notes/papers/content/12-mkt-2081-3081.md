### Group A

@s 1
The **marketing concept** is the philosophy that an organisation can achieve its goals by identifying the needs and wants of its target customers and satisfying them better than competitors, with profit through customer satisfaction.

@s 2
Two examples of shopping products: **clothes** and **mobile phones** (also shoes, furniture and household appliances).

@s 3
For medicine I would use **informative labelling** (name, ingredients, dose, batch number, manufacturing and expiry dates, price) together with **warning/grade labelling** about side effects and storage, as the law requires.

@s 4
**Price** is the amount of money, or other consideration, that a customer pays to obtain a product or service; it is the value of the product expressed in money.

@s 5
A **wholesaler** buys goods in bulk from producers, stores them, breaks the bulk into smaller lots and sells them to retailers and other businesses, also giving credit, transport and market information.

@s 6
Two public relations tools: **press releases** (news stories) and **events or sponsorships**, such as seminars, community programmes and social service activities.

@s 7
Two features of internet marketing: it is **interactive and two-way**, with instant response from customers, and it is **available globally at all hours** at a relatively low cost. It is also measurable and personalised.

@s 8
**Morality in marketing** means the standards of right and wrong, such as honesty, fairness and respect for customers, that guide the behaviour of marketers in their dealings.

@s 9
Two obligations toward the public: to supply **safe goods of the promised quality at fair prices**, and to **protect the environment** and avoid false advertising, hoarding and black-marketing.

@s 10
Two features of salesmanship: it is a **personal, face-to-face** process of persuasion, and it aims to **create and satisfy customer needs** profitably, leading to a lasting relationship.

@s 11
Two roles of salesmanship toward consumers: it **informs and guides** them about the available goods and their use, and it **helps them to choose** the product suited to their needs and budget, with after-sale service.

### Group B

@s 12
**Convenience products** are low-priced goods bought frequently, immediately and with little effort, such as soap, salt, newspapers and soft drinks. The marketing considerations are:
1. **Product.** The goods should be of consistent quality, in small, convenient packs, with an attractive package and a well-known brand.
2. **Price.** Low, because buyers compare little and are price-sensitive; margins are small but volume is large.
3. **Place.** **Intensive distribution** through every possible outlet, such as shops, kiosks and vending machines, so that the product is always at hand.
4. **Promotion.** Mass advertising to create brand awareness and repeat purchase, plus displays at the point of sale and sales promotion by the producer.
5. **Inventory.** Adequate stock to avoid stock-outs, since buyers will quickly switch to a substitute.

Types are staples (rice), impulse goods (chocolate) and emergency goods (umbrella in rain).

@s 13
**Importance of pricing to customers:**
1. **Indicator of value and quality.** Customers often judge quality by the price when other information is missing.
2. **Basis of purchase decision.** The price decides whether the goods are affordable, so buyers compare prices to get the best value.
3. **Allocation of limited income.** It guides customers on how to spend limited income among various goods.
4. **Fair price protection.** A competitive price protects customers from exploitation and gives them choice.
5. **Lower prices through competition.** Price competition brings discounts, offers and better quality.
6. **Status and satisfaction.** A price consistent with the benefit gives satisfaction, and a higher price signals prestige in luxury goods.
7. **Planning of spending** and control of inflation; stable prices let customers plan their budgets.

@s 14

1. **Producer to consumer** (zero level): direct sale by own shop, door-to-door, mail or online. 2. **Producer, retailer, consumer** (one level): used for large retailers, such as supermarkets and showrooms. 3. **Producer, wholesaler, retailer, consumer** (two level): the usual channel for convenience goods such as soap and food. 4. **Producer, agent, retailer, consumer** (agent channel): used when the market is wide and the producer is small, so an agent sells to retailers in distant areas.

@s 15
**Sales promotion** means short-term incentives such as samples, coupons, discounts, contests and displays that encourage immediate purchase. Its importance from the **producer's point of view**:
1. **Increases sales quickly** and clears excess stock.
2. **Introduces new products** by trial through free samples.
3. **Attracts new customers** and encourages brand switching from competitors.
4. **Retains loyal customers** through rewards and repeat purchase offers.
5. **Supports the dealers.** Trade offers motivate wholesalers and retailers to stock and push the product.
6. **Counters competition** by an immediate response to rivals' offers.
7. **Supports advertising and personal selling,** and gives an effect that can be measured.
8. **Raises sales in off-season** and builds goodwill during festivals.

@s 16
**Publicity** is non-paid communication of news about the company in the mass media. To maintain the company image I would use:
1. **News releases and press conferences** announcing new products, achievements and CSR activities.
2. **Feature stories and interviews** of the top managers in newspapers, radio, television and magazines.
3. **Company events and sponsorship:** sports, cultural events, trade fairs and exhibitions.
4. **Social service and CSR programmes:** donation, tree plantation and scholarships, which create goodwill.
5. **Publications and audio-visual materials:** annual reports, newsletters, brochures and a website with social media pages.
6. **Speeches and lobbying** by management at seminars and with government.
7. **Crisis communication:** a quick and honest statement when a problem arises.

Publicity is credible and low in cost, but the company cannot control what the media report.

@s 17
**Television marketing** uses television to promote and sell goods through commercials, shopping channels and sponsored programmes. The **problems in Nepal:**
1. **High cost.** Airtime and production of commercials are expensive for small firms.
2. **Limited reach.** Many rural and hill households have no television or electricity, or rely on cable that is not accessible.
3. **Clutter and short attention.** Many advertisements run together and viewers skip them.
4. **Lack of skilled manpower and creativity** for producing quality advertisements, and a shortage of research on viewers.
5. **Weak measurement.** Reliable ratings (TRP) data are lacking, so firms cannot judge the results.
6. **Competition from the internet and mobile media,** which take away young viewers.
7. **Regulation and ethics.** Rules about content, language and foreign channels' advertising, and the absence of a strong self-regulation system.
8. **Language and cultural diversity,** making one message fit all groups difficult.

**Or (Q17). Service marketing.** Yes, it is. In the 21st century services such as banking, insurance, telecom, education, health, tourism, IT and transport make a rising share of GDP and jobs. Reasons: (i) **rising income** and urban life increase the demand for convenience, education and health services; (ii) **technology** such as mobile banking and online delivery makes services easily available; (iii) **tourism and remittance-led growth** have expanded hotels, travel and finance; (iv) **competition** forces firms to differentiate through service quality, which has made marketing of services important; (v) **employment** and entrepreneurship in services have grown; and (vi) globalisation allows the **export of services**, for example IT outsourcing. The unique features of services (intangibility, inseparability, variability, perishability) call for special marketing, which is why service marketing is spreading.

@s 18
**Ethical decision making** is choosing among alternatives on the basis of what is right, fair and good for all stakeholders. The influencing factors are:
1. **Personal values and moral development** of the decision maker, such as honesty and integrity.
2. **Organisational culture and top management behaviour,** the code of conduct, rewards and punishment.
3. **Peer and group pressure,** since colleagues' behaviour sets norms.
4. **Opportunity.** Weak control and poor supervision give the chance to act unethically.
5. **Legal and regulatory environment.** Laws and consumer protection bodies.
6. **Social and cultural norms,** religion and public opinion.
7. **Competitive and financial pressure,** such as sales targets and the fear of losing the market.
8. **Nature of the issue** (the seriousness of harm) and the influence of media and NGOs.

**Or (Q18). Ethical decision-making.** It is the process of evaluating and choosing a course of action that is morally right. Steps: **recognise the ethical issue**; **collect facts** and identify the stakeholders; **list the alternatives**; **evaluate them** against law, company code, fairness (justice), consequences for all (utilitarian) and rights; **decide and act**; and **review the outcome**. A simple test is whether the decision is legal, fair to all, and one the decision maker would be happy to see published. Ethical decisions build trust, loyalty and long-term profit.

@s 19
**Salesmanship as an art.** An art is the skilful use of knowledge to achieve a result through personal ability. Selling needs **creativity, communication, persuasion and understanding of human psychology**, which are developed by practice and talent. Two salespersons with the same product achieve different results, because of their style, so salesmanship is an art.

**Salesmanship as a profession.** It needs **specialised knowledge** of the product and customer, which is learnt in training; has a **code of conduct** (honesty and fair dealing); has a **service motive** of solving the customer's problem; offers a **career and reward**; and has growing professional associations and education. However, it has no compulsory licence and no statutory body, so it is regarded as an **emerging profession**.

Hence salesmanship is both an **art** (personal skill) and a **developing profession** (organised knowledge and ethics).

### Group C

@s 20
**Product.** A product is anything that can be offered to a market to satisfy a need or want, including physical goods, services, ideas, persons and places. It is more than the physical object: it includes the brand, package, quality, design, guarantee and after-sale service.

**Levels of product (Kotler's five levels).**
1. **Core benefit.** The basic need that the customer is really buying. A hotel guest buys "rest and sleep"; a buyer of a mobile phone buys "communication".
2. **Basic (generic) product.** The basic form that delivers the core benefit: for the hotel, a bed, bathroom and room; for the phone, a handset that can call.
3. **Expected product.** The set of attributes and conditions that buyers normally expect: a clean bed, fresh towels and quiet in the hotel; battery, signal and a working charger for the phone.
4. **Augmented product.** The additional services and benefits that go beyond expectation and distinguish the offer from competitors: free Wi-Fi, breakfast and airport pick-up at the hotel; warranty, free software update and home delivery for the phone.
5. **Potential product.** All the possible additions and transformations the product may undergo in the future to keep customers: a smart hotel room with voice control, or a phone with new features through upgrades.

A marketer must study all five levels, because competition today is largely at the augmented and potential levels. A firm that only sells the generic product competes on price alone; a firm that adds benefits builds loyalty and a higher price.

@s 21
**Physical distribution** (marketing logistics) is the planning, implementing and controlling of the physical flow of goods from the point of production to the point of use so as to meet customer needs at a profit. It makes the product available at the right place, in the right quantity, at the right time and at the least cost.

**Components of physical distribution:**
1. **Order processing.** Receiving, checking, recording and filling the customer's order, and preparing invoices and delivery documents. Fast and accurate processing shortens the delivery cycle.
2. **Transportation.** Moving goods by road, rail, air, water or cable. The mode is chosen by cost, speed, safety, reliability and the nature of the goods. It is usually the largest part of the distribution cost.
3. **Warehousing (storage).** Holding goods in stock between production and sale, to balance supply and demand, protect the goods and allow quick delivery.
4. **Inventory control.** Deciding how much to keep: too little causes stock-outs and lost sales, and too much blocks capital. Tools include economic order quantity and reorder level.
5. **Materials handling.** The movement of goods within the warehouse and in loading and unloading, by pallets, forklifts and conveyors, which should be quick and without damage.
6. **Packaging and protective packing.** Packing for safe transport and easy handling (outer cartons, crates).
7. **Customer service and communication.** Delivery on time, handling of complaints and information about the order status.

The components are interdependent. The aim is to balance the **total cost** of distribution against the **level of service** to customers.

**Or (Q21). Warehousing.** **Warehousing** is the activity of storing goods in a planned place, from the time of production or purchase until they are sold. It is one of the most important components of the physical distribution system because:
1. **It balances production and consumption.** Seasonal crops (paddy, apples) are produced at one time but consumed all through the year, and factory output is steady while demand fluctuates at festivals; storage bridges the gap.
2. **It makes goods available at the right time and place,** so a store near the market delivers fast and reduces stock-outs.
3. **It protects the goods** against rain, theft, rats, fire and spoilage, and cold storage preserves perishables such as milk, meat, vegetables and medicines.
4. **It reduces transport cost,** because the goods can be carried in bulk to the warehouse and then divided into small loads for local delivery.
5. **It stabilises prices** by preventing shortages and gluts, and the farmer can avoid distress sale.
6. **It gives finance and value-added services:** warehouse receipts are accepted by banks for loans, and the warehouse does grading, sorting, packing and labelling.
7. **It supports inventory control and market information** about the demand.
8. **It supports production and trade** by making raw material and spare parts available.

Without warehousing the other components, transport, inventory and customer service, cannot work efficiently, so the statement is justified.

@s 22
**Agriculture marketing** is the whole set of activities involved in moving agricultural products from the farm to the consumer, including planning production, assembling, grading, standardisation, packing, storage, transport, processing, financing, price determination and selling. It also covers the supply of farm inputs such as seeds and fertilizer.

**Prospects of agriculture marketing in Nepal:**
1. **Agricultural base.** More than half of the people depend on agriculture, which gives a large supply of produce and a large market of farmers themselves.
2. **Growing domestic demand.** Urbanisation, rising income, hotels, restaurants and the food-processing industry increase the demand for fresh and processed food.
3. **Export potential** in organic and niche products such as tea, coffee, cardamom, ginger, honey, medicinal herbs, vegetable seeds and fruits (apple, kiwi), and open markets in India, China and others.
4. **Climatic and geographical diversity,** which allows many crops, off-season vegetables and flowers.
5. **Government and institutional support.** Subsidy, agriculture policy and development strategy, collection centres, cold storage, wholesale markets, cooperatives, agricultural banks and the programmes of the three tiers of government.
6. **Agro-processing and commercialisation** such as dairy, juice, spices and packaged foods, which add value and jobs.
7. **Infrastructure and technology.** Expansion of roads, electricity, mobile and the internet and online marketing of farm produce.
8. **Tourism and organic farming** increase the demand for local, healthy and branded food.

**To realise the prospects** the country must improve storage, grading and standardisation, market information, credit and price support, and remove the middlemen's dominance. With these, agriculture marketing can raise farm income and reduce the import of food.
