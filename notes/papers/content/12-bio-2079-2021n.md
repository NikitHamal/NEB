### Section I: Botany — Group B

@s 6
**Why meristem is called juvenile tissue (1 mark).** A meristem consists of young, thin-walled, undifferentiated cells with dense cytoplasm, a large nucleus and no vacuoles, which keep on dividing throughout the life of the plant just as the cells of an embryo do. Because they stay in this young "embryonic" condition and give rise to all the permanent tissues, the meristem is called juvenile (embryonic) tissue.

**Types of meristem on the basis of position (3 marks).**
1. **Apical meristem:** it lies at the tips of the root and the shoot (and their branches). Its cells divide to increase the **length** of the plant (primary growth) and form the primary tissues. It also gives rise to leaves and flowers at the shoot apex, and the root cap is formed at the root apex.
2. **Intercalary meristem:** it is a part of the apical meristem that is left behind between regions of permanent tissue, at the base of the internodes (as in grasses and bamboo), at the base of leaves (mint) or near the nodes. It produces rapid elongation of the internode and helps grasses to regrow after they are cut or grazed.
3. **Lateral meristem:** it lies along the sides of the stem and root, parallel to the surface of the organ. Vascular cambium and cork cambium (phellogen) are examples. Its cells divide to increase the **girth** (secondary growth), forming secondary xylem, secondary phloem and cork.

**Differences between transpiration and guttation.**

| Transpiration | Guttation |
|---|---|
| loss of water as **vapour** from aerial parts | loss of water as **liquid drops** |
| occurs mainly through stomata (also cuticle and lenticels) | occurs through hydathodes at leaf margins and tips |
| takes place mostly in the daytime, in sunlight | takes place mainly at night or in the early morning, when humidity is high |
| caused by the pulling force of evaporation (transpiration pull) | caused by root pressure |
| the water is pure (nearly) | the water contains dissolved sugars, salts and organic matter |
| a physical process controlled by guard cells | not controlled by guard cells; hydathodes stay open |
| helps in the ascent of sap and cooling of the plant | removes excess water and salts when transpiration is low |
| e.g. most plants in day-time | e.g. grasses, tomato, *Colocasia* |

**Or — Significance of polyploidy.** Polyploidy is the condition of having more than two complete sets of chromosomes (3n, 4n, 6n); it is induced artificially by colchicine and occurs naturally.
1. **Increase in size (gigantism):** polyploid plants have bigger cells, flowers, fruits and leaves, for example tetraploid tomato, apple and grape and the large flowers of ornamentals.
2. **Seedless fruits:** triploid plants are sterile, so the fruits are seedless, for example triploid banana (3n = 33) and triploid watermelon.
3. **Formation of new species and crop improvement:** *Triticale* (wheat × rye) and *Raphanobrassica* (radish × cabbage) are fertile allopolyploids, and bread wheat is hexaploid (6n = 42); polyploids are often hardier, with more vigour and better yield.
4. **Restoring fertility of hybrids:** a sterile hybrid becomes fertile when its chromosome number is doubled, for example *Primula kewensis*.
5. **Evolution:** about half of flowering plant species are polyploids, so polyploidy is a major source of speciation.

@s 8
**Development pattern of the dicot embryo (2 marks).** The development of the embryo (embryogeny) is by repeated divisions of the zygote inside the embryo sac, in the sequence:
1. The zygote rests for a while and then divides **transversely** into a large basal cell (toward the micropyle) and a small terminal cell (toward the chalaza).
2. The **basal cell** divides transversely many times to form a row of 6 to 10 cells, the **suspensor**, which pushes the embryo into the nutritive endosperm. Its first cell, next to the micropyle, is swollen and anchors the embryo (**haustorium**), and the cell next to the embryo forms the radicle tip (hypophysis).
3. The **terminal cell** divides by two vertical and one transverse divisions to form an octant and then a spherical mass of cells, the **globular** embryo.
4. Two cotyledons start to grow on the sides, so the embryo becomes **heart-shaped**; the cotyledons then elongate into the **torpedo stage**, and the plumule forms between them.
5. At maturity the embryo is curved and has two cotyledons (food stores), the plumule, the embryonal axis (epicotyl and hypocotyl) and the radicle.

**Salient features and labelled diagrams (2 marks).** The embryo of a dicot has an embryonal axis with two cotyledons attached; the part above the cotyledons is the epicotyl ending in the plumule (future shoot), and the part below is the hypocotyl ending in the radicle (future root) protected by a root cap.

@s 9
**Justification (1 mark).** Hybridisation is the crossing of two genetically different parents to combine their useful characters (disease resistance, yield, quality) in the offspring. The F₁ hybrid often shows **hybrid vigour (heterosis)**: it is better than both parents in yield, size and vigour. Hybrid maize, hybrid rice, tomato and cotton are examples, so hybridisation is a modern, planned breeding method.

**Steps of the procedure (3 marks).**
1. **Selection of parents:** two parents with the desired characters are chosen; they are usually pure lines (homozygous).
2. **Emasculation:** in the flower of the female parent the anthers are removed with forceps before they burst, so that self-pollination cannot occur. It is done in a bisexual flower in the bud stage.
3. **Bagging:** the emasculated flower is covered with a butter-paper or polythene bag to stop contamination by unwanted pollen.
4. **Tagging:** a label is attached giving the parents, the date of emasculation and the date of pollination.
5. **Pollination (dusting):** when the stigma is receptive, the ripe pollen of the male parent is dusted on it, and the flower is bagged again.
6. **Harvesting and raising the F₁:** the seeds of the pollinated flowers are collected separately and sown; the F₁ hybrids are evaluated, and desirable plants are selected, tested in several seasons and multiplied for release as a new variety.

@s 10
**Importance of mutation in evolution (2 marks).**
1. Mutation is the **source of new alleles**, and so of the genetic variation among individuals; without it there would be nothing for natural selection to act on.
2. Beneficial mutations are preserved and spread by natural selection, so populations **adapt** to a changing environment and new species arise over many generations. Example: the sickle-cell allele (HbS) protects heterozygotes from malaria, and industrial melanism in the peppered moth (*Biston betularia*) arose from a mutant dark allele that spread in polluted areas.

**How gene mutation occurs (2 marks).** A gene mutation (point mutation) is a change in the sequence of nucleotides within a gene. It arises when (a) **mistakes occur during DNA replication** (for example a base is paired wrongly because of a tautomeric shift, and is not repaired by proof-reading), or (b) the DNA is damaged by **mutagens**: physical agents (UV light, X-rays, γ-rays) and chemicals (nitrous acid, base analogues like 5-bromouracil, acridine dyes, mustard gas). A base may be substituted, or one or more bases may be inserted or deleted. As a result the codons of the mRNA, and often the amino-acid sequence of the protein, are changed.

**Types of gene mutation (4 marks).**
1. **Substitution (base-pair substitution):** one base is replaced by another.
 - *Transition:* a purine replaced by a purine (A ⇄ G) or a pyrimidine by a pyrimidine (C ⇄ T).
 - *Transversion:* a purine replaced by a pyrimidine or the reverse.
 - Its effect on the protein may be a **silent** mutation (new codon codes for the same amino acid), a **missense** mutation (a different amino acid, for example GAG → GUG replaces glutamic acid by valine in sickle-cell haemoglobin) or a **nonsense** mutation (the codon becomes a stop codon, UAA, UAG or UGA, and the protein ends early).
2. **Insertion:** one or more extra bases are added to the gene.
3. **Deletion:** one or more bases are lost from the gene.
 - Insertion or deletion of one or two bases (not a multiple of three) shifts the reading frame of every codon after that point (a **frameshift mutation**) and usually gives a nonfunctional protein.

Illustration with a sentence read in three-letter words (like codons), THE CAT SAW THE DOG:

| Mutation | Result |
|---|---|
| substitution | THE CAT **RAW** THE DOG (one letter changed) |
| insertion (B added after THE) | THE BCA TSA WTH EDO G (every later word changes: frameshift) |
| deletion (C of CAT lost) | THE ATS AWT HED OG (every later word changes: frameshift) |

**Or — Why *Drosophila melanogaster* is used in genetics (3 marks).**
1. It is **small** (about 2 mm) and cheap to keep in large numbers in a culture bottle on a banana or cornmeal-agar medium.
2. It has a **short life cycle** (egg to adult in about 10 to 14 days at 25 °C), so many generations can be studied in a short time.
3. A female is **very prolific**: it lays several hundred eggs, so statistically significant numbers of progeny are obtained from one cross.
4. Males and females are **easily distinguished** (sexual dimorphism: the male is smaller, with a dark rounded abdomen and sex combs).
5. It has only **four pairs of chromosomes** (2n = 8), many easily seen **mutant traits** (eye colour, wing shape, body colour), and giant polytene chromosomes in the salivary glands.

**Criss-cross inheritance (5 marks).** In a sex-linked trait the gene is on the X chromosome; a male gets his X from his mother and passes it only to his daughters, so the trait goes from a father to his grandson through a daughter. This is called criss-cross inheritance. T. H. Morgan (1910) showed it with the white-eye gene (w, recessive) of *Drosophila*; red eye (w⁺) is dominant.

**Cross 1: white-eyed male × homozygous red-eyed female.**

| | gamete Xʷ | gamete Y |
|---|---|---|
| X⁺ | X⁺Xʷ (red female) | X⁺Y (red male) |
| X⁺ | X⁺Xʷ (red female) | X⁺Y (red male) |

F₁: all red-eyed (the females are carriers). When the F₁ flies are inter-bred, the F₂ has red females, red males, white males in the ratio 2 : 1 : 1 (red-eyed : white-eyed = 3 : 1 and all the white-eyed flies are males).

**Cross 2 (reciprocal): red-eyed male × white-eyed female.**

| | X⁺ | Y |
|---|---|---|
| Xʷ | X⁺Xʷ (red female) | XʷY (white male) |
| Xʷ | X⁺Xʷ (red female) | XʷY (white male) |

F₁: **all daughters red-eyed, all sons white-eyed**: the trait of the mother appears in the sons and the trait of the father in the daughters. The F₁ inter-bred gives F₂ with red females, white females, red males and white males in the ratio 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. The results of the reciprocal crosses are different, which proves that the gene is on the X chromosome; the eye colour moves "criss-cross": from the white-eyed mother to her sons and from the father through the carrier daughter to the grandson.

@s 11
**Source of O₂ is water, not CO₂ (2 marks).**
1. **Van Niel (1931):** purple sulphur bacteria use H₂S instead of H₂O, giving sulphur and no oxygen: CO₂ + 2H₂S → (CH₂O) + 2S + H₂O. He generalised the reaction as CO₂ + 2H₂A → (CH₂O) + 2A + H₂O, so in green plants the "A" liberated is O₂ from H₂O (6CO₂ + 12H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O).
2. **Ruben and Kamen (1941), isotope experiment** with the alga *Chlorella*: when water labelled with ¹⁸O (H₂¹⁸O) and ordinary CO₂ was given, the O₂ evolved was labelled with ¹⁸O; when ordinary water and C¹⁸O₂ were given, the O₂ evolved was **not** labelled (the ¹⁸O was found in the carbohydrate and water). So the oxygen comes from the photolysis of water.

**Hill reaction (6 marks).** In 1937 **Robert Hill** showed that isolated chloroplasts, when illuminated in a suspension that contains a suitable artificial electron (hydrogen) acceptor but **no CO₂**, release oxygen. The overall reaction is

2H₂O + 2A —(light, chloroplasts)→ 2AH₂ + O₂

where A is an artificial acceptor such as ferricyanide (blue-green → colourless ferrocyanide), 2,6-dichlorophenol-indophenol (DCPIP, blue → colourless) or benzoquinone.

**Experiment.** A suspension of chloroplasts (from crushed spinach leaves, in cold sucrose buffer) is mixed with DCPIP and kept in light, with control tubes: (1) chloroplasts + DCPIP in light: blue colour fades, O₂ evolved; (2) the same kept in dark: remains blue; (3) boiled chloroplasts + DCPIP in light: remains blue; (4) DCPIP and buffer without chloroplasts in light: remains blue. The loss of blue colour is measured with a colorimeter.

**Importance.**
1. It proves that **O₂ evolution (photolysis of water) is a light-dependent process of the chloroplast that does not need CO₂**, so the light reaction is separate from the CO₂ fixation (dark) reaction.
2. The oxygen comes from water, since no CO₂ is present.
3. The artificial acceptor takes up the electrons and protons released by water, as NADP⁺ does in the intact cell; thus the Hill reaction is the electron-transport part of the light reaction (photosystem II). In the cell, 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂, and NADP⁺ is reduced to NADPH + H⁺, which with ATP is used in the Calvin cycle.
4. It shows that isolated chloroplasts can perform the light reaction, but not (without extra factors) the whole photosynthesis.

Simple epithelium consists of a **single layer** of cells resting on a basement membrane. It lines body cavities and ducts, and is classified by the shape of its cells.

| Type | Structure | Location | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Simple squamous** | single layer of flat, polygonal cells with a central bulging nucleus; fits like a pavement | alveoli of lungs, lining of blood vessels (endothelium), Bowman's capsule, mesothelium | diffusion and filtration |
| **Simple cuboidal** | cube-shaped cells with a round central nucleus; some have microvilli | kidney tubules (PCT has brush border), ducts of glands, thyroid follicles, germinal epithelium of ovary | secretion and absorption |
| **Simple columnar** | tall, pillar-like cells with oval nuclei near the base; may have microvilli, goblet cells or cilia | stomach and intestine (absorptive, goblet cells secrete mucus), fallopian tubes (ciliated) | secretion, absorption, movement of ovum or mucus |
| **Pseudostratified** | cells of different heights, all touching the basement membrane, but the nuclei lie at different levels so it looks layered | trachea and bronchi (ciliated), male urethra | secretion of mucus and movement of particles |

**Oogenesis** is the formation of mature ova (eggs) in the ovary of the female. It has three phases.
1. **Multiplication phase:** in the embryonic ovary the germinal epithelium forms primordial germ cells which divide by mitosis to form millions of **oogonia** (2n). This phase is finished before birth; no new oogonia are formed afterwards.
2. **Growth phase:** each oogonium enlarges, stores yolk and becomes a **primary oocyte** (2n), which starts meiosis I but stops at prophase I. It is surrounded by follicle cells (a primary follicle) and remains in this state until puberty; of about 2 million at birth only 300,000 to 400,000 remain at puberty, and only about 400 are ovulated in life.
3. **Maturation phase:** from puberty, one follicle matures every month (under FSH). The primary oocyte completes **meiosis I** just before ovulation, with unequal division of the cytoplasm, giving a large **secondary oocyte** (n) and a tiny **first polar body** (n). The secondary oocyte starts meiosis II but stops at metaphase II and is ovulated. **Meiosis II is completed only if a sperm enters**: it gives a large **ovum** (n) and a **second polar body** (n); the first polar body may also divide into two. The polar bodies degenerate.

Thus **one oogonium gives only one functional ovum and three polar bodies**, the opposite of spermatogenesis, where one cell gives four sperm; the yolk is saved for the single ovum.

**Or — *In-vitro* fertilization (IVF).** IVF is fertilization of the ovum by a sperm **outside the body (in a laboratory dish)** under controlled conditions, followed by transfer of the embryo into the female reproductive tract; the child is popularly called a "test-tube baby" (first born 1978, Louise Brown). It helps couples with blocked fallopian tubes, low sperm count or other causes of infertility.

**Process.**
1. **Ovarian stimulation:** the woman is given FSH / hMG hormone injections to produce several ova at the same time.
2. **Egg retrieval:** the mature ova are collected from the follicles with a fine needle, guided by ultrasound (or laparoscopy).
3. **Sperm collection:** semen from the husband (or donor) is processed to select active sperm.
4. **Fertilization:** the ova and sperm are mixed in a culture medium in a Petri dish and kept at 37 °C, so that fertilization takes place and zygotes form.
5. **Embryo culture:** the zygote divides to form embryos which are grown in the lab for 2 to 5 days.
6. **Embryo transfer:** an embryo of up to eight blastomeres is transferred into the fallopian tube (ZIFT, zygote intra-fallopian transfer), and one with more than eight blastomeres is placed in the uterus (IUT, intra-uterine transfer); it implants in the uterine wall and then grows normally to birth.

**Cholera (1 mark).** Cholera is an acute infection of the small intestine that causes sudden, profuse, watery diarrhoea ("rice-water stools") and rapid loss of water and salts, so that a patient can die of dehydration within hours if untreated. It occurs as epidemics in crowded places with poor sanitation, especially in the rainy season.

**Causative agent (1 mark).** *Vibrio cholerae*, a comma-shaped, motile, Gram-negative bacterium with a single polar flagellum; the serogroups O1 (classical and El Tor biotypes) and O139 cause epidemics. In the intestine it releases the **cholera toxin (choleragen)**, which makes the cells of the intestinal wall pump out large amounts of water and chloride.

**Mode of transmission (2 marks).**
1. **Faeco-oral route:** the bacteria in the stool or vomit of patients and carriers reach water, and the disease spreads by drinking **contaminated water** (the main route) or eating contaminated food (raw vegetables, uncooked seafood, street food).
2. **Houseflies** carry the bacteria from stool to food; unwashed hands and utensils also spread it.
3. Poor sanitation, open defaecation, floods and crowding favour outbreaks; there is no animal host in most cases, but carriers (people with no symptoms) spread the infection.
The incubation period is a few hours to five days.

**Symptoms (2 marks).** Sudden, painless, profuse **watery diarrhoea** (rice-water stools, up to 1 litre per hour) with flecks of mucus, followed by **vomiting**; thirst, **dehydration** (dry tongue, sunken eyes, loose skin, reduced urine), **muscle cramps** (loss of salts), weak and rapid pulse, low blood pressure, cold clammy skin, and in severe cases shock, kidney failure and death.

**Control measures (2 marks).**
1. **Safe drinking water:** boil, filter or chlorinate water; protect the water sources from contamination by sewage.
2. **Hygiene and sanitation:** wash hands with soap after the toilet and before eating; use latrines; dispose of waste properly; control flies; cover food and avoid street food and uncooked seafood.
3. **Treatment:** immediate **oral rehydration solution (ORS)**, with zinc; intravenous fluids (Ringer's lactate) for severe cases; antibiotics such as doxycycline or azithromycin shorten the illness.
4. **Prevention:** oral cholera vaccine for people at risk; notify cases to health authorities, isolate patients, disinfect their excreta and bedding, and spread health education during outbreaks.

**Digestion of proteins in the gut (4 marks).** Proteins are long chains of amino acids; they are broken at the peptide bonds by **proteolytic enzymes** (proteases) through hydrolysis, in three regions:
1. **Mouth:** no protein digestion (saliva has no protease).
2. **Stomach:** gastric glands secrete gastric juice with **HCl** (pH 1.5 to 2), which kills bacteria, denatures proteins and activates the inactive **pepsinogen** into **pepsin**. Pepsin (with HCl) splits proteins into **proteoses and peptones** (large polypeptides). In infants **rennin** (chymosin) curdles milk protein (casein → paracasein, with calcium forming calcium paracaseinate), so that pepsin can act on it. Mucus protects the stomach wall. The food becomes a semi-fluid acidic **chyme**.
3. **Small intestine (duodenum):** the acid chyme is neutralised by bile and pancreatic juice (pH about 8). Pancreatic **trypsinogen** is activated by the enzyme **enterokinase** of the intestinal wall into **trypsin**, which activates more trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen and procarboxypeptidase. **Trypsin and chymotrypsin** break proteoses and peptones into smaller peptides, and **carboxypeptidase** cuts amino acids off the carboxyl end.
4. **Intestinal juice (succus entericus):** the brush-border enzymes **aminopeptidase and dipeptidases (erepsin)** finish the digestion of peptides and dipeptides into **amino acids**.

The amino acids are absorbed by the villi of the small intestine (active transport) into the blood capillaries, and are carried by the hepatic portal vein to the liver and the cells.

Proteins —(pepsin, HCl)→ proteoses + peptones —(trypsin, chymotrypsin)→ peptides —(aminopeptidase, dipeptidase)→ amino acids.

**Relationship between amylase and lipase (1 mark).** Both are hydrolysing enzymes of the digestive juices (pancreatic juice acts in the duodenum at an alkaline pH, and saliva has a salivary amylase) that act on different substrates: **amylase** digests starch (carbohydrate) into maltose, and **lipase** digests emulsified fat into fatty acids and glycerol. They are complementary, not dependent on each other: working together at one site they digest the carbohydrate and fat of a meal, while the bile salts emulsify fats to give lipase a large surface to act on.

**Or — Sino-auricular node is the natural pacemaker (3 marks).** The **SA node** is a small patch of specialised cardiac (nodal) muscle in the wall of the right atrium near the opening of the superior vena cava. It is the pacemaker because:
1. it can **generate action potentials by itself** (the heart is myogenic) without any nerve signal;
2. it has the **highest rate of spontaneous discharge**, about 70 to 75 impulses per minute, faster than the AV node (40 to 60 per minute) and the Purkinje fibres (15 to 40 per minute), so it sets the rate for the whole heart;
3. its impulse spreads across both atria, which contract; reaches the **AV node**, passes down the **bundle of His** and its branches and the **Purkinje fibres**, so that the ventricles contract after the atria. The nervous system (sympathetic and vagus nerves) and adrenaline only speed up or slow down the SA node.

**If the natural pacemaker fails (2 marks).** The heart rate becomes too slow or irregular (bradycardia or heart block). An **artificial pacemaker** is implanted: a small battery-operated device is placed under the skin of the chest, and its wires (leads) pass through a vein to the right atrium and ventricle. It senses the heart rhythm and gives small electrical pulses to make the heart beat at a normal rate. Medicines and treatment of the cause (for example a heart attack) may also be needed, with regular check-ups and battery replacement every 8 to 10 years.

**Blood group test (3 marks).** ABO and Rh groups are tested by **agglutination** on a clean glass slide with antisera:
1. Clean the fingertip with spirit and prick it with a sterile lancet.
2. Place three separate drops of blood on the slide and add one drop of **anti-A**, **anti-B** and **anti-D (anti-Rh)** serum to the drops respectively.
3. Mix each with a separate clean stick and rock the slide for about a minute.
4. Look for **agglutination** (clumping of the red cells) with the naked eye or with a microscope.

| anti-A only | A |
| anti-B only | B |
| anti-A and anti-B | AB |
