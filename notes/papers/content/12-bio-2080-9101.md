@s 12
**Hypertonic, hypotonic and isotonic solutions.** Tonicity is the relative solute concentration of the solution outside a cell compared with the cell sap/cytoplasm.

| Basis | Hypotonic solution | Isotonic solution | Hypertonic solution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solute concentration | Lower than that inside the cell | Equal to that inside the cell | Higher than that inside the cell |
| Water potential | Higher outside than inside | Equal | Lower outside than inside |
| Net movement of water | Into the cell (endosmosis) | No net movement; equal in and out | Out of the cell (exosmosis) |
| Effect on animal cell (e.g. RBC) | Swells and bursts (haemolysis) | Normal shape | Shrinks and becomes crenated |
| Effect on plant cell | Becomes turgid (the wall resists bursting) | Flaccid but not plasmolysed | Cell membrane pulls away from the wall: plasmolysis |
| Example | Distilled water, 0.5% NaCl | 0.9% NaCl (normal saline), 5% glucose | 10% NaCl, concentrated sugar |

**Effect on the movement of water across a selectively permeable membrane.** Water (the solvent) always moves by osmosis from the region of higher water potential (lower solute concentration) to the region of lower water potential (higher solute concentration) through the membrane until the two sides are equal or until the opposing wall pressure/turgor balances the osmotic pressure.
- In a **hypotonic** medium water enters the cell; a plant cell becomes turgid and is supported, while an animal cell with no wall may burst.
- In an **isotonic** medium the rate of entry equals the rate of exit and the volume of the cell does not change.
- In a **hypertonic** medium water leaves the cell, and the cell shrinks (plasmolysis in plants, crenation in animals). This is why salt or sugar preserves pickles and jam, and why a slug dies when salt is put on it.

@s 13
*Incomplete dominance* is the phenomenon in which neither allele of a gene pair is completely dominant over the other, so the heterozygote ($F_1$) shows an intermediate (blended) phenotype between the two homozygous parents. It is also called partial dominance or blending inheritance and was reported by Correns in *Mirabilis jalapa*.

**Example: flower colour in the four o'clock plant (*Mirabilis jalapa*).** The allele $R$ gives red pigment and $r$ (or $R^W$) gives no pigment (white), but $R$ is not completely dominant over $r$.

**Mode of inheritance.**
1. *Parents:* a pure red-flowered plant ($RR$) is crossed with a pure white-flowered plant ($rr$).
2. *$F_1$ generation:* all plants are $Rr$ and bear **pink** flowers, an intermediate colour (a single dose of the $R$ allele produces less pigment).
3. *Selfing the $F_1$ ($Rr \times Rr$):* the gametes are $R$ and $r$ in equal numbers, so the $F_2$ is:

| | $R$ | $r$ |
|---|---|---|
| $R$ | $RR$ red | $Rr$ pink |
| $r$ | $Rr$ pink | $rr$ white |

**Result:** the phenotypic ratio is **1 red : 2 pink : 1 white**, which is the same as the genotypic ratio **1 $RR$ : 2 $Rr$ : 1 $rr$**. In normal (complete) dominance the phenotypic ratio would have been 3 : 1. This shows that the alleles do not blend permanently: red and white reappear unchanged in the $F_2$, so Mendel's law of segregation holds.

Other examples: the snapdragon (*Antirrhinum*) flower colour, the Andalusian fowl (black × white = blue), and wavy hair in humans (straight × curly).

@s 14
*Plasmolysis* is the shrinkage of the protoplasm of a plant cell, due to the loss of water by exosmosis, when the cell is placed in a hypertonic solution; the plasma membrane and cytoplasm pull away from the cell wall (the opposite of deplasmolysis, in which a plasmolysed cell regains water in a hypotonic solution).

**Stages:** *limiting plasmolysis* (the protoplast just begins to leave the corners of the wall), *incipient plasmolysis* (the protoplast has pulled away from the wall at several places) and *evident (complete) plasmolysis* (the protoplast is rounded and completely detached from the wall).

**Significance of plasmolysis.**
1. **Determination of osmotic pressure:** the solute potential of a cell sap is found as the concentration of an external solution that just causes incipient plasmolysis, with which the cell sap is isotonic.
2. **Food preservation:** salting of pickles, fish and meat and the use of sugar in jam and jelly create a hypertonic medium that plasmolyses bacteria and fungi, so they cannot grow.
3. **Killing weeds:** common salt or a strong herbicide solution over weeds draws water from the cells and kills them (also the reason a lawn dies if over-fertilized).
4. **Test for a living cell and for a semipermeable membrane:** plasmolysis occurs only in living cells with a selectively permeable plasma membrane; a dead cell does not plasmolyse.
5. **Wilting and agriculture:** it explains the wilting of plants in saline soil or after an excess of chemical fertilizer, so the farmer must avoid highly saline irrigation.

**Interrelation of digestive, respiratory and circulatory systems.** The body cells need glucose and oxygen to make ATP (cellular respiration, $C_6H_{12}O_6 + 6O_2 \rightarrow 6CO_2 + 6H_2O + \text{ATP}$) and must get rid of the carbon dioxide produced. No single system can do this: the three systems form a chain with the **circulatory system as the transport link**.

1. **Digestive system (supply of fuel).** Food is broken down by mechanical and enzymatic digestion into glucose, amino acids, fatty acids and glycerol. These are absorbed in the villi of the small intestine into capillaries and lacteals. *Without the blood these nutrients would not leave the gut.*
2. **Respiratory system (supply of oxygen, removal of CO₂).** In the alveoli of the lungs oxygen diffuses into the blood, where it binds with haemoglobin to form oxyhaemoglobin, and CO₂ diffuses from blood into the alveoli to be breathed out. *The oxygen is needed to oxidize the digested food.*
3. **Circulatory system (transport).** The heart pumps the blood. The hepatic portal vein takes the absorbed nutrients to the liver and then the general circulation distributes them, the pulmonary circulation exchanges gases at the lung, and the systemic circulation carries oxygenated blood and nutrients to every tissue. It brings back CO₂ to the lungs and wastes to the kidneys.

**Working together.** At the tissue, glucose from the digestive system and oxygen from the respiratory system meet, and energy is released. The CO₂ goes to the lung by the veins, the heart pumps more blood when the cells need more oxygen, and breathing and gut blood flow rise or fall with demand. For example, during exercise the heart rate and breathing rate rise to deliver more oxygen and glucose, while blood is diverted from the gut; after a meal the blood flow to the gut increases. A failure of any one system, e.g. anaemia (blood), pneumonia (lungs) or malabsorption (gut), reduces the energy supply to the whole body, showing that the three systems act as one unit to maintain homeostasis.

**Or.** *Hormones of the adrenal cortex (corticosteroids).* The adrenal (suprarenal) glands lie on the top of each kidney. The cortex has three zones and secretes three groups of steroid hormones, all made from cholesterol and controlled mainly by ACTH from the anterior pituitary:

| Group | Zone | Main hormones | Main action |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mineralocorticoids | Zona glomerulosa | Aldosterone | Regulates Na⁺ and K⁺ in the kidney tubule, hence water balance, blood volume and blood pressure |
| Glucocorticoids | Zona fasciculata | Cortisol (hydrocortisone), corticosterone | Raises blood glucose (gluconeogenesis), breaks down protein and fat, anti-inflammatory and anti-allergic action; helps the body in long-term stress |
| Sex corticoids | Zona reticularis | Androgens (small amount of oestrogen) | Development of pubic and axillary hair and the sex drive; important in women |

**Usefulness to the human body.**
1. *Water and electrolyte balance:* aldosterone conserves sodium and water and excretes potassium; its deficiency causes low blood pressure and dehydration.
2. *Metabolic control:* cortisol keeps blood sugar up between meals and during fasting and mobilizes fatty acids and amino acids for energy.
3. *Stress and immunity:* cortisol helps the body to resist stress, and it suppresses inflammation and the immune reaction (used as the drug hydrocortisone in allergy and organ transplant).
4. *Secondary sexual characters* are supported by the adrenal androgens.

*Disorders:* hyposecretion causes **Addison's disease** (weakness, low blood pressure, bronze skin); hypersecretion causes **Cushing's syndrome** (moon face, central obesity, high blood sugar) and **Conn's syndrome**.

@s 17
*Areolar tissue* is the loose connective tissue that packs the spaces between organs and under the skin. *Adipose tissue* is a specialized connective tissue that stores fat.

**Similarities (2.5 marks).**
1. Both are types of loose connective tissue (connective tissue proper) derived from the embryonic mesoderm (mesenchyme).
2. Both have cells, fibres and a ground substance (matrix) and are well supplied with blood vessels and nerves.
3. Both contain fibroblasts and the macrophages and mast cells in the matrix.
4. Both are found under the skin (the dermis and hypodermis) and around organs, giving support and packing.
5. Both are formed by the same cell line (adipocytes develop from fibroblast-like mesenchymal cells).

**Differences (2.5 marks).**

| Basis | Areolar tissue | Adipose tissue |
|---|---|---|
| Main cells | Fibroblasts, macrophages, mast cells, plasma cells | Adipocytes (fat cells) packed together, each with a large fat droplet pushing the nucleus to one side |
| Matrix | Abundant, semi-fluid, with white collagen and yellow elastic fibres | Very scanty; only a few reticular fibres |
| Fibres | Many and loosely arranged | Few |
| Function | Binds skin to muscle, fills spaces, supports blood vessels and nerves, defence | Stores fat as reserve energy, insulates against heat loss, cushions organs and gives shape |
| Location | Under skin, around blood vessels, nerves, in mesenteries and in the lamina propria | Under the skin, around the kidney and heart, in the omentum, breast and yellow bone marrow |

@s 18
*Diffusion* is the net movement of molecules or ions (of a solid, liquid or gas) from a region of their higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, down the concentration gradient, due to their random kinetic energy, until they are evenly distributed. It needs no energy and no membrane. Examples: spreading of the smell of perfume in a room, a drop of ink in water, exchange of O₂ and CO₂ in the leaf or alveolus.

**Comparison between diffusion and osmosis.**

| Basis | Diffusion | Osmosis |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Movement of particles from higher to lower concentration | Movement of solvent (water) from lower to higher solute concentration through a semipermeable membrane |
| Membrane | Not required | Selectively permeable membrane is essential |
| Medium | Solid, liquid and gas | Liquid (solvent) only |
| What moves | Solute and solvent both | Only the solvent |
| Direction | Down the concentration gradient of the substance | Towards the lower water potential |
| Pressure | No pressure is developed | Osmotic pressure and turgor pressure develop |
| Role | Gas exchange, absorption of nutrients, spread of hormones | Absorption of water by root hairs, turgidity of cells, opening of stomata |
| Example | Perfume in air | Raisins swell in water |

@s 19
*Biotechnology* is the use of living organisms or their parts (cells, enzymes, genes) to make products and processes useful to humans; modern biotechnology uses genetic engineering and tissue culture.

**Applications in medicine.** Production of human insulin, growth hormone and interferon by recombinant bacteria; vaccines (hepatitis B subunit vaccine) and monoclonal antibodies; gene therapy (ADA deficiency), DNA fingerprinting, PCR-based diagnosis, stem cell therapy and antibiotics from microbes.

**Applications in agriculture.** Bt cotton and Bt maize resistant to insect pests, herbicide-tolerant soybean, Golden rice (rich in β-carotene), virus-resistant papaya, long shelf-life tomato (Flavr Savr), micropropagation of orchid, banana and potato, biofertilizers and biopesticides, and transgenic animals with higher milk or meat yield.

| | Potential benefits | Potential drawbacks |
|---|---|---|
| Medicine | Cheap and safe medicine in bulk, early diagnosis of genetic disease, cure for hereditary disease, organs for transplant, quick vaccines | High cost, ethical concern (cloning, designer babies, embryo use), possible misuse (biological weapon), unforeseen side effects, genetic privacy |
| Agriculture | Higher yield, less pesticide, resistance to drought and pests, better nutrition, long storage, disease-free planting material | Loss of biodiversity, super weeds and resistant insects, gene flow to wild relatives, possible allergy and health effects, high seed cost to the farmer and patent control by companies |

**Analysis.** The benefits (food security, improved health, reduced chemical use) are large, but because the risks to health, environment and ethics are real, each product should be tested, regulated by biosafety laws, labelled and used with public consent so that its benefits exceed its costs.

@s 20
**Definition (1).** *Amniocentesis* is a prenatal diagnostic technique in which a small sample of the amniotic fluid, containing fetal cells shed from the skin, lungs and urinary tract of the fetus, is withdrawn from the amniotic sac of a pregnant woman with a hollow needle and examined, to detect genetic and chromosomal disorders in the fetus.

**Why it is done (1).** To detect, before birth, chromosomal abnormalities such as Down syndrome (trisomy 21), Edward syndrome and Turner's or Klinefelter's syndrome, gene disorders such as cystic fibrosis, sickle cell anaemia, Tay–Sachs disease and haemophilia, neural-tube defects (raised α-fetoprotein), the maturity of the fetal lung, and fetal infection. It is advised mainly if the mother is over 35 years or has a family history of a genetic disease.

**Procedure (6).**
1. **Counselling and consent:** the doctor explains the purpose and risk, and the written consent of the mother is taken. The test is normally done at the **15th to 18th week** of pregnancy, when the amount of amniotic fluid is enough.
2. **Ultrasound scan:** the position of the fetus, the placenta and the pocket of fluid is located so that the needle can avoid damaging them.
3. **Cleaning and anaesthesia:** the mother's abdomen is cleaned with an antiseptic and, if needed, a local anaesthetic is given.
4. **Withdrawal of fluid:** guided continuously by ultrasound, a thin hollow needle is inserted through the abdominal wall and the uterine wall into the amniotic sac and about **10 to 20 mL** of the straw-coloured amniotic fluid is withdrawn with a syringe. The needle is then removed and the fetal heart beat is checked.
5. **Separation and culture:** the fluid is centrifuged; the supernatant is used for biochemical tests (α-fetoprotein, enzymes) and the fetal cells in the pellet are cultured for 1 to 2 weeks in a nutrient medium.
6. **Karyotyping and analysis:** the dividing cells are arrested at metaphase with colchicine, stained and photographed, and the chromosomes are arranged in pairs (karyotype) to count chromosomes and see structural change. DNA analysis is also done. The result is given to the parents and the doctor, who advise them.
7. **Rest after the test:** the mother rests for a day and avoids heavy work.

**Excretory organs of human beings (4).**
1. **Kidneys:** a pair of bean-shaped, reddish-brown organs (about 10 cm long) lying against the back of the abdomen on each side of the vertebral column. Each has an outer *cortex*, inner *medulla* with pyramids and a *pelvis*. The unit is the **nephron** (about a million per kidney) with the glomerulus, Bowman's capsule, PCT, loop of Henle, DCT and collecting duct. Blood is filtered (glomerular filtration about 180 L per day), useful substances such as glucose, water and amino acids are reabsorbed and wastes (urea, creatinine, excess H⁺) are secreted, forming urine (1 to 1.5 L per day). The kidney also regulates water, salt and pH, and secretes erythropoietin and renin.
2. **Ureters:** two muscular tubes, 25 to 30 cm long, that carry urine from the renal pelvis to the bladder by peristalsis.
3. **Urinary bladder:** a muscular distensible sac in the pelvis that stores urine (up to about 500 mL); the urge to urinate arises when it is stretched, and the sphincters control the release (micturition).
4. **Urethra:** a tube that carries urine from the bladder to the outside (about 4 cm in a woman and 20 cm in a man, in whom it also carries semen).
5. **Accessory excretory organs:** the *skin* (sweat glands remove water, salt, a little urea), *lungs* (CO₂ and water vapour) and *liver* (forms urea, excretes bile pigments).

**Semi-conservative replication of DNA (6).** *Replication* is the process by which a DNA molecule makes two identical copies of itself, during the S-phase of the cell cycle. Watson and Crick (1953) suggested that it is **semi-conservative**: each daughter DNA double helix has one *old (parental) strand* and one *newly synthesized strand*. It was proved by **Meselson and Stahl (1958)** in *E. coli* grown first in ¹⁵N (heavy) medium and then in ¹⁴N (light) medium: after one generation all DNA was of intermediate (hybrid) density, and after two generations half was hybrid and half light, which fits semi-conservative replication and rules out conservative and dispersive replication.

**Steps.**
1. **Initiation:** replication starts at the *origin of replication* (*ori*; one in bacteria, many in eukaryotes). **Helicase** unwinds the double helix by breaking the hydrogen bonds between the base pairs, creating a Y-shaped *replication fork*; **single-strand binding (SSB) proteins** keep the strands apart and **topoisomerase (gyrase)** removes the supercoiling ahead of the fork.
2. **Primer synthesis:** **primase** makes a short RNA primer, giving the free 3′-OH needed because DNA polymerase cannot start a chain.
3. **Elongation:** **DNA polymerase III** adds nucleotides complementary to the template (A–T, G–C), only in the 5′→3′ direction. The **leading strand** is synthesized continuously toward the fork; the **lagging strand** is synthesized away from the fork in short pieces called **Okazaki fragments** (1000 to 2000 nucleotides in bacteria; 100 to 200 in eukaryotes), each with its own primer.
4. **Primer removal and joining:** **DNA polymerase I** removes the RNA primers and fills the gaps with DNA; **DNA ligase** seals the nicks.
5. **Termination:** the replication forks meet, and each daughter DNA, with one old and one new strand, rewinds into a double helix.

**Contribution to the accurate transmission of genetic information (2).**
- The base sequence of each old strand acts as a *template*, so strict base pairing (A–T, G–C) makes each new DNA an exact copy; hence every daughter cell in mitosis and every gamete receives a complete, identical copy of the genome.
- **Proofreading** by DNA polymerase III (3′→5′ exonuclease) and the *mismatch-repair* system correct wrong bases, so the error rate is only about one in 10⁹ to 10¹⁰ nucleotides. Thus the genetic information is stable from generation to generation, and the rare errors that escape are mutations, the source of variation for evolution.

**Or.** **Gene (point) mutation (4).** A *gene mutation* is a sudden, heritable change in the sequence of nucleotides within a gene. It may arise spontaneously or be induced by mutagens (UV rays, X-rays, nitrous acid, EMS, benzopyrene).

| Type | Change in DNA | Effect on the protein / organism |
|---|---|---|
| **Base substitution** (point mutation) | One base replaced by another: *transition* (purine↔purine or pyrimidine↔pyrimidine, e.g. A→G) or *transversion* (purine↔pyrimidine, e.g. A→T) | **Silent** (new codon codes the same amino acid, no effect), **missense** (a different amino acid, e.g. sickle-cell anaemia, GAG→GUG changes Glu to Val in β-globin), **nonsense** (a stop codon appears, a shortened, usually non-functional protein) |
| **Insertion** | One or more bases added | **Frameshift**: the reading frame changes from that point; the entire amino acid sequence after it is altered (or an early stop codon appears); the protein is usually lost |
| **Deletion** | One or more bases lost | Frameshift if 1, 2 or 4... bases are lost; if a multiple of 3, the protein loses some amino acids (e.g. cystic fibrosis ΔF508) |
| **Inversion / duplication (within a gene)** | Segment reversed or repeated | Altered or non-functional protein, extra repeats (Huntington disease: CAG repeats) |

Other kinds are *forward* and *reverse (back) mutation* and *somatic* versus *germinal* mutation. The overall effect on the genetic make-up is a new allele; most are recessive and harmful, a few are neutral or beneficial.

**Positive and negative impacts (4).** *Chromosomal mutations* are changes in the structure of chromosomes (deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation) or in their number (aneuploidy, e.g. 2n+1; polyploidy).

| | Positive (beneficial) impacts | Negative (harmful) impacts |
|---|---|---|
| **Gene mutation** | Source of new alleles and variation for natural selection and evolution; origin of high-yielding, disease-resistant varieties (induced mutation in rice, wheat, groundnut); resistance to disease (sickle-cell trait gives protection against malaria); useful for mutation breeding | Genetic disease: sickle-cell anaemia, phenylketonuria, albinism, haemophilia, cystic fibrosis; cancer through mutation in oncogenes and tumour-suppressor genes (*p53*); antibiotic resistance in bacteria; lethal mutations kill the embryo |
| **Chromosomal mutation** | Polyploidy gives larger, seedless fruits, hybrid vigour, new crop species (triticale, bread wheat, *Raphanobrassica*); inversions and translocations give new gene combinations and help in speciation | Aneuploidy: Down syndrome (21), Turner (45, X), Klinefelter (47, XXY), Patau and Edward syndromes; deletions such as *cri-du-chat*; translocation causes chronic myeloid leukaemia (Philadelphia chromosome); sterility and abortion |

**Evaluation.** Mutations are two-edged: for an individual most are harmful or neutral (causing disease, cancer and death), but for a species they are the *raw material of evolution* and of crop improvement. Therefore exposure to mutagens (radiation, tobacco, certain chemicals) should be reduced, genetic counselling and prenatal tests such as amniocentesis should be used, and induced mutation should be used wisely in breeding.
