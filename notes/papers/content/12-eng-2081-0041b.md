Television can be defined as an **electronic** device that is used for receiving sound and images that create television programs which the people watch. Television is also popularly known as T.V. or Telly.

On March 25, 1925, the Scottish **inventor** John Logie Baird made the first-ever public display of moving visuals on television. The TV set that Baird made had just one color. It could only show part of the picture. To be **precise**, it just showed the face of the person on the screen. Back then TVs didn't work well, but it was just the beginning.

The first TV station was set up in 1928 in New York City. Few families had TVs in their homes. The TV broadcasts were not meant to be watched. They showed a Felix the Cat doll for two hours a day. TVs have come a long way since John Baird's 30 line set. High-definition TVs have 1080 lines. There are also "state of the art" sets, which are called 4K TVs. They have 3,840 lines. These TVs are state of the art because people can watch TV in 3-D. Over the years, the television has really come a long way!

**(a) Neighbours.** At the end of the story the young man weeps with joy when he sees the neighbours gathered along the fence, cheering the first cry of his newborn baby. Earlier he had been uneasy with these migrant families and their noisy ways. Their gifts and care during the pregnancy and their delight now show him that they truly care about his family. He realises that real human relationships teach more than books: "the twentieth-century novel had not prepared him for this."

**(b) A Respectable Woman.** Mrs. Baroda expects Gouvernail, her husband's college friend, to be a striking, difficult literary man, and at first she finds him unremarkable and quiet and does not like him. When he stays on the plantation his calm, thoughtful manner begins to attract her, and one moonlit night she feels a strong pull towards him. Frightened by her own feelings she leaves for the city. At the end she asks her husband to invite him again. Her view changes from dislike to attraction, which she tries to control.

**Or — A Devoted Son.** Rakesh is a brilliant boy who rises from a poor family to become a doctor and seems an ideal, obedient son. As an anti-hero he is rather the opposite of a warm hero: he controls his old father Varma's life with strict diets, injections and medicines, and refuses him the sweets and fried food he longs for. He treats his father like a patient and not like a person with wishes. The town praises him, yet the old man dies resentful. The title is therefore ironic.

**(c) A Day.** The poet describes the sunrise coming "a ribbon at a time" as the sky changes colour from one tint to another. She compares the day with children at play: the sunbeams are "little yellow boys and girls" climbing a purple stile of cloud, bright, lively and full of fresh energy, just as childhood is. When the sun sets, a "dominie in gray", the evening, leads them away like a teacher. Sunrise, like childhood, is beautiful, innocent and short.

**(d) Marriage as a Social Institution.** Yes. Marriage is different from other relationships such as friendship, work ties or even kinship. It is recognised by society, law and religion, and it carries fixed rights and duties for both partners. It regulates sexual relations, begins a new family, makes children legitimate and settles questions of property and inheritance. Other networks are mostly informal and voluntary and are easily ended without such social approval and obligations. So marriage is a socially approved, binding institution.

**Or — Humility.** The Hindu nativists answer the Chinese nationalists' boast that human history began with the Yellow Emperor and the Xia and Shang dynasties by claiming a far older and greater past of their own. They say that ancient sages of the Indian subcontinent had invented aeroplanes, rockets, missiles and even nuclear weapons long before Confucius or Plato, let alone Einstein and the Wright brothers. They point to Maharishi Bhardwaj, who is said to have invented the rocket and the aeroplane, to Vishwamitra, who created and used missiles, to Acharya Kanad, called the father of atomic theory, and to the Mahabharata, which describes weapons like nuclear bombs. In this way they reject the Chinese claim as only one more proud claim.

**(e) A Matter of Husbands.** The Famous Actress lives in two personalities at once. In real life she is in love with Alfred, the Earnest Woman's husband, and he is hiding in her closet. But in front of the wife she plays the innocent, sympathetic woman who has never received a flower or a letter from him. She even invents a story that he is only pretending to make his wife jealous. Her real self and her acted self are completely different, and so the play shows a split personality. At the end she calls Alfred out of the closet.

**(a)** In Márquez's story the old man with enormous wings is weak, dependent on others and different from them, and the villagers exploit him for that reason. When he is found fallen in the courtyard in the rain, Pelayo and Elisenda first think of him only as a source of money. They lock him in a chicken coop and charge admission to people who come to see him. The crowds throw stones and food at him and even burn his side with a branding iron to make him react. Father Gonzaga, the priest, treats him as a suspicious creature instead of helping. When the public lose interest in him, Pelayo and Elisenda use the money they have earned to build a big house, but they never care for him. This shows the cruel side of human nature: people use anyone who is helpless and unlike themselves.

**Or — Human Rights and the Age of Inequality.** In the essay, Samuel Moyn begins with the story of Croesus, the wealthy king of Herodotus' history whose riches were seized by the Persian king, and uses it to show a world in which the rich enjoy freedom and comfort while the poor live with only an illusion of equality. Moyn says that human rights, such as civil liberties, protect people's freedom of speech, belief and security but do not reduce the huge gap between the rich and the poor; the Universal Declaration promised equal dignity, yet the world stayed unequal. Applying this to Nepal, civil liberties are guaranteed by our constitution: we can speak, write, organise and vote freely. But a poor farmer in Karnali, a Dalit family or a daily wage worker cannot use these rights as fully as a wealthy person in Kathmandu, because hunger, lack of schools, hospitals and legal help keep them silent and dependent. In my view, therefore, civil liberties in Nepal are real on paper but unequal in practice. To make them meaningful, the state should combine rights with fair distribution: free basic education and health, land and job opportunities, and strong, impartial courts. Only then does freedom stop being the luxury of the rich and become the everyday experience of every citizen, as Moyn's call for egalitarian justice suggests.

**(b)** If Mr. Durand had not killed himself, his life would probably have continued as a slow tragedy. He was a poor man in exile, with no regular income, heavy debts and creditors pressing him. His daughters were angry with him and blamed him for their poverty, and he was almost starving. The insurance plan would not have worked, and his daughters would have remained without money. He might have been arrested for the attempt to burn his house, and the family would have become poorer still.

He did have other options. He could have taken any available job, even a humble one, and his three daughters could have worked as well. He could have asked his relatives or friends for loans, or sold some belongings to pay his debts. Together the family could have moved to a cheaper place and started again, supporting one another. Death solved nothing: living and working together was the better and braver choice.

Both teams attacked from the opening whistle and gave an exciting performance, and the crowd cheered wildly at every move. After a goalless first half, an MMC striker scored in the second half with a powerful shot from the edge of the box, and the MMC supporters broke into wild celebrations.

**Earthquake Safety: What the Government and Individuals Should Do**

Nepal lies in an active seismic zone where the Indian plate pushes under the Eurasian plate. The 1934 earthquake and the devastating 2015 Gorkha earthquake killed thousands of people and destroyed homes, schools, temples and roads. We cannot stop earthquakes, but with careful planning and preparation we can greatly reduce the loss of life and property.

The government has the main responsibility. First, it must enforce the building code strictly so that every new house, school, hospital and bridge is earthquake resistant, and municipalities must not approve unsafe drawings. Second, old and weak public buildings should be inspected and retrofitted. Third, it should prepare hazard maps and stop settlements on weak, steep or flood-prone land. Fourth, it needs well-equipped rescue teams, stocks of tents, food and medicine, and a reliable early communication system. Fifth, earthquake drills and safety lessons should be part of the school curriculum, and masons should be trained and licensed.

Individuals also have a duty. Families should build with good materials and the advice of a trained engineer, fasten heavy furniture to the walls and keep exits clear. Every home should keep an emergency bag with water, torch, first aid kit, radio and important documents, and everyone should know the rule "drop, cover and hold on" and an open place to gather. Neighbours should learn first aid and help one another quickly after the shock.

In conclusion, the loss in an earthquake depends less on the quake itself than on how well we prepare. When government and citizens work together, build safely and practise what to do, Nepal can face the next earthquake with far less suffering.
