$$2mab^2+ma^3+b^2c=0,$$
that is,

**(b)** Let the quadrilateral ABCD be taken in order (vertices labelled round the figure) and take A as the origin, so that $\overrightarrow{AB}=\vec b$, $\overrightarrow{AC}=\vec c$, $\overrightarrow{AD}=\vec d$. The diagonal AC divides the quadrilateral into triangles ABC and ACD, so
$$\text{Area}=\tfrac12|\vec b\times\vec c|+\tfrac12|\vec c\times\vec d|.$$
Going round the quadrilateral in one sense, $\vec b\times\vec c$ and $\vec c\times\vec d$ point the same way (both perpendicular to the plane, on the same side), so the magnitudes add as vectors:
$$\text{Area}=\tfrac12\left|\vec b\times\vec c+\vec c\times\vec d\right|=\tfrac12\left|\vec c\times\vec d-\vec c\times\vec b\right|=\tfrac12\left|\vec c\times(\vec d-\vec b)\right|.$$

$$\text{Area of ABCD}=\tfrac12\left|\overrightarrow{AC}\times\overrightarrow{BD}\right|.\qquad\text{Hence proved.}$$

**(c)** Let $P(n):\ \dfrac{1}{1\cdot2}+\dfrac{1}{2\cdot3}+\dots+\dfrac{1}{n(n+1)}=\dfrac{n}{n+1}$.
*Basis step:* for $n=1$, LHS $=\dfrac{1}{1\cdot2}=\dfrac{1}{2}$ and RHS $=\dfrac{1}{2}$, so $P(1)$ is true.
*Inductive step:* assume $P(k)$ is true for some natural number $k$:
$\dfrac{1}{1\cdot2}+\dots+\dfrac{1}{k(k+1)}=\dfrac{k}{k+1}$. Add the next term $\dfrac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)}$ to both sides:
$$\text{LHS}=\frac{k}{k+1}+\frac{1}{(k+1)(k+2)}=\frac{k(k+2)+1}{(k+1)(k+2)}=\frac{(k+1)^2}{(k+1)(k+2)}=\frac{k+1}{k+2}.$$

By the principle of mathematical induction, $P(n)$ holds for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$.

**(c)** The equation is homogeneous. Put $y=vx$, so $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=v+x\dfrac{dv}{dx}$:
$$v+x\frac{dv}{dx}=\frac{1+v^2}{2v}$$
$$x\frac{dv}{dx}=\frac{1-v^2}{2v}$$
$$\frac{2v}{1-v^2}\,dv=\frac{dx}{x}$$

**Is it a polynomial?** The relation $x^2-y^2-Cx=0$ is a polynomial *equation* of degree 2 in $x$ and $y$ (a family of rectangular hyperbolas through the origin; for $C=0$ it is the pair of lines $y=\pm x$). But it does **not** give $y$ as a polynomial in $x$: solving, $y=\pm\sqrt{x^2-Cx}$, and a square root of a quadratic with two distinct roots is not a polynomial (it is not defined for $0<x<C$ and its derivative is unbounded at the roots). So the solution is not a polynomial function, except in the special case $C=0$, where $y=\pm x$ are polynomials of degree 1.
