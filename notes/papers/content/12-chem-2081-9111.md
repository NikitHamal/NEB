@s 12
**(a)** Let a solution contain $w$ g of solute of molar mass $M$ and equivalent mass $E$ in $V$ mL. Then
$$\text{Normality } N = \frac{w}{E}\times\frac{1000}{V},\qquad \text{Molarity } M_c = \frac{w}{M}\times\frac{1000}{V}$$
For an acid or base the equivalent mass is $E = \dfrac{M}{n}$, where $n$ is the number of replaceable H⁺ (or OH⁻) per formula unit (the n-factor). Substituting $w/E = n\,w/M$:
$$N = n\times\frac{w}{M}\times\frac{1000}{V} = n\times M_c$$
So **Normality = n-factor × Molarity**. For example, 0.1 M H₂SO₄ (n = 2) is 0.2 N and 0.1 M NaOH (n = 1) is 0.1 N.

**(b)** Milli-equivalents of base: NaOH is monoacidic, so $N = 0.8$ N and meq $= 0.8\times20 = 16$.
Milli-equivalents of acid: H₂SO₄ is dibasic, so $N = 2\times0.4 = 0.8$ N and meq $= 0.8\times25 = 20$.
The acid is in excess:
$$\text{meq of acid left} = 20 - 16 = 4$$
Total volume $= 20 + 25 = 45$ mL, so
$$N_{\text{resulting}} = \frac{4}{45} = 0.089\ \text{N}$$
The mixture is acidic with normality about **0.089 N** (molarity of H₂SO₄ left $= 0.044$ M).

@s 13
*Common ion effect:* the suppression of the ionisation of a weak electrolyte by adding a strong electrolyte that has an ion in common with it. Example: adding NH₄Cl to NH₄OH decreases the OH⁻ concentration, since NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ is pushed to the left.

*Solubility product principle:* in a saturated solution of a sparingly soluble electrolyte at a given temperature, the product of the molar concentrations of its ions, each raised to the power of its coefficient, is constant, $K_{sp}$. For $A_xB_y$: $K_{sp} = [A^{y+}]^x[B^{x-}]^y$. A precipitate forms when the ionic product exceeds $K_{sp}$, and a salt dissolves when the ionic product is less than $K_{sp}$.

*Applications (any three):*
1. **Salting out of soap.** Soap (RCOONa) is ionised in solution. Adding common salt NaCl raises [Na⁺] so that the ionic product $[RCOO^-][Na^+]$ exceeds $K_{sp}$ and the soap separates as a curd.
2. **Purification of common salt.** Impure brine is saturated and HCl gas is passed in. The added Cl⁻ is a common ion, the ionic product of NaCl exceeds $K_{sp}$, and pure NaCl crystallises while the soluble impurities remain in solution.
3. **Group II precipitation in qualitative analysis.** In dilute HCl the high [H⁺] suppresses the ionisation of H₂S ($H_2S \rightleftharpoons 2H^+ + S^{2-}$), so [S²⁻] is small and only the sulphides with very low $K_{sp}$ (Pb, Cu, Hg, Cd, Bi) precipitate; the sulphides of Zn, Mn, Ni, Co (higher $K_{sp}$) stay dissolved. In basic medium [S²⁻] is large and group IV sulphides precipitate.
4. **Group III precipitation.** NH₄Cl is added before NH₄OH so that [OH⁻] is small and only the hydroxides of Fe³⁺, Al³⁺ and Cr³⁺ (very low $K_{sp}$) precipitate and Mg(OH)₂ does not.

@s 14
*Order of reaction* is the sum of the powers to which the concentration terms are raised in the experimentally determined rate law. For $aA + bB\to$ products, rate $= k[A]^m[B]^n$ and the order is $m + n$. It is an experimental quantity and may be zero, fractional or an integer.

*Integrated rate equation for a first order reaction.* Take $A\to$ products with initial concentration $[A]_0 = a$. After time $t$ let $x$ mol L⁻¹ have reacted, so $[A] = a - x$. The rate is proportional to the first power of $[A]$:
$$\frac{dx}{dt} = k(a - x)$$
Separating the variables,
$$\frac{dx}{a - x} = k\,dt$$
Integrating,
$$-\ln(a - x) = kt + I$$
At $t = 0$, $x = 0$, so $I = -\ln a$. Therefore
$$kt = \ln a - \ln(a - x) = \ln\frac{a}{a - x}$$
$$k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log_{10}\frac{a}{a - x}\quad\text{or}\quad[A] = [A]_0\,e^{-kt}$$
The unit of $k$ is s⁻¹ (time⁻¹). A plot of $\ln[A]$ against $t$ is a straight line of slope $-k$. When $x = a/2$ the time is the half-life $t_{1/2} = \dfrac{2.303\log2}{k} = \dfrac{0.693}{k}$, independent of the initial concentration.

@s 16
*Water as an acid and as a base (amphoteric).* In the Bronsted-Lowry concept an acid is a proton donor and a base a proton acceptor. Water can do both depending on the other substance present.

**1. Water as a base (proton acceptor)** towards a stronger acid such as HCl:
$$\text{HCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{Cl}^-$$
Acid 1 = HCl with conjugate base Cl⁻; base 2 = H₂O with conjugate acid H₃O⁺. Conjugate pairs: (HCl, Cl⁻) and (H₃O⁺, H₂O).

**2. Water as an acid (proton donor)** towards a base such as ammonia:
$$\text{NH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{NH}_4^+ + \text{OH}^-$$
Base 1 = NH₃ with conjugate acid NH₄⁺; acid 2 = H₂O with conjugate base OH⁻. Conjugate pairs: (NH₄⁺, NH₃) and (H₂O, OH⁻).

Water accepts a proton in the first reaction and gives one in the second, so it behaves as both a Bronsted-Lowry acid and base.

*Limitation (any one):* the concept applies only to substances that contain or exchange protons, so it cannot explain the acidic behaviour of proton-free substances such as BF₃, AlCl₃, CO₂ and SO₂ (this was removed by the Lewis concept); it also cannot explain acid-base reactions in non-protic solvents.

**Or (Q17): preparation, properties and uses of white vitriol.**

*White vitriol* is hydrated zinc sulphate, ZnSO₄·7H₂O.

**Preparation.**
1. *In the laboratory:* by the action of dilute sulphuric acid on zinc, zinc oxide, zinc hydroxide or zinc carbonate, followed by evaporation to the crystallisation point and cooling:
Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑
ZnO + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂O
ZnCO₃ + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂O + CO₂↑
2. *Industrially:* zinc blende (ZnS) is roasted in a limited supply of air, ZnS + 2O₂ → ZnSO₄ (with some ZnO). The product is leached with dilute H₂SO₄, filtered, and the clear solution is concentrated and crystallised.

**Properties.**
- It is a colourless (white), transparent crystalline solid, soluble in water, and its solution is acidic to litmus because of hydrolysis.
- *Action of heat:* at about 100 °C it loses six molecules of water giving ZnSO₄·H₂O; at about 240 °C it becomes anhydrous ZnSO₄; above 800 °C it decomposes: ZnSO₄ → ZnO + SO₂ + ½O₂.
- *With NaOH:* white ppt of Zn(OH)₂ that dissolves in excess alkali giving sodium zincate: ZnSO₄ + 2NaOH → Zn(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄; Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O.
- *With BaCl₂:* white ppt of BaSO₄ (test for sulphate): ZnSO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + ZnCl₂.
- *With H₂S in ammoniacal medium:* white ZnS: ZnSO₄ + H₂S + 2NH₄OH → ZnS↓ + (NH₄)₂SO₄ + 2H₂O.
- *With BaS:* it gives lithopone: ZnSO₄ + BaS → ZnS·BaSO₄.

**Uses.** In making the white pigment lithopone; in galvanising and electroplating zinc; as an astringent in eye lotions and as an emetic in medicine; as a mordant in calico printing; as a wood preservative and fungicide, and as a zinc supplement for plants and animals.

**(a)** Taking the orders as $x$ for M and $y$ for N, the rate law is
$$\text{rate} = k[M]^x[N]^y$$

**(b)** *Order with respect to M:* compare experiments 1 and 4 where [N] is constant (0.1). [M] increases from 0.1 to 0.4 (4 times) and the rate increases from $6.0\times10^{-3}$ to $2.4\times10^{-2}$, which is also 4 times:

*Order with respect to N:* compare experiments 2 and 3 where [M] is constant (0.3). [N] doubles from 0.2 to 0.4 and the rate rises from $7.2\times10^{-2}$ to $2.88\times10^{-1}$, which is 4 times:

*Check with experiments 1 and 2:* [M] triples (factor 3) and [N] doubles (factor $2^2 = 4$), so the rate should rise by 12: $6.0\times10^{-3}\times12 = 7.2\times10^{-2}$ ✓.
The reaction is **first order in M, second order in N and third order overall** ($1 + 2 = 3$), and the rate law is rate $= k[M][N]^2$.

**(c)** From experiment 1:
$$k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[M][N]^2} = \frac{6.0\times10^{-3}}{0.1\times(0.1)^2} = \frac{6.0\times10^{-3}}{1.0\times10^{-3}} = 6.0\ \text{L}^2\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$$
(Check with experiment 3: $6\times0.3\times0.16 = 0.288$ ✓.)

**(d)** For an $n$th order reaction the rate has the unit mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ and the concentration term has (mol L⁻¹)ⁿ, so
$$k = \frac{\text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}}{(\text{mol L}^{-1})^n} = (\text{mol L}^{-1})^{1-n}\,\text{s}^{-1}$$
For the overall third order ($n = 3$): $k$ has the unit $(\text{mol L}^{-1})^{-2}\text{s}^{-1} = \text{L}^2\,\text{mol}^{-2}\,\text{s}^{-1}$.

**Or (a)** *Second law of thermodynamics* (several equivalent statements):
- *Kelvin-Planck:* it is impossible to construct a cyclic engine that takes heat from a single reservoir and converts all of it into work.
- *Clausius:* heat cannot flow by itself from a colder body to a hotter body; work must be done to do so.
- *Entropy statement:* in any spontaneous process the total entropy of the system plus surroundings increases, $\Delta S_{total} = \Delta S_{sys} + \Delta S_{surr} > 0$. At equilibrium (a reversible process) $\Delta S_{total} = 0$.

*Explanation.* The first law only says that energy is conserved; it does not say in which direction a change will occur. The second law gives that direction. Heat flows from hot to cold, gases expand into a vacuum and solutes diffuse from high to low concentration on their own, and never the reverse, because each of these raises the disorder (entropy) of the universe. No heat engine is 100 percent efficient, since some heat must always be rejected to a sink: $\eta = 1 - T_2/T_1 < 1$. A process with $\Delta S_{total} > 0$ is feasible (spontaneous); one with $\Delta S_{total} < 0$ cannot occur by itself.

**Or (b)** The *standard Gibbs free energy change* $\Delta G^{\circ}$ of a reaction is the change in Gibbs free energy when the reactants in their standard states are completely converted to products in their standard states (1 bar, usually 298 K):
$$\Delta G^{\circ} = \Delta H^{\circ} - T\Delta S^{\circ} = -RT\ln K$$
*Prediction of spontaneity from the sign of $\Delta G$ (constant T and P):*
- $\Delta G < 0$: the process is **spontaneous** (feasible in the forward direction). Example: $\Delta H < 0$ and $\Delta S > 0$ always gives a negative $\Delta G$.
- $\Delta G > 0$: the process is **non-spontaneous** (the reverse reaction is spontaneous); it takes place only if energy is supplied.
- $\Delta G = 0$: the system is **at equilibrium**; there is no net change, and $\Delta H = T\Delta S$.

**1. Concentration (froth flotation).** The ore is crushed and finely ground in a ball mill. The powder is mixed with water, a little pine oil (frothing agent) and sodium ethyl xanthate (collector) in a tank, and air is blown through. The sulphide particles are wetted by the oil and rise with the froth, which is skimmed off, while the gangue (silica, silicates) is wetted by water and sinks. The concentrate is filtered and dried; it contains about 30 percent copper.

**2. Roasting.** The concentrate is heated strongly in excess air in a reverberatory furnace (below its melting point). This
- removes moisture and volatile impurities (arsenic and antimony as As₄O₆, Sb₂O₃) and burns sulphur as SO₂, and
- partly oxidises the sulphides:
2CuFeS₂ + O₂ → Cu₂S + 2FeS + SO₂↑
2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂↑
2FeS + 3O₂ → 2FeO + 2SO₂↑
The SO₂ evolved is used to make sulphuric acid.

**3. Smelting.** The roasted ore is mixed with powdered coke and silica (SiO₂, the flux) and heated to about 1400 °C in a blast (or reverberatory) furnace.
FeO + SiO₂ → FeSiO₃ (slag, light, floats)
Cu₂O + FeS → Cu₂S + FeO
The molten mixture of Cu₂S and FeS (about 1 to 2 FeS : 1 Cu₂S), called *matte copper* (about 40 to 50 percent Cu), sinks to the bottom and is tapped off; the slag is removed from above. The matte is then put into the Bessemer converter with silica and a blast of air: the remaining FeS is oxidised into slag and Cu₂S is partly oxidised, then 2Cu₂O + Cu₂S → 6Cu + SO₂. The product, blister copper (98 to 99 percent Cu), is later refined electrolytically.

**Or (a)** Haematite is **Fe₂O₃**.

**Or (b)** In roasting (calcination) of the concentrated ore in excess air the impurities are removed as follows.
Moisture: it is driven off as steam on heating, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O → Fe₂O₃ + xH₂O↑.
Carbon (organic matter): C + O₂ → CO₂↑
Sulphur: S + O₂ → SO₂↑ (or 4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂↑)
Phosphorus: 4P + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀ (volatile, escapes; arsenic similarly goes as As₄O₆)
Also FeO is oxidised to Fe₂O₃: 4FeO + O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃.

**Or (c)** The blast furnace is a tall steel cylinder (25 to 30 m) lined with fire-bricks, wide in the middle and narrow at the top and bottom. *Charge:* calcined haematite, coke (fuel and reducing agent) and limestone (flux) are fed from the top through the cup and cone arrangement. A blast of hot air (about 800 °C) enters near the bottom through the tuyeres. The charge descends and meets the rising hot gases; the main zones from top to bottom are:
1. *Zone of reduction* (400 to 700 °C): CO produced below reduces the ore stepwise:
3Fe₂O₃ + CO → 2Fe₃O₄ + CO₂; Fe₃O₄ + CO → 3FeO + CO₂; FeO + CO → Fe + CO₂ (spongy iron).
2. *Zone of slag formation* (800 to 1000 °C): limestone decomposes, CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, and lime combines with silica gangue to form the fusible slag: CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃. Phosphorus is removed as calcium phosphate in the same way.
3. *Zone of fusion* (1000 to 1200 °C): the spongy iron melts, absorbs carbon and collects at the bottom.
4. *Zone of combustion* (about 1500 °C, the hottest): coke burns in the hot blast, C + O₂ → CO₂, and CO₂ + C → 2CO (the reducing gas).
The molten slag floats on the molten iron; both are tapped separately from the bottom. The product is *pig iron* (cast iron, about 4 percent carbon), which is later converted to wrought iron or steel. The waste gases (CO, CO₂, N₂) leave at the top and are used to heat the incoming blast in the stoves.

**(c)** *Advantages:* (1) pesticides kill insects, weeds, fungi and rodents, so crop losses fall and the yield and the food supply rise; (2) they control the carriers of diseases such as malaria (mosquito) and typhus, saving lives, and protect stored grains.
*Disadvantages:* (1) they pollute soil, air and water and their residues enter the food chain (bio-magnification), harming humans and birds (DDT); (2) they kill useful organisms (bees, earthworms, natural predators) and the pests gradually develop resistance, so more toxic doses are needed.

**(d)** Nuclear radiations (alpha, beta, gamma, neutrons and X-rays) are ionising: they knock electrons out of atoms and molecules in living cells. In the body they break the DNA and other molecules, which can cause cancer (leukaemia, thyroid cancer), cataract, burns and loss of hair, nausea, damage to bone marrow and radiation sickness; a very high dose kills quickly. Damage to the reproductive cells causes gene mutations and birth defects that are passed to the next generation (as seen after Hiroshima, Nagasaki and Chernobyl).
