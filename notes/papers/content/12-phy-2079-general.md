@s 12
**Note.** The printed stem says the relation comes from conservation of energy; the relation $I\omega = $ constant actually follows from the conservation of **angular momentum** (no external torque). It is used that way below.

**(a)** For a given mass, the moment of inertia $I = \sum mr^2$ is largest when the mass is far from the axis. A flywheel has most of its mass at the rim, so it has a large $I$. A large $I$ resists any change in angular speed ($\tau = I\alpha$ gives a small $\alpha$ for a given torque) and stores a large kinetic energy $\tfrac12I\omega^2$, so the engine runs smoothly in spite of the jerky power strokes.

**(b)** The earth moves under the gravitational pull of the sun, which is a central force. Its torque about the sun is zero, so the angular momentum of the earth about the sun is conserved:
$$L = I\omega = mr^2\omega = \text{constant}$$
When the earth comes closer to the sun, $r$ decreases, so $I = mr^2$ decreases. To keep $L$ unchanged $\omega$ must increase. The orbital angular velocity (and speed $v = r\omega$) is therefore greatest at the nearest point (perihelion) and least at the farthest point; this is Kepler's second law, equal areas in equal times.

**(c)** The earth is acted on by no external torque in its spin, so $L = I\omega$ stays constant. If it shrinks, its radius $R$ falls, $I = \tfrac25MR^2$ decreases and $\omega$ increases:
$$I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2\;\Rightarrow\;\omega_2 = \omega_1\frac{I_1}{I_2}$$
Since $T = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega}$, the time of one rotation decreases: the day becomes shorter. For example, if the radius became half, $I$ would fall to one quarter, $\omega$ would increase four times and the day would be $24/4 = 6$ hours long.

@s 13
**(a)** The basic (differential) equation of SHM is
$$\frac{d^2x}{dt^2} = -\omega^2x \quad\text{or}\quad a = -\omega^2x$$
where $x$ is the displacement from the mean position and $\omega$ the angular frequency: the acceleration is proportional to the displacement and is directed towards the mean position.

**(b)** Let the displacement of the particle be $x = A\sin(\omega t + \phi)$, where $A$ is the amplitude.

*Velocity:* $v = \dfrac{dx}{dt} = A\omega\cos(\omega t + \phi)$. Using $\cos\theta = \sqrt{1 - \sin^2\theta}$,
$$v = A\omega\sqrt{1 - \frac{x^2}{A^2}} = \omega\sqrt{A^2 - x^2}$$
It is maximum ($A\omega$) at the mean position ($x = 0$) and zero at the extremes ($x = \pm A$).

*Acceleration:* $a = \dfrac{dv}{dt} = -A\omega^2\sin(\omega t + \phi) = -\omega^2x$.
It is maximum ($A\omega^2$) at the extremes and zero at the mean position; the negative sign shows it is always directed towards the mean position.

**(c)** Frequency $f = 550$ Hz (550 vibrations in 1 s).
Angular frequency: $\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi\times550 = 3456\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$ (about $3.46\times10^3$ rad/s).
Time period: $T = \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{550} = 1.82\times10^{-3}$ s.

**Or (a)** *Surface tension* is the force acting per unit length on an imaginary line drawn on the free surface of a liquid, at right angles to the line and tangential to the surface ($S = F/l$, unit N m⁻¹). Equivalently, it is the surface energy per unit area.

**Or (b)** *Bernoulli's theorem:* for the steady, streamline flow of an ideal (incompressible, non-viscous) liquid, the sum of the pressure energy, kinetic energy and potential energy per unit volume is the same at every point along a streamline:
$$P + \tfrac12\rho v^2 + \rho gh = \text{constant}$$

**Or (c)** For a sphere falling in a viscous liquid the terminal velocity is reached when weight = upthrust + viscous drag:
$$v_t = \frac{2r^2(\rho - \sigma)g}{9\eta}$$
where $\rho = 7800$ kg m⁻³ (steel), $\sigma = 940$ kg m⁻³ (oil), $\eta = 2.42$ N s m⁻², $r = 2\times10^{-3}$ m.
$$v_t = \frac{2\times(2\times10^{-3})^2\times(7800 - 940)\times10}{9\times2.42} = \frac{2\times4\times10^{-6}\times6860\times10}{21.78} = \frac{0.5488}{21.78}$$
$$v_t \approx 2.52\times10^{-2}\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$
The ball falls with a terminal velocity of about 2.5 cm/s.

@s 14
**(a)** An *adiabatic process* is a thermodynamic process in which no heat enters or leaves the system ($\Delta Q = 0$); pressure, volume and temperature all change together, as in the sudden compression of a gas in an insulated cylinder, so the work is done at the cost of the internal energy.

**(b)** Let one mole of an ideal gas expand adiabatically from $(P_1, V_1)$ to $(P_2, V_2)$. The adiabatic equation is $PV^{\gamma} = K$ (constant), $\gamma = C_p/C_v$. The work done in a small expansion $dV$ is $dW = P\,dV$, so
$$W = \int_{V_1}^{V_2}P\,dV = K\int_{V_1}^{V_2}V^{-\gamma}dV = K\left[\frac{V^{1-\gamma}}{1-\gamma}\right]_{V_1}^{V_2} = \frac{K}{1-\gamma}\left(V_2^{1-\gamma} - V_1^{1-\gamma}\right)$$
Now $K = P_1V_1^{\gamma} = P_2V_2^{\gamma}$, so $KV_2^{1-\gamma} = P_2V_2$ and $KV_1^{1-\gamma} = P_1V_1$. Therefore
$$W = \frac{P_2V_2 - P_1V_1}{1-\gamma} = \frac{P_1V_1 - P_2V_2}{\gamma - 1}$$
Using $PV = RT$: $W = \dfrac{R(T_1 - T_2)}{\gamma - 1}$. In an expansion $T_2 < T_1$, so $W$ is positive: the gas cools and does work at the expense of its internal energy; in a compression $W$ is negative and the gas warms.

**(c)** Entropy is defined through the change $dS = \dfrac{dQ}{T}$ (for a reversible process), so for a change from state $i$ to $f$
$$\Delta S = S_f - S_i = \int_i^f\frac{dQ}{T}$$
Its unit is J K⁻¹; for a reversible adiabatic process $\Delta S = 0$.

@s 15
**(a)** An *organ pipe* is a hollow pipe (open at both ends or closed at one end) in which a column of air is set into vibration by blowing air across or into it, giving stationary waves and a musical note; the flute and the whistle are examples.

**(b)** *Closed organ pipe* (closed at one end, open at the other). At the closed end the air cannot move, so there is always a **node**; at the open end the air is free to move, so there is always an **antinode**. Let the length be $L$ and the speed of sound $v$.

- *Fundamental (first harmonic):* one node at the closed end and one antinode at the open end, so $L = \dfrac{\lambda_1}{4}$, $\lambda_1 = 4L$ and $f_1 = \dfrac{v}{4L}$.
- *Third harmonic (first overtone):* $L = \dfrac{3\lambda_3}{4}$, $f_3 = \dfrac{3v}{4L} = 3f_1$.
- *Fifth harmonic (second overtone):* $L = \dfrac{5\lambda_5}{4}$, $f_5 = \dfrac{5v}{4L} = 5f_1$.

In general $f_n = \dfrac{(2n - 1)v}{4L} = (2n-1)f_1$, $n = 1, 2, 3,\dots$. Only **odd harmonics** are present, so the frequencies are in the ratio $1 : 3 : 5 : \dots$; the even harmonics are missing.

**(c)** The antinode at the open end does not lie exactly at the mouth of the pipe; it forms slightly outside, because the air just outside the open end also takes part in the vibration. The small distance by which the effective length of the air column exceeds the actual length of the pipe is the *end correction* $e$. For a pipe of inner radius $r$, $e \approx 0.6r$ (or $0.3D$), so the effective length is $L + e$ and $f_1 = \dfrac{v}{4(L + e)}$.

**Or (a)** *Doppler's effect:* the apparent frequency of a wave heard by the observer differs from the actual frequency of the source whenever there is relative motion between the source and the observer along the line joining them (the pitch rises while they approach each other and falls while they recede).

**Or (b)** Let the source $S$ be at rest and emit sound of frequency $f$ and wavelength $\lambda = v/f$; the observer moves with speed $v_o$ towards the source. Relative to the observer, the sound travels with speed $v + v_o$, while the wavelength remains $\lambda = v/f$ because the source is at rest. The observer therefore hears
$$f' = \frac{v + v_o}{\lambda} = \frac{v + v_o}{v}\,f = f\left(1 + \frac{v_o}{v}\right)$$
Since $f' > f$ the pitch is higher. For an observer moving away from the source $v_o$ is replaced by $-v_o$.

**Or (c)** The truck is first a moving observer and then a moving source. $v = 340$ m/s, $u = 120$ km/h $= \dfrac{120\times1000}{3600} = 33.33$ m/s, $f = 150$ kHz.

Frequency received by the approaching truck: $f_1 = f\,\dfrac{v + u}{v}$.
The truck reflects it as a source moving towards the detector: $f_2 = f_1\dfrac{v}{v - u}$.
$$f_2 = f\,\frac{v + u}{v - u} = 150\times\frac{340 + 33.33}{340 - 33.33} = 150\times\frac{373.33}{306.67} = 150\times1.2174$$
$$f_2 \approx 182.6\ \text{kHz}$$
The frequency reflected back to the detector is about 183 kHz (higher than the transmitted frequency).

@s 16
**(a)**

| Seebeck effect | Peltier effect |
|---|---|
| Production of an emf (and hence a current) in a closed circuit of two dissimilar metals when their two junctions are kept at different temperatures. | Absorption or evolution of heat at the junctions of two dissimilar metals when a current is passed through the circuit. |
| Heat is converted into electricity (thermocouple, thermopile). | Electricity is converted into a heat difference (thermoelectric refrigerator). |
| The emf depends on the temperature difference of the junctions. | The heat depends on the current ($\Pi I$, with $\Pi$ the Peltier coefficient) and is proportional to $I$, not $I^2$. |
| It is not reversible by itself. | It is reversible: reversing the current changes heating to cooling, unlike Joule heating. |

**(b)** In a thermocouple with the cold junction kept at $0^{\circ}$C and the hot junction at $\theta^{\circ}$C, the thermo-emf varies with temperature not linearly but parabolically:
$$e = a\theta + \tfrac12b\theta^2$$
where $a$ and $b$ are constants of the pair of metals. As $\theta$ rises, $e$ first increases, reaches a maximum at the *neutral temperature* and then decreases, becoming zero and reversing its sign at the *temperature of inversion*.

- *Neutral temperature:* the hot-junction temperature at which the emf is maximum, $\dfrac{de}{d\theta} = 0$, so $\theta_n = -\dfrac{a}{b}$. It depends only on the metals and not on the temperature of the cold junction.
- *Temperature of inversion:* the temperature at which the emf becomes zero again, $\theta_i = -\dfrac{2a}{b} = 2\theta_n$ (when the cold junction is at $0^{\circ}$C); the neutral temperature lies midway between the cold-junction temperature and the temperature of inversion.

For example, for copper–iron $\theta_n \approx 270^{\circ}$C and $\theta_i \approx 540^{\circ}$C.

**(a)** The Lorentz force on a charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ in a magnetic field $\vec B$ is
$$\vec F = q(\vec v\times\vec B),\qquad F = qvB\sin\theta$$
where $\theta$ is the angle between $\vec v$ and $\vec B$. (In the presence of an electric field as well, $\vec F = q\vec E + q(\vec v\times\vec B)$.)

**(b)** *Fleming's left hand rule:* stretch the forefinger, the middle finger and the thumb of the left hand mutually perpendicular to each other. If the forefinger points in the direction of the magnetic field and the middle finger in the direction of the current, then the thumb points in the direction of the force (motion) on the conductor.

**(c)** For the wire to be just self-supporting the upward magnetic force must balance its weight:
$$BIl = mg\;\Rightarrow\; I = \frac{(m/l)\,g}{B}$$
Mass per unit length $m/l = 1.2\ \text{g m}^{-1} = 1.2\times10^{-3}$ kg m⁻¹, $B = 0.6$ T, $g = 9.8$ m s⁻²:
$$I = \frac{1.2\times10^{-3}\times9.8}{0.6} = 1.96\times10^{-2}\ \text{A}$$
Resistance of the wire $R = 3.8\ \Omega\,\text{m}^{-1}\times0.05\ \text{m} = 0.19\ \Omega$.

**(a)** When an electron is projected normally (perpendicular) into a uniform electric field, the force $F = eE$ acts along the field (opposite to $\vec E$ for an electron), perpendicular to its initial velocity. The velocity component along the original direction remains constant while the transverse one grows uniformly with time, so the path is a **parabola**, just like a projectile in a gravitational field: $y = \dfrac{eE}{2mv^2}x^2$.

**(b)** No. It does not mean that there are no fields. An electron can pass undeviated through a region having crossed electric and magnetic fields, provided the electric force and the magnetic force on it are equal and opposite:
$$eE = evB\;\Rightarrow\; v = \frac{E}{B}$$
Only electrons with this speed go straight, as in a velocity selector (and in Thomson's experiment for $e/m$). The net force on it is zero though two fields are present. Another case is when the electron moves parallel to a pure magnetic field (see part (c)): then there is a field but no force.

**(c)** Yes. The magnetic force on a moving charge is $F = qvB\sin\theta$. It is zero if
- the electron moves **parallel or anti-parallel** to the magnetic field ($\theta = 0^{\circ}$ or $180^{\circ}$), so $\sin\theta = 0$; or
- the electron is **at rest** ($v = 0$); a magnetic field exerts no force on a stationary charge.

In such cases the electron is not deflected and keeps moving in a straight line.

@s 19
**(a)** *Rectification* is the process of converting an alternating current (voltage) into a direct (unidirectional) current (voltage), by using the one-way conduction of p-n junction diodes. A circuit that does this is a *rectifier*.

**(b)** *Full-wave rectifier (two diodes).* The ac supply is connected to the primary of a transformer whose secondary has a centre tap. The two ends of the secondary are joined to the anodes of the diodes $D_1$ and $D_2$; the cathodes are joined together and the load $R_L$ is connected between this common point and the centre tap.

- *Positive half cycle:* the upper end $A$ of the secondary is positive with respect to the centre tap, so $D_1$ is forward biased and conducts; $D_2$ is reverse biased and does not conduct. Current flows through $R_L$ from the cathode junction to the centre tap.
- *Negative half cycle:* the lower end $B$ is positive, so $D_2$ conducts and $D_1$ is off. The current again flows through $R_L$ in the **same direction**.

So current passes through the load during both half cycles, always in one direction. The output is a pulsating dc whose frequency is twice the input frequency ($2f$) and whose average value is $V_{dc} = \dfrac{2V_m}{\pi} = 0.637V_m$. A capacitor or LC filter smooths the ripple.

**(a)** *Conditions for sustained (permanent) interference:*
1. The two sources must be **coherent**: they must emit waves of the same frequency with a constant phase difference.
2. The sources should be monochromatic (a single wavelength) so that the fringes are sharp.
3. The amplitudes of the two waves should be equal or nearly equal; the dark fringes are then completely dark and the contrast is good.
4. The two sources must be very close together (small $d$) and the screen far from them (large $D$) so that the fringes are wide enough to be seen separately.
5. The waves must be polarised in the same plane (or unpolarised), otherwise they cannot interfere fully.

**(b)** Let two coherent slits $S_1$ and $S_2$, separated by $d$, be illuminated by monochromatic light of wavelength $\lambda$, and let the screen be at a distance $D$ from the slits ($D\gg d$). Consider a point $P$ at a distance $y$ from the central point $O$.

Path difference: $\Delta = S_2P - S_1P \approx d\sin\theta \approx d\tan\theta = \dfrac{yd}{D}$ (for small $\theta$).

*Bright fringes* need $\Delta = n\lambda$, so $y_n = \dfrac{n\lambda D}{d}$, $n = 0, 1, 2,\dots$
*Dark fringes* need $\Delta = (2n+1)\dfrac{\lambda}{2}$, so $y_n' = \dfrac{(2n+1)\lambda D}{2d}$.

The distance between two successive bright fringes:
$$\beta = y_{n+1} - y_n = \frac{(n+1)\lambda D}{d} - \frac{n\lambda D}{d} = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$
and between two successive dark fringes:
$$\beta' = y'_{n+1} - y'_n = \frac{\lambda D}{d}$$
Both are given by $\beta = \dfrac{\lambda D}{d}$, which does not depend on the order $n$. So all bright and dark fringes have the same width: they are equally spaced. (Each dark fringe lies exactly half way between two bright fringes.)

**(a)** Kirchhoff's first law (junction law): the algebraic sum of the currents meeting at a junction is zero, $\sum I = 0$. Its significance is that it expresses the **conservation of electric charge**: charge cannot accumulate at a junction in a steady current, so the total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving it.

**(b)** *Kirchhoff's second law (loop law):* in any closed loop of a network the algebraic sum of the emfs is equal to the algebraic sum of the products $IR$ in that loop: $\sum E = \sum IR$ (equivalently, the sum of potential changes around a loop is zero). It expresses the **conservation of energy**: a charge taken once round a closed loop returns to its starting potential, so the energy supplied by the cells equals the energy dissipated in the resistors.

*Sign convention:* an emf is positive when the loop passes through the cell from its negative to its positive terminal; an $IR$ term is positive when the loop runs in the direction of the current.

For the loop in the figure, going clockwise from $A$: $E_1 - E_2 = I(R_1 + R_2 + R_3)$ when the two cells oppose each other.

**(d)** A *meter bridge* is a practical form of the Wheatstone bridge used to find an unknown resistance. **Or (a)** Consider a series circuit with a resistor $R$, an inductor $L$ and a capacitor $C$ connected to $V = V_0\sin\omega t$. The same current $I$ flows through all three. The voltages: $V_R = IR$ (in phase with $I$), $V_L = IX_L$ (leads $I$ by $90^{\circ}$), $V_C = IX_C$ (lags $I$ by $90^{\circ}$), with $X_L = \omega L$, $X_C = \dfrac{1}{\omega C}$.

On the phasor diagram $V_L$ and $V_C$ are in opposite directions, so their resultant is $V_L - V_C$, perpendicular to $V_R$. The applied voltage is the phasor sum:
$$V^2 = V_R^2 + (V_L - V_C)^2 = I^2\left[R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2\right]$$
The impedance is $Z = \dfrac{V}{I}$:
$$Z = \sqrt{R^2 + (X_L - X_C)^2} = \sqrt{R^2 + \left(\omega L - \dfrac{1}{\omega C}\right)^2}$$
The phase angle between $V$ and $I$ is $\tan\phi = \dfrac{X_L - X_C}{R}$: the current lags if $X_L > X_C$, leads if $X_C > X_L$.

**Or (b)** The impedance is least, and the current greatest, when the reactances cancel: $X_L = X_C$, that is
$$\omega L = \frac{1}{\omega C}\;\Rightarrow\;\omega_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{LC}},\qquad f_0 = \frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{LC}}$$
At resonance $Z = R$, $\phi = 0$ (voltage and current in phase, power factor 1) and $I_{max} = V/R$.

**(a)** *Bohr's postulates* (for the hydrogen atom):
1. *Stationary orbits:* an electron revolves round the nucleus only in certain allowed circular orbits without radiating energy; these are the stable (stationary) states.
2. *Quantum condition:* the allowed orbits are those in which the angular momentum of the electron is an integral multiple of $\dfrac{h}{2\pi}$: $mvr = \dfrac{nh}{2\pi}$, $n = 1, 2, 3,\dots$
3. *Frequency condition:* radiation is emitted or absorbed only when the electron jumps from one orbit to another, the photon energy being the difference of the energies: $hf = E_i - E_f$.

**(b)** Let an electron of mass $m$ and charge $e$ revolve in the $n^{th}$ orbit of radius $r_n$ with speed $v_n$ round the nucleus of charge $+e$. The electrostatic force provides the centripetal force:
$$\frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{e^2}{r^2}\quad(1)$$
Bohr's quantum condition:
$$mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi}\;\Rightarrow\;v = \frac{nh}{2\pi mr}\quad(2)$$
Putting (2) in (1):
$$\frac{m}{r}\frac{n^2h^2}{4\pi^2m^2r^2} = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0r^2}\;\Rightarrow\;r_n = \frac{\varepsilon_0h^2}{\pi me^2}\,n^2$$
so $r_n \propto n^2$. For $n = 1$ this is the Bohr radius $a_0 = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0h^2}{\pi me^2} = 0.529$ Å $= 5.29\times10^{-11}$ m, and $r_n = 0.529\,n^2$ Å: the orbits are spaced as $1 : 4 : 9 : 16\dots$

**(c)** For an electron accelerated through $V$, $eV = \tfrac12mv^2$, so $p = \sqrt{2meV}$ and
$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p} = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2meV}} = \frac{6.62\times10^{-34}}{\sqrt{2\times9.1\times10^{-31}\times1.6\times10^{-19}\times500}}$$
The quantity under the root is $1.456\times10^{-46}$, its square root $1.207\times10^{-23}$, so
$$\lambda = \frac{6.62\times10^{-34}}{1.207\times10^{-23}} = 5.49\times10^{-11}\ \text{m}\approx0.055\ \text{nm}$$
The de Broglie wavelength is about 0.55 Å (comparable to X-ray wavelengths and atomic spacing, which is why electrons are diffracted by crystals).

**Or (a)** *Radioactivity* is the spontaneous disintegration of the nuclei of certain heavy, unstable elements (uranium, radium, polonium), with the emission of $\alpha$-particles, $\beta$-particles or $\gamma$-rays, without any external influence of temperature, pressure or chemical combination.

**Or (b)** *Law of radioactive decay:* the rate of decay at any instant is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present. If $N$ nuclei are present at time $t$,
$$-\frac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N$$
where $\lambda$ is the decay constant. Rearranging and integrating,
$$\int_{N_0}^{N}\frac{dN}{N} = -\lambda\int_0^t dt\;\Rightarrow\;\ln\frac{N}{N_0} = -\lambda t$$
$$N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$$
where $N_0$ is the number of nuclei at $t = 0$. The number of undecayed nuclei decreases exponentially. Putting $N = N_0/2$ gives the half-life $T_{1/2} = \dfrac{0.693}{\lambda}$.

**Or (c)** The decay curve ($N$ against $t$) is an exponential: $N$ falls by the same fraction in each equal interval but approaches zero only asymptotically, so strictly it never reaches zero. An isotope with a **small decay constant $\lambda$** (long half-life, mean life $\tau = 1/\lambda$) has a curve that falls very slowly, so it stays radioactive for a very long time; one with large $\lambda$ falls steeply. The slope of the curve at any point gives the activity. The curve of the longest-lived isotopes is therefore the flattest, which is why radioisotopes of long life are chosen for dating (carbon-14, uranium-238) and why nuclear waste needs storage for ages.
