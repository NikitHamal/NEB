@s 12
**(a)** The moment of inertia of a body about an axis is the sum of the products of the mass of each particle and the square of its perpendicular distance from the axis: $I=\sum m_ir_i^2$ (unit kg m²). It is the rotational analogue of mass: the torque needed for a given angular acceleration is $\tau=I\alpha$.
*Factors on which it depends:* (1) the mass of the body; (2) the distribution of the mass about the axis, i.e. the distances of the particles from the axis; (also the position and orientation of the axis and the shape and size of the body).

**(b)** Consider a thin uniform rod of mass $M$ and length $L$, and take the axis through its centre, perpendicular to the rod. Let the mass per unit length be $\lambda=\dfrac{M}{L}$. Take a small element of length $dx$ at a distance $x$ from the axis; its mass is $dm=\dfrac{M}{L}\,dx$ and its moment of inertia is $dI=x^2\,dm=\dfrac{M}{L} x^2\,dx$. Integrating over the whole rod from $x=-\dfrac{L}{2}$ to $+\dfrac{L}{2}$:
$$I=\frac{M}{L}\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}x^2\,dx=\frac{M}{L}\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-L/2}^{L/2}=\frac{M}{L}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{L^3}{8}=\boxed{\frac{ML^2}{12}}$$

**(c)** The principle of conservation of angular momentum: if no external torque acts on a system, its total angular momentum remains constant, $L=I\omega=\text{constant}$. So when $I$ decreases, $\omega$ increases (a skater spinning faster by pulling her arms in; a diver somersaulting in a tuck).

@s 13
**(a)** The surface tension of a liquid is the force acting per unit length on an imaginary line drawn on the free surface of the liquid, the force being perpendicular to the line and tangential to the surface: $T=\dfrac{F}{l}$ (unit N m⁻¹).

**(b)** Take a rectangular wire frame with a sliding wire of length $l$ and dip it in soap solution, so that a film is formed. The film has two free surfaces, and the force due to surface tension on the wire is $F=2Tl$, directed inwards. Pull the wire outwards slowly through a distance $x$. The work done against surface tension is
$$W=F\,x=2Tl\,x$$
The increase in the surface area is $\Delta A=2lx$ (two surfaces), so $W=T\,\Delta A$. This work is stored as the surface energy of the film, so
$$\text{surface energy per unit area}=\frac{W}{\Delta A}=T$$
Thus the surface tension is numerically equal to the surface energy per unit area (J m⁻² and N m⁻¹ are the same unit). $\blacksquare$

**(c)** Capillarity is the phenomenon of the rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube (capillary tube) dipped in it. The height of rise is $h=\dfrac{2T\cos\theta}{r\rho g}$, so it is larger for a narrower tube; water rises ($\theta<90^\circ$) and mercury falls ($\theta>90^\circ$).
*Examples:* (1) oil rises through the wick of a lamp or a stove; (2) ink is soaked up by a blotting paper; (3) sap and water rise in the trunks of plants. (A towel absorbing water is another.)

@s 14
**(a)** Newton assumed that the compressions and rarefactions in a sound wave take place slowly, so the temperature stays constant (an isothermal change) and the elasticity of air is its isothermal bulk modulus $E=P$. This gave $v=\sqrt{\dfrac{P}{\rho}}=280$ m s⁻¹ in air at 0 °C, against the observed 332 m s⁻¹.
*Laplace's correction:* the changes in air are so rapid, and air is such a poor conductor of heat, that there is no time for the heat to flow away; the process is adiabatic, not isothermal. So the elasticity to be used is the adiabatic bulk modulus. For an adiabatic change $PV^\gamma=\text{constant}$; differentiating, $\gamma PV^{\gamma-1}dV+V^\gamma dP=0$, so
$$E=-\frac{dP}{dV/V}=\gamma P$$
$$\boxed{v=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma P}{\rho}}}$$
where $\gamma=\dfrac{C_p}{C_v}=1.4$ for air. At NTP: $v=\sqrt{1.4\times280^2}=331.3$ m s⁻¹, in agreement with the experiment.

**(b)** $v\propto\sqrt{T}$ (absolute temperature). $T_1=27+273=300$ K. The new speed is $v_2=v_1+0.5v_1=1.5v_1$.
$$\frac{v_2}{v_1}=\sqrt{\frac{T_2}{T_1}}\ \Rightarrow\ 1.5=\sqrt{\frac{T_2}{300}}\ \Rightarrow\ T_2=2.25\times300=675\ \text{K}$$
$$t_2=675-273=\boxed{402\ ^\circ\text{C}}$$

@s 15
**(a)** Harmonics are the notes whose frequencies are integral multiples of the fundamental frequency ($f,\ 2f,\ 3f,\dots$). The fundamental is the first harmonic, the note of frequency $2f$ is the second harmonic, and so on.

**(b)** *Open organ pipe:* the pipe is open at both ends, so there is an antinode (A) at each end, and the air column vibrates in the following modes (the speed of sound is $v$ and the length is $L$).
**Fundamental mode (first harmonic):** one node in the middle between the two antinodes: $L=\dfrac{\lambda}{2}$, so $\lambda_1=2L$ and
$$f_1=\frac{v}{2L}$$
2. *Second harmonic (first overtone):* two nodes and three antinodes: $L=\lambda_2$, so
$$f_2=\frac{v}{L}=2f_1$$
3. *Third harmonic (second overtone):* three nodes: $L=\dfrac{3\lambda_3}{2}$, so
$$f_3=\frac{3v}{2L}=3f_1$$
In general, for the $n$th harmonic $f_n=\dfrac{nv}{2L}$ with $n=1,2,3,\dots$, i.e. an open pipe contains **all** harmonics ($f:2f:3f:4f\dots$), and so its note is rich in quality.

**(c)** The antinode in an open pipe does not form exactly at the open end but a little outside it, because the air just outside the end is also set into vibration. The small correction $e$ that must be added to the length of the pipe, so that the effective length is $L+e$ for each open end, is the *end correction*. It is found that $e\approx0.6\,r$, where $r$ is the radius of the pipe, so for an open pipe $f_1=\dfrac{v}{2(L+2e)}=\dfrac{v}{2(L+1.2r)}$ and for a closed pipe $f_1=\dfrac{v}{4(L+0.6r)}$.

@s 16
**(a)** Diffraction of light is the bending of light around the edges of an obstacle or aperture, and its spreading into the region of the geometrical shadow, when the size of the obstacle or aperture is comparable to the wavelength of the light. It produces bright and dark fringes at the edges of a shadow.

**(b)** The necessary condition for diffraction is that the size of the obstacle or the width of the aperture (slit) must be comparable to the wavelength of the light ($a\approx\lambda$, of the order of $10^{-6}$ m or less). If the aperture is very large compared with $\lambda$, light travels in a straight line and there is no noticeable diffraction.

**(c)(i)** Coherent sources are two sources of light which emit waves of the same frequency (wavelength), of nearly the same amplitude, and with a constant phase difference between them (which may be zero) at all times. Two independent bulbs are not coherent; two slits illuminated by the same source are.

**(c)(ii)** Let the path difference between the two waves at a point be $\Delta x$ and the phase difference $\phi=\dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta x$.
- *Constructive interference* (bright fringe, maximum intensity): the waves arrive in phase, $\phi=2n\pi$, i.e. the path difference $\Delta x=n\lambda$ ($n=0,1,2,\dots$). The resultant amplitude is $a_1+a_2$.
- *Destructive interference* (dark fringe, minimum intensity): the waves arrive in opposite phase, $\phi=(2n+1)\pi$, i.e. $\Delta x=(2n+1)\dfrac{\lambda}{2}$ ($n=0,1,2,\dots$). The resultant amplitude is $|a_1-a_2|$, which is zero for equal amplitudes.

@s 17
**(a)** *Principle of the potentiometer:* when a constant current flows through a wire of uniform cross-section, the potential drop across any length of the wire is directly proportional to that length, $V\propto l$ (more exactly $V=(IR/L)\,l=\phi\,l$, where $\phi$ is the potential gradient).

**(b)** A voltmeter has a large but finite resistance, so when it is joined across a cell it draws a small current and reads the terminal potential difference $V=E-Ir$, which is less than the emf $E$. At the balance (null) point of a potentiometer the galvanometer shows no deflection, so no current is drawn from the cell, the internal resistance has no effect and the potentiometer measures the true emf. In addition, the potentiometer is more sensitive and accurate, because a long wire gives a small potential gradient and a small difference of emf can be detected, and it needs no calibration of a scale reading. Hence it is preferred.

**(c) Kirchhoff's laws:**
1. *Current law (junction law):* the algebraic sum of the currents meeting at a junction is zero, $\sum I=0$ (the total current entering a junction equals the total current leaving it). It is based on the conservation of charge.
2. *Voltage law (loop law):* in any closed loop of a circuit the algebraic sum of the emfs equals the algebraic sum of the products $IR$ round the loop, $\sum E=\sum IR$ (equivalently the sum of the potential changes round a closed loop is zero). It is based on the conservation of energy.

**Or — (a) Biot-Savart's law.** It gives the magnetic field $d\vec B$ at a point $P$ due to a small current element $I\,d\vec l$. *Statement:* the magnitude of the magnetic field $dB$ at a point $P$ at a distance $r$ from the element is (i) directly proportional to the current $I$, (ii) directly proportional to the length $dl$ of the element, (iii) directly proportional to $\sin\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between $d\vec l$ and the line joining the element to $P$, and (iv) inversely proportional to $r^2$:
$$dB=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,dl\sin\theta}{r^2}\qquad\text{or}\qquad d\vec B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2}$$
Here $\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}$ T m A⁻¹. The direction is perpendicular to the plane containing $d\vec l$ and $\vec r$, given by the right hand screw rule (or the right hand thumb rule). The field is zero along the line of the element ($\theta=0$) and greatest at $\theta=90^\circ$.

**(b)** Consider a circular coil of radius $a$ with $N$ turns carrying a current $I$. Take a small element $dl$ on the coil. Every element is at a distance $r=a$ from the centre $O$ and is perpendicular to the radius, so $\theta=90^\circ$, $\sin\theta=1$. The field at the centre due to the element is
$$dB=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,dl}{a^2}$$
directed perpendicular to the plane of the coil. The field due to every element has the same direction, so the magnitudes add. For one turn $\sum dl=2\pi a$:
$$B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I}{a^2}\cdot2\pi a=\frac{\mu_0I}{2a}$$
For a coil of $N$ turns,
$$\boxed{B=\frac{\mu_0NI}{2a}}$$

@s 18
**(a)** *Conversion of a galvanometer into an ammeter.* A galvanometer is a sensitive instrument which gives a full-scale deflection for a small current $I_g$ only. To measure a larger current $I$, a low resistance wire called a *shunt* of resistance $S$ is connected **in parallel** with the galvanometer coil (resistance $G$). Most of the current $(I-I_g)$ passes through the shunt and only $I_g$ passes through the galvanometer, so the full-scale deflection corresponds to the current $I$. The potential difference across the two parallel paths is the same:
$$I_gG=(I-I_g)S\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{S=\frac{I_g\,G}{I-I_g}}$$
The effective resistance of the ammeter is $\dfrac{GS}{G+S}$, which is smaller than $S$, and so very small. For example, a galvanometer with $G=50\ \Omega$ and $I_g=2$ mA is made a 1 A ammeter by a shunt $S=\dfrac{0.002\times50}{1-0.002}=0.1\ \Omega$ in parallel.

**(b)** An ammeter is used to measure the current flowing in a branch of a circuit, so the same current must pass through it; this is possible only if it is placed in series with the circuit element, so that the current has no other path. Also the ammeter has a very small resistance. If it were connected in parallel with a component, it would provide a path of almost zero resistance (a short circuit), a very large current would flow through it, and the ammeter would be damaged and the circuit current would be changed. In series, its small resistance hardly changes the current that is to be measured.

@s 19
**(a)** *Einstein's photoelectric equation.* According to Einstein's quantum theory, light consists of packets of energy called photons, each of energy $E=h\nu$. When a photon is absorbed by an electron in the metal, part of its energy, equal to the work function $\phi_0$, is used to free the electron from the surface, and the rest appears as the maximum kinetic energy of the emitted electron:
$$\boxed{h\nu=\phi_0+\tfrac12mv_{\max}^2=h\nu_0+K_{\max}}$$
It explains why: the maximum kinetic energy rises with the frequency but not with the intensity; there is a threshold frequency below which there is no emission; and the emission is instantaneous.
*Threshold frequency* ($\nu_0$) is the minimum frequency of the incident light below which no photoelectrons are emitted from a metal surface, however great the intensity: $\nu_0=\dfrac{\phi_0}{h}$.

**(b)** The maximum kinetic energy is $K_{\max}=E-\phi_0=5-1.8=3.2$ eV
$$K_{\max}=3.2\times1.6\times10^{-19}=5.12\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}$$
$$\tfrac12mv^2=K_{\max}\ \Rightarrow\ v=\sqrt{\frac{2K_{\max}}{m}}=\sqrt{\frac{2\times5.12\times10^{-19}}{9.1\times10^{-31}}}=\sqrt{1.125\times10^{12}}\approx\boxed{1.06\times10^{6}\ \text{m s}^{-1}}$$

**Or — (a)** A rectifier is a device (usually made of p-n junction diodes) that converts an alternating current (voltage) into a unidirectional (direct) current (voltage).

**(b) Full wave rectifier with two diodes.** The circuit has a transformer whose secondary winding is centre-tapped, two identical junction diodes $D_1$ and $D_2$, and a load resistance $R_L$. The ends of the secondary are joined to the p-sides (anodes) of $D_1$ and $D_2$, and the n-sides (cathodes) are joined together; the load is connected between this common point and the centre tap.
*Working:* during the positive half-cycle of the input, the upper end of the secondary is positive with respect to the centre tap: $D_1$ is forward biased and conducts, while $D_2$ is reverse biased and does not. The current flows through $D_1$ and the load, from the common cathode point to the centre tap. During the negative half-cycle the lower end becomes positive: $D_2$ conducts and $D_1$ is off, and the current again flows through $R_L$ **in the same direction**. Hence the current through the load has the same direction during both half-cycles and the output is a unidirectional pulsating dc of frequency $2f$ (twice the input frequency). A filter (capacitor) is added to smooth it. The efficiency is about 81.2%, twice that of the half wave rectifier.

**(c)** The input waveform is a continuous sine wave; the output is a series of positive half-sine pulses (both half-cycles appear on the same side of the axis), as drawn below.

@s 20
**(a)** Simple harmonic motion is the to-and-fro (periodic) motion of a particle in which the acceleration is directly proportional to the displacement from the mean (equilibrium) position and is always directed towards that position: $a=-\omega^2y$.

**(b)** For $Y=a\sin(\omega t-kx)$ the velocity is the first derivative with respect to time:
$$v=\frac{dY}{dt}=a\omega\cos(\omega t-kx)$$
and the acceleration is the second derivative:
$$\text{acceleration}=\frac{d^2Y}{dt^2}=-a\omega^2\sin(\omega t-kx)=\boxed{-\omega^2Y}$$
The acceleration is proportional to the displacement and has the opposite sign; the maximum acceleration is $a\omega^2$.

**(c)** Let a mass $m$ be suspended from a light helical spring of force constant (spring constant) $k$. In equilibrium the spring is stretched by $e$, so $mg=ke$. Now pull the mass down a further distance $y$ and release it. At that displacement the restoring force of the spring (Hooke's law) is $k(e+y)$ upwards, and the weight is $mg$ downwards, so the net force is
$$F=mg-k(e+y)=mg-ke-ky=-ky$$
By Newton's second law, $ma=-ky$, so
$$a=-\frac{k}{m}y=-\omega^2y,\qquad\omega^2=\frac{k}{m}$$
The acceleration is proportional to the displacement and directed towards the equilibrium position; this is the condition of SHM. $\blacksquare$ The time period is
$$T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega}=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{m}{k}}$$

**(d)** $m=200\ \text{g}=0.2$ kg, amplitude $A=20\ \text{mm}=0.02$ m, $F_{\max}=0.8$ N. The maximum force is $F_{\max}=m\omega^2A$, so

$$v_{\max}=A\omega=0.02\times14.14=\boxed{0.283\ \text{m s}^{-1}}$$

**(a)** The r.m.s. (root mean square) value of an alternating current is the value of the steady direct current which produces the same heating effect in a given resistor in a given time as the alternating current does; it is the square root of the mean of the squares of the instantaneous current over a cycle: $I_{rms}=\sqrt{\overline{i^2}}$.

**(b)** For a sinusoidal current $i=I_0\sin\omega t$ the mean of $i^2$ over a cycle is $\dfrac{I_0^2}{2}$, so
$$I_{rms}=\frac{I_0}{\sqrt{2}}=0.707\,I_0\qquad\text{and similarly}\qquad V_{rms}=\frac{V_0}{\sqrt{2}}$$
where $I_0$ is the peak value. (The mains 220 V is the rms value; the peak is $220\sqrt{2}=311$ V.)

**(c)** Let an alternating emf $V=V_0\sin\omega t$ be applied across a pure inductor of inductance $L$ (negligible resistance). The current $i$ varies, so a back emf $-L\dfrac{di}{dt}$ is induced. Since there is no resistance, the applied voltage must balance the back emf:
$$V_0\sin\omega t=L\frac{di}{dt}\ \Rightarrow\ di=\frac{V_0}{L}\sin\omega t\,dt$$
Integrating,
$$i=-\frac{V_0}{\omega L}\cos\omega t=\frac{V_0}{\omega L}\sin\left(\omega t-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=I_0\sin\left(\omega t-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)$$
where $I_0=\dfrac{V_0}{\omega L}=\dfrac{V_0}{X_L}$ and $X_L=\omega L$ is the inductive reactance. Comparing with $V=V_0\sin\omega t$, the current lags behind the voltage by a phase angle $\dfrac{\pi}{2}$ (90°). $\blacksquare$

**(a)** A cross field is a region in which an electric field and a magnetic field exist together and are perpendicular to each other (crossed fields), e.g. $\vec E\perp\vec B$ in the velocity selector.

**(b)** Let an electron of charge $e$ and mass $m$ enter a uniform magnetic field $B$ with a velocity $v$ perpendicular to the field. The magnetic force $F=evB$ is always perpendicular to the velocity (and to the field), so it does no work: it changes only the direction, not the speed, and acts as a centripetal force. A particle that is acted upon by a constant-magnitude force perpendicular to its velocity moves in a circle (in the plane perpendicular to $B$) of radius $r$:
$$evB=\frac{mv^2}{r}\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{r=\frac{mv}{eB}}$$
The radius is constant, so the path is a circle. The time period is
$$T=\frac{2\pi r}{v}=\frac{2\pi}{v}\cdot\frac{mv}{eB}=\boxed{\frac{2\pi m}{eB}}$$
The period is independent of the speed and radius; this is the principle of the cyclotron. $\blacksquare$
