**1. C.** Carbohydrates, mainly glucose, are broken down in respiration to release energy quickly. Lipids store more energy but release it slowly.

**2. B.** Almost all enzymes are globular proteins (a few catalytic RNAs, the ribozymes, are exceptions).

**3. C.** Mitochondria make most of the cell's ATP by aerobic respiration.

**4. B.** Schleiden (1838, plants) and Schwann (1839, animals) proposed that all organisms are made of cells; Virchow added that cells arise from pre-existing cells.

**5. A.** Plant cell walls are made of cellulose. Chitin is in fungal walls and peptidoglycan in bacterial walls.

|---|---|---|
| Cell wall | Present (cellulose), outside the plasma membrane | Absent |
| Plastids | Present (chloroplasts for photosynthesis) | Absent |
| Vacuole | One large central vacuole | Small, temporary or absent |
| Centrioles | Absent in higher plants | Present |
| Shape | Fixed, usually rectangular | Irregular or rounded |
| Reserve food | Starch | Glycogen |

@s 12
**Enzyme (4 marks).** An enzyme is a biological catalyst, usually a protein, which speeds up a biochemical reaction by lowering its activation energy without being used up.

*Lock and key model* (Emil Fischer, 1894): the enzyme has a specific region called the **active site**, whose shape is complementary to the shape of its **substrate**, as a key fits only its own lock.
1. The substrate binds to the active site and forms the **enzyme-substrate complex**.
2. The enzyme changes the substrate into the **products** at the active site (bonds are broken or formed).
3. The products leave and the free enzyme is ready to act again.

This explains the specificity of enzymes: a substrate of a different shape cannot fit. The *induced fit* model of Koshland later added that the active site changes its shape slightly when the substrate binds.

@s 13
**Fluid mosaic model (4 marks).** Proposed by Singer and Nicolson (1972). The plasma membrane is a **fluid** double layer of phospholipids in which proteins float like a **mosaic**.
- **Phospholipid bilayer:** each molecule has a hydrophilic (water-loving) head facing outward and a hydrophobic (water-hating) tail facing inward. The layers move sideways, so the membrane is fluid.
- **Proteins:** integral (intrinsic) proteins span the bilayer and form channels and carriers; peripheral (extrinsic) proteins lie on its surface.
- **Cholesterol:** lies between the phospholipids in animal cells and controls the fluidity.
- **Carbohydrates:** attached to lipids (glycolipids) and proteins (glycoproteins) on the outer surface, they act in cell recognition.

The membrane is selectively permeable and controls what enters and leaves the cell.

@s 14
**Bryophytes (4 marks).** Bryophytes (mosses and liverworts) are the "amphibians of the plant kingdom".
- Small, simple, non-vascular plants without true roots, stems or leaves; they are anchored by rhizoids.
- They grow in damp, shady places, and need water for fertilization because the male gametes swim.
- The dominant, visible plant is the haploid **gametophyte**; the **sporophyte** (capsule on a stalk) is dependent on it.
- Sex organs are multicellular: antheridia (male) and archegonia (female).
- Spores are produced in the capsule and germinate into a protonema. *Examples:* *Marchantia* (liverwort) and *Funaria* (moss).

@s 15
**Gram staining (4 marks).** Gram staining, devised by Hans Christian Gram (1884), is a differential staining technique that separates bacteria into two groups by the reaction of their cell walls to crystal violet and a counterstain (safranin).

| Point | Gram-positive | Gram-negative |
|---|---|---|
| Colour after staining | Purple or violet | Pink or red |
| Cell wall | Thick peptidoglycan layer | Thin peptidoglycan layer |
| Outer membrane | Absent | Present (with lipopolysaccharide) |
| Effect of alcohol | Stain is retained | Stain is washed out |
| Examples | *Staphylococcus*, *Bacillus* | *E. coli*, *Salmonella* |

@s 16
**Ten percent law (4 marks).** *Law* (Lindeman, 1942): only about 10% of the energy of one trophic level is passed on to the next level; the rest is lost as heat in respiration or remains unused or as waste.

$$\text{Producers} = 10{,}000\ \text{J}$$
$$\text{Primary consumers} = 10\%\ \text{of}\ 10{,}000 = 1{,}000\ \text{J}$$
$$\text{Secondary consumers} = 10\%\ \text{of}\ 1{,}000 = 100\ \text{J}$$
$$\text{Tertiary consumers} = 10\%\ \text{of}\ 100 = 10\ \text{J}$$

Because so little energy remains at the top, food chains seldom have more than four or five trophic levels.

### Group C

@s 17
**(a) Watson and Crick model (4 marks).** James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the double helix model of DNA in 1953, using the X-ray data of Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins.
- DNA is made of **two polynucleotide chains** wound round each other in a right-handed double helix, running in opposite directions (**antiparallel**: 5' to 3' and 3' to 5').
- The **sugar-phosphate backbones** are on the outside; the nitrogenous bases are inside.
- The bases pair by **hydrogen bonds**: adenine with thymine (two bonds) and guanine with cytosine (three bonds). So A = T and G = C in amount (Chargaff's rule).
- One complete turn is 3.4 nm long and has 10 base pairs, with 0.34 nm between neighbouring pairs; the helix is 2 nm wide.

**(b) DNA and RNA (4 marks).**

| Point | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Bases | A, T, G, C | A, U, G, C (uracil instead of thymine) |
| Strands | Usually double-stranded helix | Usually single-stranded |
| Location | Nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplast | Nucleus, cytoplasm and ribosomes |
| Function | Stores and transmits genetic information | Protein synthesis (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA) |
| Stability | Stable | Less stable, short-lived |

@s 18
**(a) Nitrogen cycle (4 marks).** Air is about 78% nitrogen, but most organisms cannot use it directly. The nitrogen cycle moves it between the air, soil and living things in four main steps.
1. **Nitrogen fixation:** atmospheric $N_2$ is converted to ammonia by *Rhizobium* (in legume root nodules), *Azotobacter* and cyanobacteria such as *Nostoc*, and to nitrates by lightning.
2. **Nitrification:** *Nitrosomonas* oxidizes ammonia to nitrite, and *Nitrobacter* oxidizes nitrite to nitrate, which plants absorb.
3. **Assimilation:** plants use nitrates to make amino acids and proteins, and animals get nitrogen by eating plants.
4. **Ammonification and denitrification:** decomposers turn dead matter and wastes back into ammonia; *Pseudomonas* then converts nitrates back into $N_2$, which returns to the air.

**(b) Bacteriophage (4 marks).** A bacteriophage is a virus that infects bacteria. The T4 phage has a **head** (a protein coat, the capsid, enclosing double-stranded DNA), a **collar**, a hollow **tail** with a contractile sheath, and a **base plate** with tail fibres that attach to the host.

*Lytic cycle:*
1. **Attachment:** the tail fibres attach to receptors on the bacterial wall.
2. **Penetration:** the sheath contracts and the DNA is injected; the empty capsid stays outside.
3. **Biosynthesis:** the phage DNA takes over the host and makes phage DNA and proteins.
4. **Assembly and release:** new phages are assembled; the cell bursts (lysis) and releases about 100 to 200 phages to infect others.
