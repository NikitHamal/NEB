@s 12
**Differences between diffusion and osmosis.**

| Basis | Diffusion | Osmosis |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Movement of molecules (solid, liquid or gas) from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration | Movement of solvent (water) molecules from a region of lower solute concentration (higher water potential) to higher solute concentration through a semipermeable membrane |
| Membrane | A membrane is not necessary | A semipermeable (selectively permeable) membrane is essential |
| Medium | Occurs in all three states of matter | Occurs only in liquids (solvent) |
| Particles moving | Both solute and solvent can move | Only the solvent moves |
| Direction | Down the concentration gradient of the diffusing substance | Down the water potential gradient (towards the lower water potential) |
| Pressure | No pressure is developed | Develops osmotic pressure (turgor, osmotic pressure) |
| Example | Spreading of the smell of a perfume; exchange of O₂ and CO₂ in leaves | Absorption of water by root hairs; swelling of raisins in water |

**Or.** *Transpiration* is the loss of water in the form of vapour from the aerial parts of a living plant, mainly through the stomata of the leaves.
*Types:*
1. **Stomatal transpiration:** about 90 to 95 percent of the total loss, through the stomata (on the lower surface of dorsiventral leaves). The stomata open in light as the guard cells become turgid, and close at night or in water stress.
2. **Cuticular transpiration:** 5 to 10 percent, through the thin cuticle on the epidermis of leaves and stems; it is important mainly when the stomata are closed and in plants with a thin cuticle.
3. **Lenticular transpiration:** about 0.1 percent, through lenticels, the small pores in the bark of woody stems and fruits.
4. **Bark transpiration** (sometimes included): a very small loss through the bark of stems of woody plants. Transpiration helps to cool the leaf, to pull water and minerals up the xylem (the transpiration pull) and to keep the cells turgid.

@s 13
**Applications of polyploidy.** *Polyploidy* is the condition of having more than two sets of chromosomes (3n, 4n, ...), produced by colchicine or by failure of spindle formation. It is very useful in crop improvement and breeding:
1. **Larger size and higher yield:** autopolyploids often have bigger flowers, fruits and seeds with more food (tetraploid tomato, maize, grapes, rice).
2. **Seedless fruits:** triploid plants cannot form normal gametes, so triploid watermelon, banana and grapes are seedless and sweeter.
3. **New species / creation of fertile hybrids:** allopolyploidy overcomes the sterility of hybrids: *Raphanobrassica* (radish × cabbage), triticale (wheat × rye), and bread wheat is a hexaploid.
4. **Increased vigour and hybrid vigour (heterosis),** which is fixed because the polyploid is stable.
5. **Disease and stress resistance:** polyploids are more resistant to disease, pests and cold, and are better adapted to extreme environments.
6. **Increased content of useful substances:** more vitamins, proteins and oil in forage, sugar in beet and alkaloids in medicinal plants.
7. **Evolutionary study:** polyploidy explains the origin of many species and is used to restore the fertility of wide crosses.

@s 14
*Co-dominance* is the phenomenon in which both alleles of a gene pair are fully expressed in the heterozygote; neither allele is dominant over the other, so the F₁ shows the phenotypes of both parents together.

*Example: ABO blood groups in humans.* The gene $I$ has three alleles: $I^A$ (antigen A), $I^B$ (antigen B) and $i$ (no antigen). $I^A$ and $I^B$ are dominant over $i$, but they are co-dominant with each other. A person of genotype $I^AI^B$ has both antigen A and antigen B on the red blood cells, giving blood group AB; both alleles express themselves equally.

| Genotype | $I^AI^A$ or $I^Ai$ | $I^BI^B$ or $I^Bi$ | $I^AI^B$ | $ii$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Blood group | A | B | AB | O |

A cross between a group A heterozygote ($I^Ai$) and a group B heterozygote ($I^Bi$) gives the four types in the ratio 1 AB : 1 A : 1 B : 1 O. Other examples: roan coat colour in cattle (red × white gives roan with both red and white hairs) and the AB blood group. Co-dominance differs from incomplete dominance, where the heterozygote shows an intermediate (blended) phenotype such as pink flowers in *Mirabilis*.

@s 15
*Meristem* is a group of young, undifferentiated living cells that retain the power of continuous cell division and give rise to new cells that later differentiate into permanent tissues.

**Characteristics of meristematic cells:**
- They are small, spherical, oval or polygonal, and are closely packed without intercellular spaces.
- The cell wall is thin, elastic and made of cellulose.
- The cytoplasm is dense with a large, prominent nucleus.
- Vacuoles are absent or very small; no reserve food and no ergastic substances.
- Plastids are in the proplastid stage, and metabolic activity is very high.

**Types of meristem:**
1. *On the basis of origin:* promeristem (primordial), primary meristem (derived from the promeristem; protoderm, procambium and ground meristem) and secondary meristem (formed from permanent tissue that regains the power of division, e.g. cork cambium).
2. *On the basis of position:* **apical** meristem (at the tips of roots and shoots; increases the length), **intercalary** meristem (at the base of nodes or leaves, as in grasses; increases the length locally) and **lateral** meristem (vascular cambium and cork cambium; increases the girth).

*Hormones related to male reproduction:*
1. **FSH (follicle stimulating hormone)** from the anterior pituitary: stimulates the Sertoli cells and spermatogenesis.
2. **LH / ICSH (interstitial cell stimulating hormone)** from the anterior pituitary: stimulates the Leydig (interstitial) cells to secrete testosterone.

4. **Inhibin** from the Sertoli cells: inhibits the secretion of FSH (negative feedback).
5. **GnRH** from the hypothalamus: stimulates the release of FSH and LH.

@s 18
*Osmosis* is the movement of the solvent (water) molecules from a solution of lower solute concentration to a solution of higher solute concentration through a semipermeable membrane.

*Types:*
1. **Endosmosis:** movement of water into a cell or an osmometer when it is placed in a hypotonic solution (the cell swells or becomes turgid).
2. **Exosmosis:** movement of water out of a cell placed in a hypertonic solution (the cell shrinks; plasmolysis in plant cells).

*Significance of osmosis:*
1. Absorption of water from the soil by the root hairs, and its movement from cell to cell across the cortex into the xylem.
2. Maintains the turgidity of cells, which gives rigidity to herbaceous parts and keeps leaves and flowers erect.
3. Opening and closing of the stomata depends on the turgor change of the guard cells, which regulates transpiration and gas exchange.
4. Cell growth and enlargement, and movements such as the folding of leaves of *Mimosa pudica* (touch-me-not).
5. Seed germination (absorption of water by seeds) and the preservation of food by salting and sugaring (plasmolysis of the microbes).

@s 19
**Disease resistant plants by biotechnology (genetic engineering).** Plants can be made resistant to diseases caused by viruses, bacteria, fungi and insect pests by isolating a gene that gives resistance and transferring it into the plant by recombinant DNA technology.
*Steps:* (1) the resistance gene is isolated from the source organism; (2) it is inserted into a vector, usually the Ti plasmid of *Agrobacterium tumefaciens*, or delivered by a gene gun; (3) the plant cells or protoplasts are transformed and grown on a tissue culture medium; (4) the transformed cells are regenerated into whole transgenic plants, which are then tested and selected.
*Examples:*
1. **Bt cotton, Bt maize and Bt brinjal:** the *cry* gene of *Bacillus thuringiensis* produces a toxin that kills bollworms and stem borers (insect resistance).
2. **Virus-resistant papaya (ring spot virus) and potato, tomato:** the coat protein gene of the virus is inserted so that the plant is protected (protection by the coat protein).
3. **Chitinase gene (from bean or bacteria):** placed in tobacco, rice and tomato; the enzyme breaks the chitin wall of fungal pathogens, giving fungal resistance.
4. Other methods: **RNA interference (RNAi)** silencing of the pathogen genes (nematode-resistant tobacco), and **disease-free plants by meristem culture** (virus-free potato, banana and orchids).
These crops reduce the use of pesticides, cut losses and raise the yield.

**Amniocentesis** is a prenatal diagnostic technique in which a sample of the amniotic fluid surrounding the foetus is withdrawn and examined for the genetic or chromosomal condition of the foetus.

*Process (5):*
1. It is done at about the 15th to 18th week of pregnancy (not before 14 weeks).
2. The position of the foetus and placenta is first located by ultrasound so that the needle does not hurt them. The abdomen is cleaned and, under local anaesthesia if needed, a thin hollow needle is passed through the abdominal and uterine wall into the amniotic sac.
3. About 10 to 20 mL of amniotic fluid containing foetal cells (shed skin, gut and urinary cells) is withdrawn with a syringe.
4. The foetal cells are separated by centrifugation and cultured for one to two weeks. The fluid is tested biochemically (e.g. $\alpha$-fetoprotein for neural tube defects, enzymes for metabolic disorders).
5. The cells are stained and the karyotype is prepared from the dividing cells in metaphase; the chromosome number and structure are studied under the microscope and the result is reported.

*Why it is done (1):* to detect the chromosomal abnormalities (Down syndrome, Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome), the sex-linked genetic and metabolic disorders (haemophilia, sickle-cell anaemia, thalassaemia, Tay-Sachs disease, phenylketonuria) and neural tube defects of the foetus, so that parents can decide on the continuation of pregnancy.

*Misuse (1):* it is misused for sex determination of the foetus. When the sex is found to be female, some families go for sex-selective abortion (female foeticide), which reduces the sex ratio. It is illegal in many countries to reveal the sex of the foetus for this purpose.

*Risks (1):* it may cause miscarriage (about 0.5 to 1 percent), injury to the foetus or the placenta with the needle, infection of the uterus (amnionitis), leakage of the amniotic fluid, bleeding, Rh sensitisation of an Rh-negative mother and premature labour.

**Differences between enzymes and hormones.**

| Enzymes | Hormones |
|---|---|
| Biocatalysts (proteins) that speed up biochemical reactions | Chemical messengers that regulate growth, metabolism and reproduction |
| Produced by cells in all living tissues, and they work in the cell or in the tube of the digestive tract (mostly at the site of production) | Produced by the endocrine (ductless) glands and carried by the blood to the target organs |
| All are proteins | May be proteins, peptides, amines or steroids |
| Not used up; act again and again, specific for substrate | Used up and destroyed (in the liver) after action, specific to target tissue |

**Thyroid gland — structure.** The thyroid is the largest endocrine gland, situated in the neck in front of the trachea just below the larynx. It is butterfly (H) shaped, with two lobes, right and left, connected by a narrow strip of tissue, the isthmus. It weighs about 25 to 30 g and is enclosed by a connective tissue capsule. Inside, the gland is made of numerous spherical follicles lined with cuboidal epithelium and filled with colloid (thyroglobulin) in which the hormones are stored; parafollicular (C) cells between the follicles secrete calcitonin.

**Functions.** The follicular cells take iodine from the blood and, using the amino acid tyrosine, make *thyroxine (T₄)* and *tri-iodothyronine (T₃)*; their release is controlled by TSH from the anterior pituitary.
1. They control the basal metabolic rate and the heat production of the body (calorigenic effect).
2. They are needed for normal growth of the body, bones and the nervous system, especially in children, and for the metamorphosis of amphibians.
3. They regulate the metabolism of carbohydrates, proteins and fats, and help the maturation of the reproductive organs.
4. *Calcitonin* (from the C cells) lowers the blood calcium level by depositing it in the bones.
*Disorders:* hypothyroidism gives cretinism in children, myxoedema in adults and simple goitre in iodine deficiency; hyperthyroidism gives exophthalmic goitre (Graves' disease), with a raised metabolic rate, weight loss and protruding eyes.

*Dihybrid cross* is a cross between two parents differing in two pairs of contrasting characters (alleles of two different genes).

**Mendel's experiment.** He crossed pea plants with round yellow seeds (RRYY) with plants with wrinkled green seeds (rryy). R (round) is dominant to r (wrinkled), and Y (yellow) is dominant to y (green).
- Parents: RRYY (round yellow) × rryy (wrinkled green). Gametes: RY and ry.
- F₁: all RrYy, round yellow.
- Self-pollination of the F₁ (RrYy × RrYy): each F₁ plant forms four types of gametes in equal numbers, RY, Ry, rY and ry (the alleles of the two genes assort independently), so a 4 × 4 Punnett square gives 16 combinations:

| ♂ \ ♀ | RY | Ry | rY | ry |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **RY** | RRYY | RRYy | RrYY | RrYy |
| **Ry** | RRYy | RRyy | RrYy | Rryy |
| **rY** | RrYY | RrYy | rrYY | rrYy |
| **ry** | RrYy | Rryy | rrYy | rryy |

*F₂ phenotypic ratio:* round yellow 9 : round green 3 : wrinkled yellow 3 : wrinkled green 1 (the 9:3:3:1 ratio).
*F₂ genotypic ratio:* 1 RRYY : 2 RRYy : 2 RrYY : 4 RrYy : 1 RRyy : 2 Rryy : 1 rrYY : 2 rrYy : 1 rryy, nine genotypes.

**Conclusions.**
1. Four phenotypes appear in the F₂, two of them (round green and wrinkled yellow) are new combinations not seen in the parents (recombinants).
2. Each pair of characters, taken alone, gives the monohybrid ratio: round : wrinkled = (9 + 3) : (3 + 1) = 3 : 1 and yellow : green = 3 : 1.
3. The two pairs of characters are inherited independently of each other.

**Law formulated:** the *Law of independent assortment*: when two pairs of contrasting alleles are crossed, the alleles of one gene pair assort (separate) independently of the alleles of the other pair during gamete formation, so any allele of one gene can combine with either allele of the other gene. (This holds for genes on different chromosomes.)

**Or.** *Nucleic acids* are long-chain macromolecules (polymers) of nucleotides, present in all living cells, which store and transmit the genetic information. Each nucleotide has a pentose sugar, a nitrogenous base and a phosphate group.
*Types:* **DNA** (deoxyribonucleic acid) and **RNA** (ribonucleic acid).

| Basis | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Mainly in the nucleus (chromosomes); also in mitochondria and chloroplasts | Nucleolus, cytoplasm and ribosomes; made in the nucleus |
| Structure | Double stranded, a right-handed double helix with antiparallel strands | Usually single stranded (may fold locally) |
| Sugar | Deoxyribose | Ribose |
| Bases | Adenine, guanine, cytosine, **thymine** | Adenine, guanine, cytosine, **uracil** |
| Base pairing | A=T, G≡C (Chargaff's rule A = T, G = C) | A–U, G–C in folded regions only |
| Types | One kind | mRNA, tRNA, rRNA |
| Function | Stores genetic information, replicates and is passed to the next generation | Takes part in protein synthesis: mRNA carries the code, tRNA brings amino acids, rRNA forms the ribosome |
| Stability | Stable | Less stable, short lived |

**Structure of RNA.** RNA is a polymer of ribonucleotides joined by 3′-5′ phosphodiester bonds into a single polynucleotide strand. The backbone is made of alternating ribose sugar and phosphate groups, with a nitrogenous base (A, U, G or C) attached to the 1′ carbon of each ribose. It is usually single stranded, but it can fold back on itself to form hairpin loops with base pairs A–U and G–C. The three main types differ in shape: **mRNA** is a long linear strand with codons; **tRNA** is a clover-leaf with an anticodon loop and an amino acid attachment (CCA) arm; **rRNA** is folded and combined with proteins to make ribosomes.
