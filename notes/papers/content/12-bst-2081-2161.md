### Group A

@s 1
Two functions of middle level management: (i) it converts the broad objectives and policies of top management into departmental plans and programmes; (ii) it coordinates and supervises the work of lower level managers and reports the performance to top management.

@s 2
Pitfalls (limitations) of planning: planning is time-consuming and costly; it is based on forecasts, which may be wrong in a changing environment; it makes the organisation rigid and reduces initiative; and it cannot remove uncertainty.

@s 3
The conditions of decision making are (i) **certainty**, where the outcomes of each alternative are known; (ii) **risk**, where the probabilities of outcomes are known; and (iii) **uncertainty**, where neither outcomes nor probabilities are known.

@s 5
Motivation is the process of stimulating and inspiring people to act willingly and enthusiastically so that organisational goals are achieved while their own needs are satisfied. It is the inner drive that directs behaviour toward a goal.

@s 6
Two roles of a supervisor: (i) he or she is the link between management and workers, conveying plans and instructions downward and workers' problems upward; (ii) he or she directs and guides daily work, maintains discipline and ensures the quality and quantity of output.

### Group B

@s 12
A profession is an occupation that rests on a specialised body of knowledge, needs formal education and training, has an ethical code and a professional body, and puts service ahead of profit. Management can be judged against these features.

| Feature of a profession | Position of management |
|---|---|
| Specialised knowledge | A well-developed body of principles and theory (planning, organising, leading, controlling) exists and is taught in universities |
| Formal education and training | Degrees such as BBS, BBA and MBA and management development programmes are available, but they are **not compulsory** to become a manager |
| Professional association | Bodies such as the Nepal Management Association and Institute of Management exist, but membership is not necessary |
| Code of conduct | Ethical guidelines exist for business and professional managers, but there is no strictly enforced code and no licence |
| Service motive | Managers work for profit and the interest of owners as well as for society |

**Conclusion.** Management fulfils most characteristics of a profession, since it has a knowledge base, training and growing codes of ethics, but it lacks a compulsory licence and a binding code. It is therefore called an **emerging profession**, and it is increasingly professional in Nepal as institutions demand qualified managers.

@s 13
Frederick W. Taylor, the father of scientific management, argued in *The Principles of Scientific Management* (1911) that the "one best way" to do each job can be found by scientific study. His principles:
1. **Science, not rule of thumb.** Replace guesswork with scientific study of each task by time, motion and fatigue studies, to find the best method.
2. **Harmony, not discord.** Management and workers should cooperate, with a "mental revolution" that sees prosperity of both as the same goal.
3. **Cooperation, not individualism.** Management and workers should work together in friendly spirit instead of competing.
4. **Maximum output in place of restricted output.** Aim at the greatest output, not a limited one, so as to raise both wages and profit.
5. **Scientific selection, training and development of workers.** Choose the right person for each job and train him or her in the best method.
6. **Equal division of work and responsibility.** Managers plan and supervise, workers perform; each does what he or she is best suited for.
Taylor also proposed techniques such as functional foremanship, standardisation, differential piece-rate wages and the exception principle.

@s 14
**Decision making** is the process of choosing the best alternative from the available alternatives to solve a problem. The steps:
1. **Identify the problem.** Define the problem clearly and distinguish its cause from its symptoms (for example, falling sales).
2. **Gather information and analyse the problem.** Collect facts about the causes and the environment.
3. **Develop alternatives.** List all possible solutions, e.g. reduce price, advertise more, improve quality.
4. **Evaluate the alternatives.** Compare them in terms of cost, benefit, risk, resources and effect on the objective.
5. **Select the best alternative.** Choose the one that best solves the problem, using experience, experiment or analysis.
6. **Implement the decision.** Communicate it to the people concerned, assign responsibility and give resources.
7. **Follow up and evaluate the result.** Compare the result with the expected outcome and take corrective action if needed.

**Or (Q14). Planning** is deciding in advance what to do, when to do it, how to do it and who will do it. It bridges the gap between where we are and where we want to go.

**Rationale (importance) of planning.**
1. **Provides direction.** Clear objectives and the means to reach them guide everyone's effort.
2. **Reduces uncertainty and risk.** Forecasting prepares the organisation for change.
3. **Avoids waste and duplication.** Resources are used economically and activities are coordinated.
4. **Facilitates control.** Plans give the standards against which actual performance is measured.
5. **Encourages innovation and competitive advantage.** Planning forces managers to think ahead and to find better methods.
6. **Improves decision making and motivates.** Managers know what is expected, which raises morale.
Thus planning is the primary function of management because the other functions depend on it.

@s 15
Delegation of authority is the downward passing of **part** of a manager's authority to a subordinate for a specific task, while decentralisation is the **systematic and policy-level** dispersal of decision power to all lower levels of the organisation.

| Basis | Delegation of authority | Decentralisation of authority |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Entrusting a specific job and the authority needed to a subordinate | Systematic spreading of decision-making power throughout the organisation |
| Nature | An act or process between two persons (superior and subordinate) | An organisation-wide philosophy and policy |
| Scope | Narrow, applies to individual jobs | Wide, covers departments and levels |
| Necessity | Essential in every organisation; no manager can do all the work | Optional; an organisation may choose a centralised system |
| Withdrawal | Authority can be taken back easily at any time | Not easily reversed because it is a policy |
| Accountability | The delegator remains ultimately accountable | The responsibility is shared with the units that hold the authority |
| Control | Superior controls through direct supervision | Control is exercised through standards and reports |
| Example | Branch manager asks an officer to approve small loans | A bank gives all branches the power to decide on loans up to a limit |

In short, delegation is a **means**, decentralisation is an **end result**; decentralisation is delegation applied throughout the organisation.

@s 16
Leadership is the ability to influence people to work willingly toward goals. Successful managers need these qualities:
1. **Vision and foresight.** The ability to see future opportunities, set goals and bring the team along.
2. **Intelligence and knowledge.** Good technical and managerial knowledge and sound judgement.
3. **Integrity and honesty.** Ethical conduct earns trust and respect.
4. **Communication skills.** The ability to listen, explain and persuade.
5. **Self-confidence and decisiveness.** Taking decisions at the right time and standing by them.
6. **Emotional stability and empathy.** Staying calm under pressure and understanding subordinates' feelings.
7. **Initiative and innovation.** Willingness to take calculated risks and try new ideas.
8. **Human relations and motivation skills.** Ability to inspire, to build a team and to resolve conflict.
Other qualities are physical and mental energy, fairness, a sense of responsibility and adaptability.

@s 17
Controlling is the process of measuring actual performance, comparing it with the standard set in the plans, and taking corrective action to remove the deviations. It is important because:
1. **Ensures achievement of goals.** It checks that activities stay on the path of the plan.
2. **Judges the accuracy of standards.** Continuous review shows whether standards are realistic and need revision.
3. **Makes efficient use of resources.** It reduces waste and loss of material, time and money.
4. **Improves employee motivation and discipline.** When employees know they are being measured against clear standards they work carefully; rewards can be linked to performance.
5. **Ensures order and discipline.** It creates an atmosphere of order and reduces dishonest behaviour.
6. **Facilitates coordination and decision making.** Information from control helps all departments move together and helps managers decide.
7. **Copes with change and complexity.** It helps the organisation to detect and adapt to the changes in the environment.
Thus planning without control is of little use, and control without planning has no standard.

@s 18
Conflict is a disagreement between individuals or groups arising from differences in goals, interests or perceptions. Likely sources in Nepalese organisations:
1. **Differences in goals and interests.** Departments (sales and production) or owner and employee pursue different goals and compete.
2. **Scarcity of resources.** Limited budget, staff and equipment lead departments to compete for them.
3. **Communication problems.** Poor, distorted or insufficient information causes misunderstanding and rumour.
4. **Role ambiguity and task interdependence.** Unclear authority and overlapping duties cause blame between units.
5. **Personality, cultural and value differences.** Differences of caste, ethnicity, region, language, politics and attitude bring friction.
6. **Trade-union and political interference.** Party-affiliated unions in many Nepali public and private enterprises create labour-management conflict over wages, bonus and promotion.
7. **Unfair treatment and favouritism.** Biased promotion, transfer, pay and appraisal, and "source-force" (nepotism) generate resentment.
8. **Family disputes in family-owned firms** over ownership and succession.

**Or (Q18). Importance of supply chain management.**
1. **Reduces cost.** Better purchasing, inventory control and transport lower the total cost of goods.
2. **Improves customer service.** Products reach the customer in the right quantity, at the right place and time, which raises satisfaction.
3. **Reduces inventory and wastage.** Information-sharing with suppliers avoids overstocking and stock-outs.
4. **Competitive advantage.** A quick, low-cost chain beats competitors on price, speed and quality.
5. **Better coordination and relationships.** Close links with suppliers and distributors build trust and long-term partnerships.
6. **Risk management and flexibility.** A well-managed chain adapts to demand change or supplier failure; the pandemic showed the need for this.
7. **Improves profit and quality.** Fewer defects and faster cash flow increase profitability.

@s 20
**Organising function (2 marks).** Organising is the managerial function of identifying and grouping the work to be done, defining and delegating authority and responsibility, and establishing relationships so that people can work together effectively to achieve the objectives. It follows planning and gives the plan a structure.

**Principles of organising (6 marks).**
1. **Principle of objective (unity of objective).** The structure should be designed to help achieve the objectives of the enterprise; every department should contribute to them.
2. **Specialisation (division of work).** Work should be divided according to skills and ability, so that each person does the job he or she does best.
3. **Unity of command.** Each employee should receive orders from one superior only, to avoid confusion and conflict.
4. **Scalar chain (line of authority).** There should be a clear chain from the top to the lowest level, which is the path for communication and authority.
5. **Span of management (span of control).** A manager can effectively supervise only a limited number of subordinates; the span depends on the nature of work and skill of the people.
6. **Authority and responsibility.** Authority should match responsibility; responsibility without authority makes people ineffective and authority without responsibility leads to misuse.
7. **Delegation and accountability.** Authority is delegated to the lowest competent level, but the superior stays accountable.
8. **Coordination.** All units and activities must be harmonised, so that the parts work toward a common goal.
9. **Balance, flexibility and continuity.** The structure should balance centralisation and decentralisation, adapt to change and continue to function when people leave.
10. **Efficiency.** The structure should achieve objectives at the least cost.

**Maslow's need hierarchy theory.** Abraham Maslow (1943, *A Theory of Human Motivation*) said that people have five levels of needs arranged in a hierarchy; a lower need must be reasonably satisfied before the next one becomes a motivator, and a satisfied need no longer motivates.

```figure caption="Maslow's hierarchy of needs (workplace examples)."
from matplotlib.patches import Polygon
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.4,2.9)); ax.axis('off'); ax.set_xlim(0,12); ax.set_ylim(0,5.4)
lv=[('Physiological','salary, food, rest',1),('Safety','job security, insurance',2),('Social','team, friendship',3),('Esteem','status, recognition',4),('Self-actualisation','growth, creativity',5)]
for name,ex,i in lv:
    y0=(i-1)*1.0; y1=y0+0.95
    w0=5.2-(i-1)*0.9; w1=5.2-i*0.9 if i<5 else 0.2
    ax.add_patch(Polygon([(6-w0,y0),(6+w0,y0),(6+w1,y1),(6-w1,y1)],fc=['#e8eef7','#d9e9fa','#cfe9c8','#fbe3c8','#f6d3d3'][i-1],ec=INK,lw=0.9))
    ax.text(6,y0+0.45,name,ha='center',va='center',fontsize=7.5 if i<5 else 6.5)
    ax.text(11.9,y0+0.45,ex,ha='right',va='center',fontsize=6.5,color=ACCENT)
ax.annotate('',xy=(0.5,4.7),xytext=(0.5,0.2),arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='-|>',color=INK,lw=1))
```

**The five levels.** (1) *Physiological*: food, water, shelter, basic pay. (2) *Safety*: job security, safe working conditions, pension, insurance. (3) *Social (belonging)*: friendship, teamwork, acceptance in the group. (4) *Esteem*: recognition, status, promotion, respect. (5) *Self-actualisation*: realising one's full potential, creativity and growth.

**Critical examination.**
*Merits.* (i) It is simple, logical and easy to apply; (ii) it shows that human needs are many and that managers must find which need is active; (iii) it widely influenced later theories (Alderfer's ERG, Herzberg's two-factor); (iv) it focuses on the whole person, not only on money.
*Limitations.* (i) It is based on little empirical evidence and the hierarchy is not rigid, since some people seek esteem before belonging; (ii) levels overlap and several needs operate at the same time; (iii) it ignores culture, income and individual differences; (iv) a satisfied need can still motivate (for example money, which also gives esteem), and it is difficult to measure satisfaction; (v) in Nepal's context, a person with low income may strive for security even when social needs are not met, or sacrifice basic needs for family honour.
*Conclusion.* In spite of these criticisms the theory remains a helpful practical guide: managers should design pay, security, teamwork, recognition and growth opportunities to match employees' dominant needs.

**Or (Q21). Communication (2 marks).** Communication is the process of exchanging information, ideas and feelings between two or more persons so that a common understanding is reached. It involves a sender, a message, a channel, a receiver and feedback.

**Barriers to effective communication (6 marks).**
1. **Semantic (language) barriers.** Jargon, ambiguous words, different languages and wrong translation distort meaning.
2. **Psychological barriers.** Prejudice, fear, inattention, emotions, a closed mind and poor retention stop the receiver from understanding.
3. **Organisational barriers.** A rigid hierarchy, long chains of command, complex structure, and distance between departments delay and filter information.
4. **Physical (environmental) barriers.** Noise, poor lines, distance, faulty equipment and interruptions.
5. **Personal barriers.** Differences in status, lack of trust between superior and subordinate, fear of authority, and poor listening or reading ability.
6. **Cultural and social barriers.** Different customs, gestures and values (for example the meaning of a nod) lead to misunderstanding.
7. **Information overload and technological barriers.** Too many messages, or the lack of access to technology, reduce attention and speed.
8. **Lack of feedback and filtering.** The message is changed or cut by intermediaries and the sender does not know the result.
**Overcoming them:** use clear and simple language, encourage feedback and active listening, shorten the chain of communication, select a suitable medium and train staff.

@s 22
A **business plan** is a written document that describes the goals of a business and how they will be reached, covering marketing, operations, people and finance. For an existing firm it is prepared to expand, to get a loan or to restructure the business. Its main components are:
1. **Executive summary.** A short summary of the whole plan: the business, products, market, financial need and expected result; it is written last but placed first.
2. **Business description.** Name, location, legal form (proprietorship, partnership, company), history, mission, vision and the present state of the firm.
3. **Products and services.** Features, uses, quality, price and how they differ from competitors'; plans for new products.
4. **Market (industry) analysis.** The target market, size and growth of demand, customer profile and competitor analysis (SWOT).
5. **Marketing plan.** Strategy for product, price, place and promotion, and the sales forecast.
6. **Operation (production) plan.** Location, plant and equipment, raw material and suppliers, technology, capacity and quality control.
7. **Organisation and management plan.** Ownership, organisation chart, key managers with their experience, staffing and training needs.
8. **Financial plan.** Past financial statements, projected income statement, cash flow and balance sheet for three to five years, the funds required and their sources, and the break-even analysis.
9. **Risk analysis and contingency plan.** Possible risks (market, finance, legal) and how to meet them.
10. **Implementation schedule and appendix.** The timetable of activities and supporting documents such as licences, resumes and quotations.
A good plan is realistic, specific and measurable, and is reviewed periodically to track progress.
