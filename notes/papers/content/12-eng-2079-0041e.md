@q 1
Read the following text and complete the tasks that follow. [15]

Today, bicycles are elegantly simple machines that are common around the world. Many people ride bicycles for **recreation**, where as others use them as a means of transportation. The first bicycle, called a draisienne, was invented in Germany in 1818 by Baron Karl de Drais de Sauerbrun. Because it was made of wood, the draisienne wasn't very durable nor did it have pedals. Riders moved it by pushing their feet against the ground. In 1839, Kirkpatrick Macmillan, a Scottish blacksmith, invented a much better bicycle. Macmillan's machine had tires with iron rims to keep them from getting **worn down**. He also used foot-operated cranks, similar to pedals, so his bicycle could be ridden at a quick pace. It didn't look much like the modern bicycle, though, because its back wheel was substantially large than its front wheel. Although Machmillan's bicycles could be ridden easily, they were never produced in large numbers. In 1861, Frenchman Pierre Michaux and his brother Ernest invented a bicycle with an improved crank mechanism. They called their bicycle a velocipede, but most people called it a "bone shaker" because of the jarring effect of the wood and iron frame. Despite the unflattering nickname, Velocipede was a hit. After a few years, the Michaux family was making hundreds of the machines annually, mostly for fun-seeking young people.

Ten years later, James Starley, an English inventor, made several **innovations** that **revolutionized** bicycle design. He made the front wheel many times larger than the back wheel, put a gear on the pedals to make the bicycle more efficient, and lightened the wheels by using wire spokes. Although this bicycle was much lighter and less tiring to ride, it was still **clumsy**, extremely top-heavy, and ridden mostly for entertainment. It wasn't until 1874 that the first truly modern bicycle appeared on the scene. Invented by another Englishman, H.J. Lawson, the safety bicycle would look familiar to today's cyclists. The safety bicycle had equal-sized wheels, which made it much less **prone** to toppling over. Lawson also attached a chain to the pedals to drive the rear wheel. By 1993, the safety bicycle had been further **improved** with air-filled rubber tires, a diamond shaped frame, and easy braking. With the improvements provided by Lawson, bicycles became extremely popular and useful for transportation. Today, they are built, used, and enjoyed all over the world.

@s 2
**(a) Fractured dialogue (Neighbours).** The phrase means conversation that is broken, patchy and incomplete. In the story the neighbours come from different backgrounds and speak limited or broken English, so their talk across the fences is made of half-sentences, gestures and smiles. The exchanges are imperfect, yet through them the characters slowly overcome strangeness and build warmth and trust.

**(b) Moral of 'The Treasure in the Forest'.** In my opinion the story teaches that greed is destructive. Wealth gained by suspicion, deceit and selfishness brings fear and ruin instead of happiness. Real peace comes from honesty and trust, so human life and relationships are worth more than treasure.

**(c) Reference to the context (I Was My Own Route, Julia de Burgos).** These lines come from the second stanza of the poem by the Puerto Rican poet Julia de Burgos. The speaker has chosen her own path of freedom away from patriarchal expectations. She says that with each step forward she was hurt by the "old guard", the conservatives who defend male-dominated tradition. Their "desperate flapping wings" show their panic as the old order loses control. The lines show the pain and resistance that women face when they move towards liberation.

**(d) Marriage as an institution.** Marriage is an institution because it is a lasting social arrangement, recognised by custom, religion and law, with fixed rules and roles. It regulates sexual relations and the rights and duties of husband and wife, and it gives legitimacy to children and family. It also decides inheritance and links families and kin groups, so it is more than a private union.

**(e) Universal human abilities (Humility).** According to the essay, universal human abilities are those shared by all people in every culture: morality, art, spirituality and creativity. They are seen as part of human nature, found in people everywhere, which shows that all humans share one basic capacity and one human family.

@s 3
**(a) 'Facing Death' as a modern tragedy.** A classical tragedy tells of kings and heroes who fall because of fate or a noble flaw. A modern tragedy takes an ordinary person and shows how money, family and society crush him. August Strindberg's one-act play *Facing Death* fits this description closely.

- **Common hero.** Monsieur Durand is a widower and a former railway worker who runs a shabby boarding house. He is neither a king nor a warrior.
- **Realistic problems.** Debt, hunger, a failing business and a loveless household are the forces that destroy him, not gods or destiny. The whole action takes place in one ordinary dining room.
- **Family breakdown.** His three daughters blame him for their poverty and treat him with contempt. They are unaware that their late mother had wasted the family's money.
- **Loneliness.** Durand loves them but cannot make them understand, so he suffers in silence and isolation.
- **Tragic end.** He sees only one way to secure their future. He sets the house on fire and calmly drinks poisoned milk so that the daughters will receive the insurance money. They understand his love only when it is too late.

The play therefore has a common man as hero, a realistic setting, social and economic causes of suffering and an unhappy end. Hence it is a typical modern tragedy.

**(b) Abuse and cruelty in 'An Old Man with Enormous Wings'.** I agree with the statement. Gabriel García Márquez uses the story to show how people treat someone who is helpless and different.

- **Imprisonment.** After Pelayo finds the old winged man in his courtyard, he and Elisenda lock him in a chicken coop with the hens, as if he were an animal.
- **Exploitation.** The couple charge the crowds an entrance fee and become rich. The old man is only a source of money.
- **Cruelty of the crowd.** Visitors throw food and stones at him, and some burn him with a branding iron to see whether he is alive. He reacts only with weary patience.
- **Indifference of authority.** Father Gonzaga does not help him; the priest merely writes to higher authorities and waits for their answer.
- **Neglect.** When a travelling spider-woman attracts the crowd's interest, the old man is abandoned. Later he lives ill and untended in the yard, and Elisenda feels relief when he finally flies away.

These details show human selfishness, greed and lack of compassion. The cruelty is tragic because the old man, a possible angel, forgives it. Thus the theme of abuse and cruelty is clear in the story.

@s 4
**Social Media: Boon and Bane**

Social media has become a part of everyday life. Platforms such as Facebook, TikTok and Instagram connect billions of people, and their effects are both good and bad.

On the positive side, social media lets us keep in touch with friends and relatives anywhere in the world at almost no cost. It is a quick source of news and learning, since students can join online groups and watch free lessons. It also gives small businesses and young artists a cheap place to show their work, and it helps people raise their voice for social causes or in emergencies.

However, there are serious negative effects. Many users waste hours scrolling and neglect their studies, sleep and physical activity. False news spreads quickly and can confuse or even harm society. Cyberbullying and online fraud are growing, and constantly comparing oneself with perfect-looking posts can cause stress and low self-esteem. Privacy is also easily lost.

In conclusion, social media is a tool whose value depends on its user. If we use it wisely, for limited time and for useful purposes, it benefits us; if we misuse it, it harms us.

@s 6
**Physical Classrooms or Online Learning?** (about 300 words)

The pandemic forced schools all over the world to move online, and the discussion about which mode is better has continued since then. I largely agree that students learn much better when they are physically present in the classroom, although online learning has a useful place.

First, a classroom provides direct interaction. Students can ask a question at once, and the teacher can see confused faces and explain again. In online classes, poor internet, muted microphones and switched-off cameras make this exchange slow and weak. Second, the classroom brings discipline. A fixed timetable, a quiet room and the teacher's presence keep students attentive, while at home they are distracted by phones, games, family work and the comfort of their beds. Third, the classroom develops social skills. Students learn teamwork, sharing, leadership and friendship through group work, sports and assemblies. These cannot be gained from a screen. Fourth, practical subjects such as science need laboratories, which cannot be replaced by videos. Finally, in countries like Nepal many students have no stable electricity, internet or device, so online teaching leaves them behind and widens inequality.

On the other hand, online learning has advantages. It saves travelling time and cost, gives access to recorded lessons and world-class teachers, and allows learning to continue during disasters or lockdowns. It is useful as a supplement for revision and for students in remote places.

In conclusion, online learning is a helpful tool in emergencies and for extra study, but it cannot fully replace the personal guidance, discipline, social life and practical training of a physical classroom. The best approach is to treat the classroom as the main place of learning and to use online resources as a support.
