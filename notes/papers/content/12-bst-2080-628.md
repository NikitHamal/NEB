### Group A

@s 1
Management is the process of planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling the efforts of people and the use of resources so that the objectives of an organisation are achieved efficiently and effectively. Its main characteristics are as follows.
1. **Goal oriented.** Management exists to achieve predetermined objectives, such as profit, growth or service. Every managerial action is judged by how far it moves the organisation towards its goals.
2. **Universal.** Every organisation, whether a bank, school, hospital, factory or government office, needs management; the principles apply everywhere, though practice differs.
3. **Continuous process.** Planning, organising, directing and controlling go on without end, because objectives and conditions keep changing.
4. **Group activity.** Management works through people. It brings individuals together and coordinates their efforts into teamwork.
5. **Both science and art.** It has organised principles that can be tested (science) and requires personal skill in applying them to a situation (art).
6. **Multi-dimensional.** It deals with work, people and operations at the same time.
7. **Dynamic function.** Managers adjust their methods to a changing environment of technology, law, competition and customer taste.
8. **Intangible force.** It cannot be seen, but its effect is seen in order, discipline, productivity and results.
9. **Authority and accountability.** Managers use authority to direct others and are answerable for the results.

In short, management is the common thread that turns scattered resources into purposeful, productive activity.

@s 2
**Henri Fayol** (France) gave fourteen principles of administrative management in *General and Industrial Management*. Any eight are explained below.
1. **Division of work.** Work is divided into specialised tasks so that each worker becomes skilled and output rises.
2. **Authority and responsibility.** The right to give orders must go with the responsibility for results; authority without responsibility leads to misuse.
3. **Discipline.** Obedience, respect and observance of agreed rules are needed, backed by good leadership and fair penalties.
4. **Unity of command.** An employee should receive orders from one superior only, to avoid confusion and conflict.
5. **Unity of direction.** All activities with one objective should have one head and one plan.
6. **Subordination of individual interest to general interest.** The interest of the organisation comes before that of any person or group.
7. **Remuneration.** Pay should be fair to both the employee and the employer and should reward good performance.
8. **Scalar chain.** A chain of authority runs from top to bottom; Fayol's gang plank allows direct contact between equals at the same level in urgent cases, with the knowledge of superiors.
9. **Order, equity, stability of tenure, initiative and esprit de corps** are the remaining principles, covering orderly placement of people and things, kindness with justice, security of job, freedom to plan, and team spirit.

Fayol's principles are flexible guidelines and remain the base of modern management.

@s 3
Planning is deciding in advance what to do, how, when and by whom. Though it is the first function of management, it has limitations.
1. **Rigidity.** Once a plan is made, managers may follow it strictly even when conditions change, losing flexibility and the chance to use new opportunities.
2. **Inaccurate forecasting.** Plans rest on forecasts of sales, prices and technology. The future is uncertain, so wrong assumptions make a plan useless.
3. **Costly.** Collecting data, hiring experts and holding meetings need time and money. For small firms the cost may exceed the benefit.
4. **Time consuming.** Detailed planning delays action, and in an emergency there may be no time for it.
5. **Resistance to change.** Employees may oppose new plans that change their routine, status or income.
6. **Limited by external factors.** Government policy, political change, economic slowdown, natural disaster and competitor moves are beyond the control of the planner.
7. **False sense of security.** Managers may believe that a written plan guarantees success and neglect daily monitoring.
8. **Lack of reliable information and skill.** Poor data or untrained planners produce poor plans.
9. **Reduces initiative.** Detailed plans leave lower-level employees little freedom to innovate.

These limits do not remove the need for planning; they only show that plans must be realistic, flexible and revised regularly.

@s 4
**Decision making (2 marks).** Decision making is the process of selecting the best course of action from two or more alternatives in order to solve a problem or achieve an objective.

**Importance of decision making (6 marks).**
1. **Basis of all managerial functions.** Planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling each need decisions at every step.
2. **Achievement of objectives.** Right decisions guide the organisation along the shortest path to its goals.
3. **Efficient use of resources.** Decisions on how to use money, materials, machines and manpower reduce waste and raise productivity.
4. **Solving problems.** Managers identify problems and choose solutions through decisions; a delayed decision lets the problem grow.
5. **Facing change and competition.** Decisions on price, product, technology and market let the firm react to competitors and customers.
6. **Motivation and morale.** When employees take part in decisions they feel valued and carry them out willingly.
7. **Coordination.** A decision that is clear and communicated unifies the work of departments.
8. **Measure of managerial ability.** The quality of a manager is judged mainly by the soundness of the decisions made.

Therefore decision making is called the heart of management.

@s 5
Motivation is the process of stimulating people to act to accomplish goals. Managers use either fear or reward, which gives two kinds of motivation.

| Basis | Positive motivation | Negative motivation |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Inducing people to work by giving rewards and incentives | Inducing people to work by fear of punishment or loss |
| Approach | Carrot approach; "do this and you will get that" | Stick approach; "do this or you will be punished" |
| Examples | Bonus, promotion, praise, recognition, better pay | Demotion, fine, warning, suspension, dismissal |
| Effect on morale | Raises morale, trust and commitment | Lowers morale and creates resentment |
| Effect on behaviour | Employees work willingly and with initiative | Employees work only to avoid punishment |
| Time effect | Long-lasting results | Short-term results |
| Relationship | Improves the relationship between manager and worker | Spoils the relationship and creates conflict |
| Suitable for | Normal conditions, creative work | Indiscipline and repeated failure, as a last resort |
| Cost | Financial cost to the firm | Little financial cost but high hidden cost (turnover) |

**Conclusion.** Positive motivation builds lasting commitment, while negative motivation prevents bad behaviour. A wise manager relies mainly on positive motivation and uses negative motivation sparingly and fairly.

@s 6
Leadership is the ability to influence people so that they willingly work towards group goals. A manager who leads performs the following functions.
1. **Setting goals and vision.** The leader gives the group a clear direction and explains how its work contributes to the larger purpose.
2. **Guiding and directing.** The leader instructs, trains and guides subordinates so that they know what to do and how to do it.
3. **Motivating employees.** By means of incentives, recognition and personal example, the leader builds enthusiasm and commitment.
4. **Building morale and teamwork.** The leader creates trust and cooperation, turns individuals into a team and keeps spirits high in difficulty.
5. **Coordination.** The leader aligns the efforts of different people and departments so that they pull in one direction.
6. **Communication.** The leader links top management and workers, passing orders down and feedback up.
7. **Representing the group.** The leader speaks for the group before higher authorities, outsiders and the public.
8. **Conflict handling.** The leader settles disputes fairly before they harm work.
9. **Introducing change and innovation.** The leader inspires the group to accept new methods and prepares it for the future.
10. **Developing future leaders.** The leader delegates and trains able subordinates to take up higher roles.

Good leadership turns plans into results and makes the difference between an average and an excellent organisation.

@s 7
Supervision means overseeing the day-to-day work of employees at the operating level to make sure that plans are carried out. The supervisor is the link between management and workers. The following factors affect supervision.
1. **Nature of work.** Routine work needs little supervision, while complex or risky work needs close and frequent supervision.
2. **Skill and attitude of workers.** Skilled, self-motivated workers need only light supervision; unskilled or careless workers need close guidance.
3. **Competence of the supervisor.** Technical knowledge, leadership and human relations skills decide how well the supervisor can guide and win respect.
4. **Span of supervision.** If one supervisor has too many subordinates, effective contact is lost; a narrow span permits closer supervision.
5. **Delegation of authority.** A supervisor with enough authority can decide quickly and act effectively; one without it becomes powerless.
6. **Communication system.** A clear, open and two-way communication makes instructions and feedback easy to pass.
7. **Organisational climate and policy.** Rules, culture, trade union relations and the style of top management shape the supervisor's freedom.
8. **Working conditions and technology.** Machines, layout and safety conditions change the amount and the method of supervision needed.
9. **Size and location of the work place.** Scattered branches or shifts make direct supervision difficult.

Supervision succeeds when these factors are balanced and the supervisor treats workers with fairness and respect.

@s 8
Coordination is the process of unifying the efforts of different people, departments and activities so that they work in harmony towards common objectives. Its importance is as follows.
1. **Unity of action.** An organisation has many departments (production, marketing, finance). Coordination makes them work as one body rather than as separate islands.
2. **Avoids duplication and conflict.** Clear roles and timing prevent two departments doing the same work or pulling against each other.
3. **Achievement of objectives.** When all efforts are linked, the organisation reaches its goals faster and at lower cost.
4. **Efficient use of resources.** Proper sequencing of work avoids idle time, wasted materials and delay.
5. **Growth and specialisation.** As the organisation grows and tasks become more specialised, only coordination can hold the parts together.
6. **Better human relations.** It builds mutual understanding between employees and between departments, raising morale and teamwork.
7. **Improves reputation.** A well-coordinated firm serves customers on time and with consistent quality.
8. **Supports other functions.** Planning, organising, directing and controlling are all linked by coordination, which is therefore called the essence of management.
9. **Reduces misunderstanding** among levels by regular meetings and communication.

Without coordination even excellent people and resources produce disorder.

@s 9
Controlling means measuring actual performance against plans and correcting any deviation so that objectives are achieved. The process has the following steps.
1. **Setting performance standards.** Plans are converted into measurable targets, such as sales of Rs 10 lakh a month, cost per unit, or rejection under 2 per cent. They should be clear and attainable.
2. **Measuring actual performance.** Actual results are measured by reports, personal observation, inspection, sample checks and accounts. Measurement should use the same units as the standard.
3. **Comparing actual with standard.** The actual results are compared with the standard to find the gap, called the deviation, and to see whether it is favourable or adverse.
4. **Analysing deviations.** The size and cause of the deviation are studied. Management by exception directs attention to significant deviations only, ignoring minor ones.
5. **Taking corrective action.** If performance is below standard, steps are taken to correct it: better training, change in method, repair of machines, or redesign of the plan; if the standard itself was unrealistic, it is revised.
6. **Follow-up and feedback.** The effect of the corrective step is watched, and the lessons are fed back into the next plan.

```
Standards -> Measure performance -> Compare -> Analyse deviation -> Corrective action
     ^                                                                    |
     +-------------------------------- feedback --------------------------+
```

The process is continuous, and effective control depends on timely information and a clear link with planning.

@s 10
Conflict is a disagreement between individuals or groups because of incompatible goals, interests or views. Managers use the following techniques to manage it.
1. **Avoidance (withdrawal).** The manager ignores a minor issue or postpones action, hoping it will cool down. It suits trivial matters only.
2. **Smoothing (accommodation).** The manager stresses common interests and plays down differences, or gives in to the other side to maintain harmony.
3. **Compromise.** Each party gives up something to reach a middle position. It is quick and fair, although neither party is fully satisfied.
4. **Competition (forcing).** One side uses authority or power to impose its view. It is useful in emergencies, but it may leave resentment.
5. **Collaboration (problem solving).** The parties openly discuss the problem and look for a solution that satisfies both, a win-win outcome. It is the best but needs time and trust.
6. **Superordinate goals.** A larger goal that both sides need is introduced, so that they must cooperate to reach it.
7. **Third-party intervention.** A mediator or arbitrator, such as a senior manager or labour office, helps to settle serious disputes.
8. **Changing structure or people.** Redefining roles, transferring staff or improving communication removes the root of recurring conflicts.
9. **Expanding resources.** If the conflict is over scarce resources, adding resources removes the cause.

The choice of technique depends on the type, size and urgency of the conflict.
