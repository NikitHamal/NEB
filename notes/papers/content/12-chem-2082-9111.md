### Group B

@s 12
**(a)** The law of chemical equivalence states that substances react with each other in the ratio of their equivalents, i.e. one equivalent of a substance always reacts with exactly one equivalent of another. So in a titration, at the end point, the number of equivalents of the acid equals the number of equivalents of the base.

**(b)** Let $V_1$ mL of an acid of normality $N_1$ exactly neutralise $V_2$ mL of a base of normality $N_2$. Normality is the number of gram equivalents per litre, so
$$\text{milli-equivalents of acid} = N_1V_1,\qquad \text{milli-equivalents of base} = N_2V_2$$
By the law of equivalence, at the end point
$$\boxed{N_1V_1 = N_2V_2}$$
This is the normality equation (the volumetric equation). Knowing three quantities, the fourth is found.

**(c)** Total milli-equivalents $= N_1V_1 + N_2V_2 + N_3V_3$:
$$\frac{1}{2}\times10 + \frac{1}{10}\times30 + \frac{1}{5}\times60 = 5 + 3 + 12 = 20\ \text{meq}$$
Total volume $= 10 + 30 + 60 = 100$ cc. All three are acids, so nothing is neutralised and
$$N = \frac{20}{100} = 0.2\ \text{N}$$

**Or — first law of thermodynamics.**
*Statement:* Energy can neither be created nor destroyed, but only changed from one form to another; the total energy of an isolated system is constant.
*Explanation:* if a system absorbs heat $q$ and work $w$ is done on it, its internal energy rises by
$$\Delta U = q + w$$
(sign convention: heat absorbed and work done on the system are positive). For work of expansion against a constant pressure $w = -P\Delta V$, so $\Delta U = q_V$ at constant volume and $\Delta H = q_P$ at constant pressure. For an isolated system $q = w = 0$, hence $\Delta U = 0$. A perpetual motion machine of the first kind, which gives work without taking energy, is therefore impossible.
*Home appliances:* (1) an electric heater (or geyser) converts electrical energy into heat; (2) a refrigerator or an electric fan converts electrical energy into heat/mechanical work (a pressure cooker, in which heat raises the internal energy of the steam, also works on this principle).

@s 13
**(a)** The pH of a solution is the negative logarithm (to base 10) of the hydrogen ion concentration in mol L⁻¹: $\text{pH} = -\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$.

**(b)** Applications of pH in daily life: (1) the pH of soil decides the choice of the crop and the fertiliser or lime to be added; (2) the pH of blood (7.35 to 7.45) is kept constant, and a change of 0.4 unit is dangerous; (3) tooth decay starts when the mouth pH falls below 5.5; (4) it is used to test the quality of drinking water and in medicines, food, soap and dye industries.

**(c)** pOH is the negative logarithm of the hydroxide ion concentration: $\text{pOH} = -\log_{10}[\text{OH}^-]$. For water at 25 °C the ionic product is $K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 10^{-14}$. Taking the negative logarithm of both sides:
$$\text{pH} + \text{pOH} = 14$$
So a solution of pH 3 has pOH 11, and a larger pH means a smaller pOH.

**(d)** H₂SO₄ is dibasic and (taking it as fully ionised) gives two H⁺ per molecule:
$$[\text{H}^+] = 2\times5\times10^{-4} = 1\times10^{-3}\ \text{M}$$
$$\text{pH} = -\log(10^{-3}) = 3$$

@s 14
Consider a zero order reaction $A \to \text{products}$. The rate is independent of the concentration of the reactant:
$$\text{rate} = -\frac{d[A]}{dt} = k[A]^0 = k$$
Rearranging, $d[A] = -k\,dt$. Integrating from $t = 0$ (concentration $[A]_0$) to time $t$ (concentration $[A]$):
$$\int_{[A]_0}^{[A]} d[A] = -k\int_0^t dt\ \Rightarrow\ [A] - [A]_0 = -kt$$
$$\boxed{k = \frac{[A]_0 - [A]}{t}\qquad\text{or}\qquad [A] = [A]_0 - kt}$$
This is the integrated rate equation. It is the equation of a straight line: a plot of $[A]$ against $t$ is a straight line of slope $-k$ and intercept $[A]_0$. The unit of $k$ is mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.

*Half-life:* it is the time $t_{1/2}$ in which the concentration falls to half, $[A] = [A]_0/2$. Putting this in the equation:
$$\frac{[A]_0}{2} = [A]_0 - kt_{1/2}\ \Rightarrow\ \boxed{t_{1/2} = \frac{[A]_0}{2k}}$$
So the half-life of a zero order reaction is directly proportional to the initial concentration (for a first order reaction it is independent of it). Examples: decomposition of NH₃ on a hot platinum surface, and of HI on gold.

**(d)** The standard electrode potential ($E^\circ$) of an electrode is the potential developed (the tendency to lose or gain electrons) when the electrode is dipped in a 1 M solution of its own ions at 298 K (and 1 atm for gases), measured against the standard hydrogen electrode. By the IUPAC convention it is written as the reduction potential: $E^\circ_{\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}} = -0.76$ V and $E^\circ_{\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}} = +0.34$ V.

@s 16
**Derivation.** Consider a process at constant temperature $T$ and constant pressure $P$. The second law says that for a spontaneous process the total entropy of the system plus the surroundings increases:
$$\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} + \Delta S_{\text{surr}} > 0$$
The heat lost by the system at constant pressure, $-\Delta H$, is gained by the surroundings, which are so large that their temperature stays $T$:
$$\Delta S_{\text{surr}} = \frac{q_{\text{surr}}}{T} = -\frac{\Delta H_{\text{sys}}}{T}$$
Substituting,
$$\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta S_{\text{sys}} - \frac{\Delta H_{\text{sys}}}{T}$$
Multiplying by $-T$:
$$-T\Delta S_{\text{total}} = \Delta H_{\text{sys}} - T\Delta S_{\text{sys}}$$
Gibbs defined the left-hand side as the change in free energy, $\Delta G = -T\Delta S_{\text{total}}$, so (dropping the subscript)
$$\boxed{\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S}$$
Since $\Delta S_{\text{total}} > 0$ for a spontaneous change, $\Delta G < 0$: a process is spontaneous if $\Delta G$ is negative, non-spontaneous if positive and at equilibrium if zero.

**Why 'free energy'.** From $G = H - TS$ we see that the part $TS$ of the enthalpy is bound energy, the disordered thermal energy of the system that cannot be turned into work. The remaining part, $G$, is the energy that is free (available) for doing work. The decrease in Gibbs energy at constant $T$ and $P$ equals the maximum useful (non-expansion) work obtainable from the system, $-\Delta G = w_{\text{useful,max}}$ (for example the electrical work of a cell, $-\Delta G = nFE$). That is why the useful work of the system is called free energy.

**Functional isomers of C₃H₆O.** The two common functional isomers are
1. $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CHO}$, an aldehyde, **propanal**;
2. $\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3$, a ketone, **propanone** (acetone).
(Other less common isomers are the unsaturated alcohol prop-2-en-1-ol and the cyclic ether methyloxirane.)

**Tollen's test.** Tollen's reagent is ammoniacal silver nitrate, $[\text{Ag(NH}_3)_2]^+$. Warm it with each isomer in a clean test tube:
- *Propanal* is a reducing agent: it is oxidised to propanoate and the silver ion is reduced to silver, which deposits as a bright silver mirror on the wall of the tube.
$$\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CHO} + 2[\text{Ag(NH}_3)_2]^+ + 3\text{OH}^- \to \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{COO}^- + 2\text{Ag}\downarrow + 4\text{NH}_3 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
- *Propanone* is not oxidised by mild oxidising agents, so there is no silver mirror.

**Preparation by dehydrogenation of alcohols.** Passing the vapour of the alcohol over copper at 573 K (300 °C) removes two hydrogen atoms (over the copper catalyst at 573 K):
- a primary alcohol gives an aldehyde:
$$\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{OH} \longrightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CHO} + \text{H}_2$$
- a secondary alcohol gives a ketone:
$$\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)CH}_3 \longrightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3 + \text{H}_2$$
(Propan-1-ol gives propanal and propan-2-ol gives propanone.)

**(a) Dow's process (phenol from chlorobenzene).** Chlorobenzene is heated with aqueous NaOH at 623 K and 300 atm (the conditions over the arrow), giving sodium phenoxide, which is acidified:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{NaOH} \to \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$$

**(e) Phenol from benzenesulphonic acid.** The sodium salt of the acid is fused with NaOH at about 573 K; the sodium phenoxide formed is acidified:
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{SO}_3\text{Na} + 2\text{NaOH} \longrightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{ONa} + \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{ONa} + \text{HCl} \to \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{NaCl}$$

**Or — compound (A) is chloroform, CHCl₃** (its vapour is an anaesthetic, and conc. HNO₃ converts it into chloropicrin, CCl₃NO₂, the tear gas).

**Rate of a chemical reaction.** It is the change in the concentration of a reactant or product per unit time:
$$\text{rate} = -\frac{d[\text{reactant}]}{dt} = +\frac{d[\text{product}]}{dt}\quad(\text{mol L}^{-1}\,\text{s}^{-1})$$

**Factors affecting the rate:**
1. *Concentration.* The rate is proportional to the product of the concentrations of the reactants raised to the powers of their orders (law of mass action, $\text{rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n$). More concentrated reactants mean more molecules per unit volume, more collisions per second and a faster reaction. Magnesium burns faster in pure oxygen than in air.
2. *Temperature.* For most reactions the rate doubles or triples for a 10 K rise (temperature coefficient 2 to 3). A higher temperature gives molecules more kinetic energy, so a larger fraction has energy equal to or greater than the activation energy, as the Arrhenius equation $k = Ae^{-E_a/RT}$ shows. Food cooks faster in a pressure cooker.
3. *Catalyst.* A catalyst provides an alternative path of lower activation energy, so more collisions are effective and the rate rises, without the catalyst being consumed (MnO₂ in the decomposition of KClO₃; V₂O₅ in the contact process).
4. *Surface area of the reactants.* A solid reacts at its surface. Powdered reactants have a large exposed surface and so react much faster than lumps: powdered CaCO₃ reacts faster with dilute HCl than marble chips, and coal dust explodes in air.

**Numerical.** For a first order reaction $k = \dfrac{0.693}{t_{1/2}} = \dfrac{0.693}{3\ \text{h}} = 0.231\ \text{h}^{-1}$.
If the initial amount is 100, then 87.5% completion leaves $[A] = 12.5$:
$$t = \frac{2.303}{k}\log\frac{[A]_0}{[A]} = \frac{2.303}{0.231}\log\frac{100}{12.5} = 9.97\times\log 8 = 9.97\times0.9031 \approx 9\ \text{h}$$
Check: after 3 h, 50% is left; after 6 h, 25%; after 9 h, 12.5%. So three half-lives are needed and **t = 9 hours**.

**Or — solubility and solubility product**

| Point | Solubility | Solubility product |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | The maximum amount of solute that dissolves in a given amount of solvent at a given temperature to form a saturated solution | The product of the molar concentrations of the ions of a sparingly soluble salt in its saturated solution, each raised to the power of its coefficient |
| Symbol and unit | $S$ in g L⁻¹ or mol L⁻¹ | $K_{sp}$ (units depend on the salt, e.g. mol² L⁻² for AgCl) |
| Depends on | Temperature, nature of solute and solvent, and the presence of a common ion | Temperature only |
| Example | Solubility of AgCl is $1.3\times10^{-5}$ mol L⁻¹ | $K_{sp}(\text{AgCl}) = [\text{Ag}^+][\text{Cl}^-] = 1.8\times10^{-10}$ |
| Relation | For AgCl, $K_{sp} = S^2$; for $A_2B$ or $AB_2$ salts, $K_{sp} = 4S^3$ | |

**Application of the solubility product principle in qualitative analysis.** The principle states that a salt is precipitated from its solution when the ionic product exceeds its $K_{sp}$; it stays dissolved when the ionic product is less than $K_{sp}$. By adding a reagent with a common ion we control the concentration of the precipitating ion, so that only the cations of one group have ionic product greater than $K_{sp}$:
1. *Group I (Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺).* On adding dilute HCl, the ionic products $[\text{Ag}^+][\text{Cl}^-]$ etc. exceed the very small $K_{sp}$ of AgCl, PbCl₂ and Hg₂Cl₂, so they precipitate as chlorides. The chlorides of other groups have large $K_{sp}$ and stay in solution. Excess HCl is avoided, as complexes form.
2. *Group II (Pb²⁺, Cu²⁺, Cd²⁺, Hg²⁺, Bi³⁺, As³⁺).* H₂S is passed in the presence of dilute HCl. HCl is a source of H⁺, the common ion, which suppresses the ionisation of weak H₂S ($\text{H}_2\text{S} \rightleftharpoons 2\text{H}^+ + \text{S}^{2-}$) and so lowers $[\text{S}^{2-}]$. The ionic product exceeds $K_{sp}$ only for the very insoluble sulphides (CuS, CdS, HgS, PbS), while the more soluble ZnS, MnS, NiS and CoS stay in solution.
3. *Group III (Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, Cr³⁺).* NH₄Cl is added before NH₄OH. The common ion NH₄⁺ suppresses the ionisation of the weak base NH₄OH and keeps $[\text{OH}^-]$ low, so that only the hydroxides of low $K_{sp}$ (Fe(OH)₃, Al(OH)₃, Cr(OH)₃) are precipitated, while Mg(OH)₂, which has a higher $K_{sp}$, remains dissolved.
4. *Group IV (Zn²⁺, Mn²⁺, Ni²⁺, Co²⁺).* H₂S is passed in ammoniacal medium. The OH⁻ ions remove H⁺ and raise $[\text{S}^{2-}]$, so the sulphides of higher $K_{sp}$ (ZnS, MnS, NiS, CoS) are now precipitated.
5. *Group V (Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺).* (NH₄)₂CO₃ is added in the presence of NH₄Cl and NH₄OH; the carbonates BaCO₃, SrCO₃ and CaCO₃ precipitate, while Mg²⁺ (as soluble Mg-ammonium carbonate complex) stays in solution.
Thus the separation of the cations into groups, and the sharp precipitation of each, rests on the solubility product principle together with the common-ion effect.

**(c) Reactions in the different zones** (from the top to the bottom, the charge of calcined ore, coke and limestone moves down against the rising hot gases):
1. *Zone of reduction* (top, 400 to 700 °C): carbon monoxide reduces the oxide in steps:
3Fe₂O₃ + CO → 2Fe₃O₄ + CO₂; Fe₃O₄ + CO → 3FeO + CO₂; FeO + CO → Fe + CO₂ (spongy iron).
2. *Zone of slag formation* (800 to 1000 °C): limestone decomposes, CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, and lime unites with the silica gangue: CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag). Phosphorus pentoxide is removed as 3CaO + P₂O₅ → Ca₃(PO₄)₂.
3. *Zone of fusion* (1000 to 1200 °C): spongy iron melts, absorbs carbon and collects at the bottom.
4. *Zone of combustion* (about 1500 °C, the hottest part at the tuyeres): coke burns in the hot blast, C + O₂ → CO₂, and CO₂ + C → 2CO, which is the reducing gas.
The molten slag floats over the molten iron and both are tapped separately. The product is pig iron (about 4% carbon).

**(d) Applications of iron in daily life:**
1. Wrought iron is used for chains, anchors, nails, railings, gates and agricultural tools, as it is tough and malleable.
2. Cast iron is used for pipes, stoves, cooking pots, manhole covers, engine blocks and machinery bases, because it is hard and can be cast in moulds.
3. Steel (an alloy of iron with carbon) is used for bridges, buildings, rails, vehicles, ships, surgical instruments and kitchen utensils.
4. Iron compounds are used in medicine (ferrous sulphate for anaemia) and, as haemoglobin, iron carries oxygen in our blood. Iron is the cheapest and most abundant useful metal.

**Or — extraction of zinc.**
*Ore:* the chief ore is zinc blende, ZnS (others: calamine ZnCO₃, zincite ZnO).
*Principle:* zinc is a fairly reactive metal, but its oxide is easily reduced by carbon at a high temperature (above its boiling point, 907 °C), when the zinc distils as vapour. So the sulphide is first converted to oxide by roasting, and then reduced by carbon: $\text{ZnO} + \text{C} \to \text{Zn} + \text{CO}$.

*Process:*
1. *Concentration.* The ore is crushed and powdered and concentrated by froth flotation: the sulphide particles go up with the froth (pine oil, xanthate, air) and the gangue sinks.
2. *Roasting.* The concentrated ore is heated in excess of air in a reverberatory furnace at about 1200 K, giving zinc oxide: $2\text{ZnS} + 3\text{O}_2 \to 2\text{ZnO} + 2\text{SO}_2$. The SO₂ is used to make H₂SO₄. (A calcined carbonate ore gives ZnO directly: $\text{ZnCO}_3 \to \text{ZnO} + \text{CO}_2$.)
3. *Reduction (smelting).* The ZnO is mixed with powdered coke and heated at about 1673 K (1400 °C) in fire-clay retorts. $\text{ZnO} + \text{C} \to \text{Zn(vapour)} + \text{CO}$. Zinc vapour passes out through the clay condensers, where it condenses to molten zinc, and the first dusty part is the 'zinc dust'.
4. *Refining.* The crude zinc (spelter, 98% pure, with Pb, Cd, Fe, As) is purified by distillation, by liquation or by electrolysis (impure zinc as anode, pure zinc as cathode, ZnSO₄–H₂SO₄ electrolyte).

*Action of moist air.* On exposure to moist air (containing CO₂), zinc is slowly covered by a thin, tough, greyish layer of basic zinc carbonate: $4\text{Zn} + 2\text{O}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2 \to \text{ZnCO}_3\cdot3\text{Zn(OH)}_2$. This coat is adherent and insoluble and protects the metal underneath from further corrosion, which is why zinc is used to galvanise iron. In dry air zinc is unaffected at ordinary temperatures.

**(a)(i) Radioactivity** is the spontaneous disintegration of the nucleus of an unstable (heavy) atom, with the emission of alpha particles, beta particles or gamma rays, to form a more stable nucleus. It is a nuclear property, independent of temperature, pressure or chemical combination, and was discovered by Henry Becquerel in uranium salts. Its SI unit is the **becquerel (Bq)**, one disintegration per second; the older unit is the curie, 1 Ci = $3.7\times10^{10}$ Bq (the activity of 1 g of radium).

**(ii) Artificial radioactivity** is the radioactivity induced in a stable nucleus by bombarding it with high-energy particles (α-particles, protons, neutrons or deuterons), so that an unstable radioactive isotope is formed (discovered by Irene Curie and F. Joliot in 1934). *Example:* aluminium bombarded with α-particles gives radioactive phosphorus,
$$^{27}_{13}\text{Al} + {}^{4}_{2}\text{He} \to {}^{30}_{15}\text{P} + {}^{1}_{0}\text{n}$$
and ³⁰P is a positron emitter, $^{30}_{15}\text{P} \to {}^{30}_{14}\text{Si} + {}^{0}_{+1}e$. Radioisotopes such as ⁶⁰Co and ¹³¹I are made in this way for medical use.
