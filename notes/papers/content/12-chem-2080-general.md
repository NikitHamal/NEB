@s 12
**(i) Relationship between normality and molarity.**
Normality is the number of gram equivalents of solute per litre of solution and molarity is the number of moles per litre. Let w g of solute of molar mass M₀ be present in V litres, and let its n-factor be n (basicity of an acid, acidity of a base, or the number of electrons exchanged in a redox reaction).
$$\text{Equivalent mass } E = \frac{M_0}{n}$$
$$N = \frac{w/E}{V} = \frac{w\,n}{M_0\,V} \qquad M = \frac{w/M_0}{V}$$
Therefore **N = M × n**. For example, 1 M H₂SO₄ is 2 N, 1 M NaOH is 1 N, and 1 M Na₂CO₃ is 2 N.

**(ii)** NaOH is a monoacidic base, so its normality equals its molarity: N = 0.8 N.
H₂SO₄ is dibasic, so N = 0.4 × 2 = 0.8 N.
Milli-equivalents of base = 0.8 × 20 = 16 meq
Milli-equivalents of acid = 0.8 × 25 = 20 meq
The acid is in excess: 20 − 16 = 4 meq of acid remain (the solution is acidic) in the total volume 20 + 25 = 45 mL.
$$N_{resulting} = \frac{4}{45} = 0.089\ \text{N}$$
The resulting solution is **0.089 N** (acidic), i.e. 0.044 M with respect to H₂SO₄.

@s 13
**(i) Derivation of ΔG = ΔH − TΔS.**
For a process at constant temperature T and pressure P the process is spontaneous if the total entropy change of the universe is positive:
$$\Delta S_{total} = \Delta S_{sys} + \Delta S_{surr} > 0$$
The heat lost by the system at constant pressure is ΔH, and it is gained by the surroundings at the same temperature, so
$$\Delta S_{surr} = -\frac{\Delta H_{sys}}{T}$$
Substituting,
$$\Delta S_{total} = \Delta S_{sys} - \frac{\Delta H_{sys}}{T}$$
Multiplying by −T: $-T\Delta S_{total} = \Delta H_{sys} - T\Delta S_{sys}$.
Gibbs defined the free-energy function G = H − TS, so that at constant T and P, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, and
$$\Delta G = -T\Delta S_{total}$$
A process is spontaneous when ΔS_total > 0, that is when **ΔG < 0**; it is at equilibrium when ΔG = 0 and non-spontaneous when ΔG > 0.

**(ii) Decrease in free energy equals useful work.**
Consider a reversible change at constant T and P. By the first law, ΔU = q − w, where w is the total work done by the system. For a reversible process q = TΔS, so the maximum work is
$$w_{max} = T\Delta S - \Delta U$$
This work has two parts: the pressure–volume work PΔV and the useful (net, non-expansion) work w_useful, such as electrical work:
$$w_{max} = P\Delta V + w_{useful}$$
$$w_{useful} = T\Delta S - \Delta U - P\Delta V = -(\Delta U + P\Delta V - T\Delta S)$$
Since ΔH = ΔU + PΔV,
$$w_{useful} = -(\Delta H - T\Delta S) = -\Delta G$$
Hence **−ΔG = w_useful(max)**: the decrease in free energy of a system at constant temperature and pressure equals the maximum useful work that can be obtained from it. For a cell, −ΔG = nFE°.

@s 14
**Open-hearth process (Siemens–Martin process).** It is used to convert pig iron into steel of good and controlled quality, using pig iron, scrap iron and iron ore as the charge.

**Furnace.** A large shallow, saucer-shaped hearth, 10 to 15 m long, is built of refractory brick with a low roof. The lining is *acidic* (silica) if the pig iron is low in phosphorus and *basic* (dolomite or magnesite, MgO·CaO) if the iron is rich in phosphorus. A pair of regenerators (chambers of checker brickwork) stand at each end. Producer gas and air are pre-heated by passing through the hot bricks of one regenerator, burn over the charge and reach about 1700 K; the waste gases heat the other regenerator, and the flow is reversed every 15 to 20 minutes, so heat is saved.

**Charge.** Pig iron + scrap steel + haematite (Fe₂O₃, the oxidising agent) + limestone (flux), about 8 to 10 tonnes per heat in modern furnaces of 100 tonnes or more.

**Chemistry.** The flame and the haematite oxidise the impurities:
3C + Fe₂O₃ → 2Fe + 3CO↑ (carbon burns off as CO)
Si + 2FeO → SiO₂ + 2Fe; 2Mn + O₂ → 2MnO
4P + 5O₂ → P₄O₁₀ (and S + O₂ → SO₂)
Slag formation: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃; P₄O₁₀ + 6CaO → 2Ca₃(PO₄)₂ (in the basic-lined furnace). The slag floats on the molten metal.

**Control and finishing.** The process takes 8 to 10 hours. Samples are taken from time to time and their carbon content is tested. When the required carbon content is reached, a calculated amount of ferromanganese or spiegeleisen (Fe + Mn + C) is added to remove dissolved oxygen and to restore the carbon, and the molten steel is tapped from the tap-hole into ladles and cast.

**(b)** General characteristics of transition metals (any four):
1. *Variable oxidation states:* (n−1)d and ns electrons both take part in bonding, e.g. Mn shows +2 to +7, Fe shows +2 and +3.
2. *Coloured compounds:* d–d transitions of unpaired electrons, e.g. Cu²⁺ blue, Fe³⁺ yellow-brown, MnO₄⁻ purple.
3. *Catalytic activity:* variable oxidation states and surface adsorption, e.g. Fe in the Haber process, V₂O₅ in the contact process.
4. *Paramagnetism:* unpaired d electrons, e.g. Fe³⁺ (5 unpaired electrons) is strongly paramagnetic.
5. *Complex formation* (small, highly charged ions with vacant d orbitals) and the formation of alloys and interstitial compounds. They are also hard metals with high melting and boiling points.

@s 16
**Preparation of chlorobenzene (any two).**
1. *Direct chlorination of benzene* in the dark with a halogen carrier:
C₆H₆ + Cl₂ —(anhyd. FeCl₃ or AlCl₃)→ C₆H₅Cl + HCl
2. *Sandmeyer reaction:* benzenediazonium chloride is warmed with cuprous chloride in HCl:
C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ —(CuCl / HCl, Δ)→ C₆H₅Cl + N₂↑
3. *Raschig process (industrial):* C₆H₆ + HCl + ½O₂ —(CuCl₂ / FeCl₃, 500 K)→ C₆H₅Cl + H₂O

**Why ortho and para products?** In chlorobenzene, Cl is more electronegative than carbon and withdraws electrons through the σ-bond (−I effect), which deactivates the ring overall. But the lone pair on Cl takes part in resonance with the ring (+R effect), which increases the electron density mainly at the ortho and para positions:
the resonance structures show a negative charge on C-2, C-4 and C-6 only. An electrophile therefore attacks the ortho and para positions. The arenium ion formed by ortho or para attack has an extra resonance structure in which Cl, with its octet complete, carries the positive charge, so it is more stable than the arenium ion from meta attack. Hence the substitution is ortho/para directing, though slower than in benzene.

**Action on chloral.** Chlorobenzene condenses with chloral (trichloroethanal) in the presence of conc. H₂SO₄ to give the insecticide DDT:
2C₆H₅Cl + CCl₃CHO —(conc. H₂SO₄)→ (p-ClC₆H₄)₂CH–CCl₃ + H₂O
(DDT = 1,1,1-trichloro-2,2-bis(p-chlorophenyl)ethane.)

@s 17

**(a) Factors affecting the rate of a reaction.**
1. *Concentration of reactants:* the rate increases with concentration, because more molecules per unit volume mean more collisions (rate law: rate = k[A]^m[B]^n). For gases, a rise in pressure has the same effect.
2. *Temperature:* a rise of 10 °C roughly doubles or triples the rate (temperature coefficient 2 to 3). More molecules have energy greater than the activation energy: $k = Ae^{-E_a/RT}$ (Arrhenius equation).
3. *Catalyst:* it provides an alternative path with lower activation energy and so increases the rate without being consumed, e.g. MnO₂ in the decomposition of KClO₃.
4. *Nature of reactants:* ionic reactions are fast (AgNO₃ + NaCl), those involving bond-breaking in covalent molecules are slow.
5. *Surface area:* the finer a solid is divided, the larger the exposed area and the faster the reaction (powdered CaCO₃ against a lump).
6. *Light (radiation):* photochemical reactions such as H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl are sped up by light.

**(b)** Let the rate law be rate = k[A]ˣ[B]ʸ.

(i) *Order with respect to B:* experiments 3 and 4 have the same [A] = 0.5 but [B] changes 0.1 → 0.5 (five times), while the rate stays at 1.0 × 10⁻². So $5^y = 1$ and **y = 0**; the reaction is zero order with respect to B.
*Order with respect to A:* experiments 1 and 3 have the same [B] = 0.1 but [A] changes 0.1 → 0.5 (five times), while the rate changes from 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ to 1.0 × 10⁻², a factor of 25:
$$\frac{r_3}{r_1} = \left(\frac{0.5}{0.1}\right)^x \Rightarrow 25 = 5^x \Rightarrow x = 2$$
The reaction is **second order with respect to A**, and the overall order is 2 + 0 = 2.
Check with experiment 2: [A] doubled (0.1 → 0.2) should multiply the rate by 2² = 4: 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 4 = 1.6 × 10⁻³ ✓ (and B has no effect).

(iii) **Rate law:** rate = k[A]² (independent of [B]).

(ii) Rate constant, from experiment 1:
$$k = \frac{\text{rate}}{[A]^2} = \frac{4.0\times10^{-4}}{(0.1)^2} = 4.0\times10^{-2}\ \text{L mol}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$$
Check with experiment 3: $1.0\times10^{-2}/(0.5)^2 = 4.0\times10^{-2}$ ✓.

(iv) For [A] = 0.2 mol L⁻¹ and [B] = 0.35 mol L⁻¹:
$$\text{rate} = 4.0\times10^{-2} \times (0.2)^2 = 1.6\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol L}^{-1}\text{s}^{-1}$$
The value of [B] does not matter because the reaction is zero order in B.

**Or.** *Common ion effect:* the solubility of a sparingly soluble electrolyte is decreased by adding a soluble electrolyte that has an ion in common with it. *Solubility product principle:* a salt precipitates when the ionic product exceeds its solubility product, Q > K_sp, and the precipitate dissolves if Q < K_sp. Both principles are used in the group separation of cations in qualitative analysis:

| Group | Group reagent | How the principles are used |
|---|---|---|
| II (Pb²⁺, Cu²⁺, Hg²⁺, Cd²⁺, Bi³⁺ ...) | H₂S in presence of dil. HCl | HCl supplies H⁺, which shifts H₂S ⇌ 2H⁺ + S²⁻ to the left, lowering [S²⁻]. Only the very insoluble sulphides (CuS, PbS, HgS; very low K_sp) reach their K_sp and precipitate. ZnS, NiS, MnS, CoS (higher K_sp) stay dissolved. |
| III (Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, Cr³⁺) | NH₄OH in presence of NH₄Cl | NH₄Cl supplies NH₄⁺, which suppresses the ionisation of NH₄OH, so [OH⁻] is small. Only the hydroxides with very low K_sp (Fe(OH)₃, Al(OH)₃, Cr(OH)₃) precipitate; Mg(OH)₂ and Mn(OH)₂ do not. |
| IV (Zn²⁺, Ni²⁺, Co²⁺, Mn²⁺) | H₂S in presence of NH₄OH (alkaline) | OH⁻ removes H⁺ and raises [S²⁻], so sulphides of higher K_sp now precipitate. |
| V (Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺) | (NH₄)₂CO₃ with NH₄Cl | NH₄Cl keeps the carbonate concentration low enough that Mg²⁺ stays in solution while the carbonates of Ba, Sr and Ca precipitate. |
| I (Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺) | Dil. HCl | Q = [M⁺][Cl⁻] exceeds K_sp of AgCl, PbCl₂, Hg₂Cl₂, which precipitate. Excess Cl⁻ is avoided because AgCl dissolves as the complex [AgCl₂]⁻. |

*Numerical.* Assume the HCl is added to 1 litre of saturated AgCl solution without a change in volume.
AgCl ⇌ Ag⁺ + Cl⁻, K_sp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻] = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰
Solubility of AgCl in water: $s = \sqrt{K_{sp}} = \sqrt{1.8\times10^{-10}} = 1.34\times10^{-5}$ mol L⁻¹, so the saturated solution has [Ag⁺] = [Cl⁻] = 1.34 × 10⁻⁵ M.
After adding 0.2 mol HCl, [Cl⁻] ≈ 0.2 M (the original 1.34 × 10⁻⁵ is negligible). To keep K_sp constant,
$$[Ag^+] = \frac{1.8\times10^{-10}}{0.2} = 9.0\times10^{-10}\ \text{M}$$
Moles of Ag⁺ removed as AgCl = 1.34 × 10⁻⁵ − 9.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ ≈ 1.34 × 10⁻⁵ mol.
Mass precipitated = 1.34 × 10⁻⁵ × 143.5 = **1.93 × 10⁻³ g (about 1.9 mg per litre)**.
The new solubility is only 9.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ mol L⁻¹, about 15 000 times smaller than in pure water: the common ion effect.

*Comparison of boiling points:* **1° > 2° > 3°.** A primary amine has two N–H bonds, so it forms the most intermolecular hydrogen bonds (N–H···N). A secondary amine has only one N–H, so hydrogen bonding is weaker. A tertiary amine has no N–H, so it cannot form hydrogen bonds with its own molecules; only weak dipole–dipole and van der Waals forces act, and it boils lowest.

**(ii)** In aqueous solution the order of increasing basic strength is
**(CH₃)₃N < CH₃CH₂CH₂NH₂ < CH₃CH₂NHCH₃** (3° < 1° < 2°).
The alkyl groups push electrons to nitrogen (+I effect), which raises basicity. But the conjugate acid is stabilised by hydrogen bonding with water, and this is most effective for R₃NH⁺ the less alkylated the amine, and steric hindrance to solvation is greatest in the tertiary amine. The balance of the +I effect, solvation and steric hindrance makes the secondary amine strongest and the tertiary amine weakest (pK_b ≈ 3.0, 3.3 and 4.2).

**(iii) Separation by Hoffmann's method** (diethyl oxalate method).
1. The mixture of amines is treated with diethyl oxalate, (COOC₂H₅)₂. The three classes behave differently:
2RNH₂ + (COOC₂H₅)₂ → (CONHR)₂ + 2C₂H₅OH (solid dialkyl oxamide, from the primary amine)
R₂NH + (COOC₂H₅)₂ → R₂N–CO–COOC₂H₅ + C₂H₅OH (liquid dialkyl oxamic ester, from the secondary amine)
R₃N + (COOC₂H₅)₂ → no reaction (tertiary amine)
2. The mixture is distilled. The unreacted tertiary amine, (CH₃)₃N (b.p. 3 °C), distils over first and is collected.
3. The residue is cooled. The solid oxamide of the primary amine is filtered off; the liquid oxamic ester of the secondary amine is the filtrate, and it is recovered from the other liquids by distillation.
4. Each product is hydrolysed separately by boiling with aqueous KOH:
(CONHR)₂ + 2KOH → 2RNH₂ + (COOK)₂ (primary amine, distilled off)
R₂NCOCOOC₂H₅ + 2KOH → R₂NH + (COOK)₂ + C₂H₅OH (secondary amine, distilled off)
So the primary, secondary and tertiary amines are obtained separately.
