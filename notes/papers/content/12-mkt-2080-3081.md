### Group A

@s 1
A **target market** is the specific group of customers, with similar needs and buying power, that a firm selects from the total market and decides to serve with a particular marketing mix.

@s 2
Accessory equipment is equipment that helps in production but does not become part of the product. Two examples are **hand tools** and **office equipment** such as computers, typewriters and desks, or portable drills.

@s 3
For a physical product I would use **primary packaging** (the container touching the product, such as a bottle or wrapper) together with **secondary packaging** (a carton that protects and displays it) and **shipping packaging** for transport, chosen for protection, convenience and promotion.

@s 4
**Allowances** are reductions from the list price given to buyers for specific reasons, for example a trade-in allowance for an old product, or a promotional allowance for advertising the seller's goods.

@s 5
A **distribution channel** is the route or path through which goods move from the producer to the final consumer or industrial user, through intermediaries such as wholesalers and retailers.

@s 6
Two consumer promotion tools: **samples** (free trial of the product) and **discount coupons**. Premiums, contests and rebates are also used.

@s 7
Two features of service marketing: services are **intangible** (they cannot be seen or touched before purchase) and **inseparable** (production and consumption occur at the same time). Perishability and variability are also features.

@s 8
**Social responsibility in marketing** is the obligation of a marketer to take decisions and actions that serve the interests of the society and the environment as well as the profit of the firm.

@s 9
Two costs of ignoring social responsibility: **loss of reputation and customers**, and **legal penalties, fines or boycotts**. A producer may also face higher cost to clean up pollution.

@s 10
Two functions of middlemen: they **make goods available** at the right place and time by **storing and transporting** them, and they **provide credit and market information** to producers and customers.

@s 11
Two physical qualities of a good salesperson: a **pleasing and healthy personality** with neat dress, and a **good voice and gestures** (clear speech, smile, good posture) that create a favourable impression.

### Group B

@s 12
**Shopping products** are consumer goods for which buyers compare price, quality, style and suitability in several shops before buying. Examples are clothes, shoes, mobile phones, furniture and electrical appliances.

**Features:**
1. **Comparison.** Customers spend much time and effort to compare alternatives before choosing.
2. **Higher price** than convenience goods, so the purchase is made with care.
3. **Less frequent purchase**, usually after a long interval.
4. **Selective distribution.** They are sold through fewer outlets, often in a shopping area, so that customers can compare.
5. **Brand and style are important**, and **sales support** by trained salespersons is useful.
6. **Promotion** relies on advertising and personal selling to show differences from competing products.

Shopping products may be homogeneous (same quality, price compared) or heterogeneous (style and quality compared).

@s 13
**External factors** are those outside the control of the firm that influence the price decision:
1. **Demand and customers.** The buyers' capacity to pay, their needs and the elasticity of demand limit the highest price.
2. **Competition.** The price and quality of competitors' products; a firm cannot charge much more than close substitutes.
3. **Economic conditions.** Inflation, recession, interest rates and purchasing power change what buyers accept.
4. **Government regulations.** Price controls, taxes, VAT, import duties and consumer protection laws.
5. **Channel members.** Wholesalers and retailers expect a margin, which affects the final price.
6. **Suppliers' prices** and cost of raw materials.
7. **Social and cultural factors and technology**, which change attitudes toward the product.

I would study all these before fixing the price within the range between the cost and the price buyers will pay.

@s 14
**Meaning.** Physical distribution (marketing logistics) is the set of activities involved in moving finished goods from the producer to the customer: order processing, transportation, warehousing, inventory control and materials handling. It makes the product available at the **right place, in the right quantity, at the right time and at the least cost**.

**Objectives:**
1. To **deliver goods on time** and in good condition so that customers are satisfied.
2. To **minimise the total distribution cost** (transport, storage, inventory).
3. To **ensure availability** of products in all the target markets and to avoid stock-outs.
4. To **keep the inventory at a suitable level**, so that capital is not blocked.
5. To **speed up order processing** and flow of information.
6. To **reduce damage and loss** of goods during handling and transit.
7. To **gain a competitive advantage** by better service than rivals.

@s 15
**Advertising** is paid, non-personal communication about a product by an identified sponsor through the mass media. From the **customers' point of view** its importance is:
1. **Information.** It tells customers about the availability, features, price and place of products, helping them to choose.
2. **Better choice.** Competing advertisements show alternatives, so customers can compare and buy the best.
3. **Quality assurance.** Advertisers must maintain the promised quality to protect the brand, which benefits customers.
4. **Saves time and effort** in searching for goods.
5. **Education.** Advertisements teach the use of new products and spread awareness on health, safety and social issues.
6. **Lower price.** By increasing sales and economies of scale, advertising can bring prices down.
7. **Standard of living.** It creates awareness of new goods and so raises the standard of living.
8. **Entertainment** and employment in media and creative industries.

@s 16
An **indoor salesperson** sells inside the shop, showroom or counter to customers who come in, so the steps are:
1. **Preparation.** Know the product, its price, uses, competing goods and the stock position, and keep the shop neat.
2. **Greeting and approach.** Welcome the customer with a smile and politeness and find out the need.
3. **Determining needs.** Ask questions and listen to understand what the customer wants, the budget and the purpose.
4. **Presentation and demonstration.** Show suitable goods, explain the features and benefits and let the customer touch and try them.
5. **Handling objections.** Answer doubts about price, quality or brand honestly and with evidence.
6. **Closing the sale.** Recognise the buying signal, suggest the choice and complete billing and packing.
7. **Follow-up and after-sale service.** Thank the customer, give guarantee information and invite him or her to return, which builds loyalty.

@s 17
**Agriculture marketing** is the set of activities that move farm products from the farmer to the consumer: assembling, grading, packing, storage, transport, processing and sale. Nepal is an agricultural country, with more than half of the people dependent on farming, so the **prospects** are good:
1. **Large domestic demand** from a growing urban population and from hotels, restaurants and processors.
2. **Export potential** in organic and high-value products such as tea, coffee, cardamom, ginger, honey, herbs and vegetable seeds, and access to the markets of India, China and other countries.
3. **Diverse climate and geography**, which allow off-season vegetables, fruits, flowers and herbs.
4. **Government support:** subsidies, the Agriculture Development Strategy, collection centres, cold storage and agricultural markets, and cooperatives.
5. **Agro-processing and commercialisation** can add value and employment, and the growth of road and communication links brings farmers closer to buyers.
6. **Tourism and organic farming** raise the demand for local and healthy food.

The prospects can be realised only by improving storage, transport, market information, grading and price support, since these weaknesses presently limit farm income.

**Or (Q17). Internet marketing.** Yes, internet marketing is becoming very popular in the 21st century, because: (i) **internet and smartphone use** has grown rapidly, so most customers are online; (ii) it is **low-cost and wide-reaching**, since a firm can reach local and global customers with a website, email or social media at a small cost; (iii) it is **interactive**, giving instant feedback, personalised offers and two-way communication; (iv) it is **available 24 hours**, and customers can compare prices and order from home; (v) results can be **measured** (clicks, views, sales), so the plan can be improved; (vi) **e-payment and delivery services** like eSewa, Khalti and online shops make buying easy; and (vii) it favours **small businesses**, which can compete with large firms. Problems of trust, security and low internet literacy remain, but the growth justifies the statement.

@s 18
**Strategic barriers** are the obstacles that prevent an organisation from growing as planned. The main ones are:
1. **Lack of vision and clear strategy.** Without clear goals, resources are scattered and growth is slow.
2. **Limited finance.** Shortage of capital restricts expansion, technology and promotion.
3. **Intense competition** from established firms and imports.
4. **Resistance to change** by managers and employees, which prevents adoption of new methods.
5. **Weak organisational structure and management skill.** Poor delegation and coordination.
6. **Rapid change in technology and customer taste**, which makes products obsolete.
7. **Government policy and political instability**, such as taxes, controls and unrest.
8. **Poor market information and research** leading to wrong decisions.

An organisation overcomes them through planning, training, innovation, and a flexible structure.

**Or (Q18). Levels of morality development (Kohlberg).** In marketing, the moral development of a person or firm can be outlined in three levels, each with two stages.
- **Pre-conventional level.** The marketer acts to **avoid punishment** (stage 1) and to **gain rewards** (stage 2), for example obeying the law only to avoid a fine or giving honest weight only to get more sales.
- **Conventional level.** The marketer wants to be **approved by others** (stage 3) and to **follow rules and duty** (stage 4), for example following industry codes, being a "good corporate citizen" and meeting the expectations of society.
- **Post-conventional level.** The marketer follows **social contract and democratic principles** (stage 5) and **universal ethical principles** such as justice and human rights (stage 6), for example refusing to sell a harmful product even if it is legal and profitable.

Moving up the levels builds trust and long-term profit.

@s 19
**Salesmanship** is the skill of persuading prospective customers to buy goods and services, while satisfying their needs. It can be treated as a **profession**, because it has the features of one:
1. **Specialised knowledge.** A salesperson needs knowledge of products, customers, psychology and selling methods, which are taught in courses.
2. **Training and education.** Sales training institutes, management programmes and degrees in marketing are available.
3. **Service motive.** A good salesperson helps customers to solve their problems and buys the right goods, and not only to make a sale.
4. **Code of ethics.** Honesty, fair dealing and courtesy are expected, and professional bodies such as sales associations promote them.
5. **Career and earnings.** There is a clear path to sales manager and marketing director, with salary, commission and bonus.
6. **Social importance.** Salesmanship moves goods to consumers, creates employment and raises the standard of living.

However, there is **no compulsory licence or statutory body**, so it is a growing, semi-professional occupation. With increasing competition it is moving toward full professional status.

### Group C

@s 20
**Brand.** A brand is a name, term, sign, symbol, design or a combination of these used to identify the goods or services of a seller and to distinguish them from those of competitors, for example "Wai Wai" noodles or "Dhaka topi". It has a **brand name** (the part that can be spoken, for example Coca-Cola) and a **brand mark** (the symbol, colour or design that cannot be spoken). A brand builds identity, recall and loyalty, helps in pricing and promotion, and gives a guarantee of quality.

**Trade mark.** A trade mark is a brand, or a part of it, that has been given **legal protection** by registration with the government (the Department of Industry in Nepal). The owner gets the **exclusive right** to use it and can sue anyone who copies it. In short, all trade marks are brands, but not every brand is a trade mark: a brand becomes a trade mark when it is registered.

| Basis | Brand | Trade mark |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Name or symbol to identify the product | Legally registered brand |
| Protection | Not necessarily protected | Fully protected by law |
| Registration | Not required | Required |
| Scope | Wider, includes all identification marks | Narrower, only the registered mark |

**Reasons for not branding (unbranded or generic goods).**
1. **Low cost.** Branding needs expenses on design, packaging, advertising and registration, which small firms cannot afford.
2. **Homogeneous products** such as salt, sugar, grain, vegetables, sand and cement are not easy to differentiate.
3. **Low price strategy.** Selling without a brand allows a cheaper price to customers.
4. **Risk of legal liability** and responsibility for poor quality is avoided.
5. **Small size and local market.** When the producer sells at the local level, personal contact is enough.
6. **Products sold by weight or in bulk**, where buyers decide by inspection.
7. **Short life or one-time products**, where brand loyalty cannot be built.

@s 21
Distribution gets the product to the right place at the right time, and in Nepal this depends mostly on **transportation** and **warehousing**.

**Transportation.** Nepal is a landlocked and mountainous country, and goods move mainly by road (the Mahendra, Prithvi and East-West highways), with some air and cable transport and limited rail. Good transport:
1. **Moves goods quickly and cheaply** from producers and ports to markets; for example vegetables from Dolakha to Kathmandu need quick delivery.
2. **Widens the market**, since the same product can reach remote districts.
3. **Brings price stability**, by removing local shortages and surpluses.
4. **Reduces damage and wastage**, especially of perishable goods.
5. **Links the producing and consuming regions**, such as Terai and hill areas.
However, poor roads, landslides and strikes raise cost and delay, so efficiency in the distribution system suffers.

**Warehousing.** A warehouse is a place to store goods between production and sale. It:
1. **Balances supply and demand.** Seasonal crops like paddy and potato are stored and sold through the year, and the goods of continuous production can wait for seasonal demand such as festivals.
2. **Protects goods** from damage, theft and spoilage; cold storage for fruit, vegetables and medicine.
3. **Helps the price.** The farmer can avoid distress sale at harvest, and the price is stabilised.
4. **Provides finance and services,** such as grading, packaging and warehouse receipts to get credit.
5. **Allows quick delivery** to retailers from a store close to the market.

Because the transport links and storage together decide **how fast, how safely and at what cost** the goods reach the customers, an effective distribution system largely depends on them. The statement is therefore well supported.

**Or (Q21). Channel conflict.** **Channel conflict** is a situation in which the members of a distribution channel (producers, wholesalers, retailers) disagree and compete because one member believes that another is acting against its interest. It can be **vertical** (between levels, e.g. producer and retailer, when the producer sells directly) or **horizontal** (between members at the same level, e.g. two dealers in the same area).

**Measures to resolve it:**
1. **Clear roles and territories.** Define the rights, duties and area of each member in a written agreement.
2. **Open communication.** Hold regular meetings, a dealer council and feedback channels to find causes early.
3. **Fair margins and incentives,** and pay discounts and bonuses that treat the members equally.
4. **Common goals.** Link the members through shared targets and profit sharing.
5. **Dual-distribution rules.** If the company also sells online or directly, set prices that do not hurt the dealers.
6. **Negotiation, mediation or arbitration** by a neutral person when the dispute continues.
7. **Training and support** to the weak members, and joint planning and promotion.
8. **Exchange of members or removal** of a member as the last resort.

@s 22
**Industrial marketing** (business-to-business marketing) is the marketing of goods and services to **organisations**, such as manufacturers, institutions and government, that use them to produce other goods, to operate their business or for resale, and not for personal consumption. Examples are machinery, raw materials, components, office supplies and consulting services. Its features are fewer but large buyers, derived demand, technical buying decisions, personal selling and negotiated prices.

**Problems of industrial marketing in Nepal:**
1. **Small market size** and limited purchasing power, which prevent large-scale production.
2. **Dependence on imports** of raw materials, machinery and technology, mostly from India, which raises cost and creates supply risk.
3. **Weak infrastructure,** especially unreliable electricity, poor roads and high transport cost.
4. **Shortage of capital** and high interest rates, which restrict buying and credit sales.
5. **Lack of skilled manpower** and technical sales staff who can explain complex products.
6. **Low technology and quality,** so that local goods find it hard to compete with imported goods.
7. **Lack of market information and research,** and poor promotion tools such as trade fairs.
8. **Political instability, strikes and labour problems,** and unstable policy.
9. **Long buying process and delayed payments,** especially by government buyers.

**Remedies.** Improving power and transport, giving credit and tax support, training, promoting industrial fairs and strengthening quality standards would help industrial marketing grow.
