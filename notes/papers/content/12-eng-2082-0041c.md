@q 1
Read the following text and do the tasks that follow:

Arteries of the heart blocked by **plaque** can reduce the flow of blood to the heart possibly resulting in heart attack or death. Plaque is actually fat and cholesterol that accumulates on the inside of the arteries. The arteries of the heart are small and can be blocked by such accumulations. There is a medical procedure that creates more space in the blocked artery by inserting and inflating a tiny balloon into the blood vessel. It is called coronary balloon **angioplasty**. Angioplasty means "blood vessel repair". When the balloon is inflated, it compresses the plaque against the wall of the artery, creating more space and improving the flow of blood.

Many doctors choose this technique, because it is less **invasive** than bypass surgery. Yes, both involve entering the body cavity, but in bypass surgery, the chest must be opened, the ribs must be cut, and the section of diseased artery must be removed and replaced. To replace it, the patient's body is opened, once again, to acquire a healthy section of artery. Usually, this blood vessel is removed from an artery located in the calf of the leg. This means the patient now has two painful incisions that must heal at the same time. There is far more risk in such bypass surgery than in angioplasty, which involves threading a thin tube, called a **catheter**, into the circulatory system and working it to the damaged artery.

Angioplasty may take between 30 minutes to 3 hours to complete. It begins with a distinctive dye that is injected into the bloodstream. A thin catheter is then inserted into the **femoral** artery of the leg, near the groin. The doctor monitors the path of the dye using x-rays. He moves the tube through the heart and into the plaque filled artery. He inflates the balloon, creating more space, deflates the balloon, and removes the tube. It is important to note that the plaque has not been removed; it has just been compressed against the sides of the artery. Sometimes, a **stent** may be implanted, a tiny tube of stainless steel that is expandable when necessary. Its function is to keep the artery open.

There is good news and there is bad news. The good news is that the statistics compiled are superb. Ninety percent of all angioplasty procedures are successful. The risk of dying during an operation of this type is less than 2%. The risk of heart attack is also small: 3-5%. Yet heart surgeons do not take any risk lightly; therefore, a team of surgeons stands ready to perform bypass surgery if needed. The length of hospitalization is only three days. The bad news is twofold. First, this procedure treats the condition but does not eradicate the cause. In 20% of the cases, there is a recurrence of plaque. Second, angioplasty is not recommended for all patients. The surgeons must consider the patient's age, physical history, how severe the blockage is, and, finally, the degree of damage to the artery before they make their determination.

**C. Answers.**
(a) It is a medical procedure that opens a blocked artery of the heart by inserting and inflating a tiny balloon in it; the balloon presses the plaque against the artery wall, creating space and improving blood flow. "Angioplasty" means "blood vessel repair".
(b) In angioplasty a thin catheter is threaded into the blood vessels to the damaged artery and a balloon is inflated, so the body is not cut open much. In bypass surgery the chest is opened, the ribs are cut and the diseased artery is replaced by a healthy section taken from the leg, leaving two painful wounds and greater risk.

@s 2
**(a) Neighbours.** At the end of the story the young man weeps when he hears the neighbours cheering at the fence for his newborn baby. His tears show regret for the way he had thought of them. He had been uneasy about the migrant families and their noisy ways, yet they gave gifts and care during the pregnancy and now shared his joy. He realises that the real community has taught him more than books, since "the twentieth-century novel had not prepared him for this."

**(b) A Devoted Son.** When Rakesh's result is announced, the news of his first position in the examination spreads quickly and the family's small house fills with joy. Relatives and neighbours crowd in to congratulate him, sweets are shared, and people praise the boy who came from a poor family. Rakesh, humble and respectful, touches his father's feet. His father Varma is overjoyed and proud, and he weeps with happiness. The atmosphere is one of warm, shared pride and great hope for the family.

**Or — On Libraries.** Oliver Sacks, the author, is not interested in the digital library because it has taken away what he loves most about a library: the books themselves and the freedom of wandering among the shelves. He was horrified to find the shelves of a library nearly empty, with most of the books thrown out after being digitised, and almost nobody objected. He felt that a crime had been committed and that centuries of knowledge were being destroyed. For him, screens cannot give the quiet companionship of readers, the surprise of finding an old book by chance, or the sense of community that a real library gives.

**(c) Every Morning I Wake.** The poet prays to God and not to other creatures because God is the creator and protector of all beings. Kings, rich men and scientists are themselves mortal and cannot save anyone from death, fear or trouble. Only the all-knowing Almighty can look after the helpless people of Milk Wood by day and night, understand their weakness, and judge them by their best qualities. The poet therefore has faith in God alone and asks for his mercy and protection.

**(d) Humility.** The Chinese nationalists assert that human history really began with the Yellow Emperor and the Xia and Shang dynasties of China. In their boastful view whatever the Westerners, Muslims, Indians and others achieved later was only a pale copy of the original Chinese breakthrough. They believe that human civilization started in China and spread from there, so their own culture, history and achievements stand at the centre of world history.

**Or — Knowledge and Wisdom.** Russell names Queen Elizabeth I as a ruler who combined knowledge with wisdom. She and Henry IV of France kept free from the common errors of their time, particularly the bitter fight between Protestants and Catholics. She did not let hatred or religious prejudice guide her; she was impartial, took a broad view and thought of the whole nation. Her rule shows wisdom, in Russell's sense, as freedom from narrow feeling and a sense of proportion.

**(e) A Matter of Husbands.** No, in the play an ordinary woman and an actress are different. The Earnest Woman is simple, honest and timid: she believes what she is told and fears losing her husband. The Famous Actress is clever, confident and skilled at acting and persuasion; she weaves a false story so smoothly that the wife believes it, while Alfred is hiding in her closet. The actress turns her talent for illusion into real life, whereas the ordinary woman cannot. Their difference is shown in the conversation and in the end.

@s 3
**(a)** In Lamichhane's story the tourist guest admires Nepal's beauty, the Buddha and the generosity of its people, and the guide takes her to a poor house where a child paralysed by polio lies, able to move only his eyes. The scene stands very close to the statue of the Buddha with half-closed eyes, and the child's gaze is compared to the Buddha's calm, steady look. The contrast is the writer's message: the country that honours a god of compassion does not see the suffering child beside the shrine. The guide's small lie that the visitor is a doctor makes the family hope and treat her as their own, and their eyes are filled with kindness and gratitude. Lamichhane thus indicates the gap between spiritual glory and real hardship, the neglect of the disabled and the poor, and the need for true compassion, which the tourist is meant to feel as the real Nepal.

**(b)** The poet of 'Soft Storm', Abhi Subedi, sees "homeless children crying with hunger under the bat-bearing trees" in Thamel, Kathmandu's lively tourist quarter. The reference is important because it places hungry, abandoned children beside the shops, hotels and visitors of a prosperous place and so shows a painful gap between wealth and poverty. The image comes from the poem 'Soft Storm' itself, so it is closely related to it: in the poem the speaker "becomes soft" at each disturbing sight, whether violence, a lost child or an innocent man beaten, and the crying children awaken his deepest compassion. They stand for the city's neglected humanity. The poet suggests that history and dreams play hide and seek in an open museum where compassion has almost disappeared, and the reference calls on readers to feel for the hungry and act.

**Or — The Bull.** Yes, I agree. In Bhimnidhi Tiwari's play the king's favourite bull Male dies, and the bull doctor Laxminarayan and the cowherds Gore and Jitman fear for their lives, because the king once threatened a punishment for anyone who reported such news. Laxminarayan does not speak the truth: he tells the king, with masterly flattery, that the bull "does not wake up, does not eat, does not move" but sleeps as if relaxed. The cowherds massage and fan the dead animal and weep loudly when the king himself declares it dead. The flattered king rewards them with money and orders a proper burial, and they sigh, "Thank God, we survived!" as soon as he leaves. An honest report would have brought punishment, while tearful flattery (*chakari*) brings reward. The play satirises a feudal system in which survival depended on pleasing the ruler, and honest work and wisdom counted for little.

@s 4
**The Minister in Disguise**

A minister wanted to know the real situation of the country, so he dressed as an ordinary civilian and went out alone. Nobody recognised him as he walked through markets and villages and listened to people. In one town a police patrol grew suspicious of his strange questions and took him for a terrorist. They arrested him and asked for his identity, but the minister denied saying who he was, because he wanted to see how they would behave. The police interrogated him cruelly and threatened to keep him in prison for years.

At last the minister revealed his identity. The police were shocked and apologised again and again. The minister forgave them but advised them to investigate carefully so that innocent people would not be troubled. He promised to see that no trouble came to ordinary citizens.

**Moral:** Power and uniform should never be used to harass the innocent; a careful, fair investigation protects justice.

**The Sunrise at Poon Hill**

Three years ago, in the winter holidays, I joined my cousins for a trek to Poon Hill in Myagdi. I was a city boy who thought a walk was something to be done on flat roads, so I started the trip with doubts and a heavy bag.

The first day began easily, but the stone steps to Ulleri seemed never to end. My legs burned, my breath came in gasps, and I wanted to turn back. A porter, hardly older than I, passed me with a load twice the size of mine and smiled. "Slowly, slowly," he said. I felt ashamed of my complaining and walked on, one step at a time.

On the second morning we woke at four and climbed in the dark with torches. The cold bit my fingers and the stars were so close that I felt I could touch them. When we reached the top, the whole crowd stood silent. Slowly the first golden light touched the peak of Dhaulagiri, and then Annapurna glowed like a lamp. Tears filled my eyes. I had never felt so small and yet so happy.

On the way down, a teahouse family shared their warm millet porridge and refused extra money. Their kindness taught me as much as the mountains.

That trip changed me. I learned that every great goal is reached by patient steps, that difficulty is not a reason to quit, and that the simplest people can be the most generous. Since then, whenever I face a hard task, I remember the porter's words and the light on the mountains, and I tell myself to go slowly, but go on.
