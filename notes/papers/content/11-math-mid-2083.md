@s 11
**Equivalence by truth table (4 marks).**

| $p$ | $q$ | $p\rightarrow q$ | $\sim p$ | $\sim p\vee q$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T | T | T | F | T |
| T | F | F | F | F |
| F | T | T | T | T |
| F | F | T | T | T |

The columns for $p\rightarrow q$ and $\sim p\vee q$ are identical (T, F, T, T) in all four rows, so $p\rightarrow q \equiv \sim p\vee q$.

@s 12
**Venn diagram (4 marks).** Let $M$ and $S$ be the sets of students studying Mathematics and Science. $n(M\cup S) = 50 - 8 = 42$. By the addition formula:

$$n(M\cup S) = n(M)+n(S)-n(M\cap S)$$
$$42 = 30+25-n(M\cap S)$$
$$n(M\cap S) = 13$$

So **13 students** study both subjects. Only Mathematics: $30-13=17$. Only Science: $25-13=12$. Check: $17+13+12+8=50$.

@s 13
**Irrationality of $\sqrt2$ (4 marks).** Use proof by contradiction. Suppose $\sqrt2$ is rational: $\sqrt2 = p/q$ where $p$ and $q$ are integers, $q\ne0$, with no common factor other than 1.

$$2 = \frac{p^{2}}{q^{2}}$$
$$p^{2} = 2q^{2}$$

So $p^{2}$ is even, which forces $p$ to be even. Write $p=2k$:

$$4k^{2} = 2q^{2}$$
$$q^{2} = 2k^{2}$$

So $q^{2}$ is even, and $q$ is even. Now $p$ and $q$ both have the factor 2, contradicting that they have no common factor. The assumption is false, so $\sqrt2$ is irrational.

@s 14
**Domain and range (4 marks).** $f(x)=\sqrt{9-x^{2}}$ is real only when $9-x^{2}\ge0$:

$$x^{2}\le9 \iff -3\le x\le3$$

*Domain* $=[-3,3]$.

For $x$ in the domain, $x^{2}$ varies from 0 to 9, so $9-x^{2}$ varies from 9 down to 0 and the square root is non-negative. The maximum is $f(0)=3$ and the minimum is $f(\pm3)=0$.

*Range* $=[0,3]$. (The graph is the upper half of the circle $x^{2}+y^{2}=9$.)

@s 15
**Arithmetic means (4 marks).** Six terms are in AP with $a=3$ and last term $t_6=23$.

$$t_6 = a+5d$$
$$23 = 3+5d$$
$$d = 4$$

The means are $3+4=7$, $11$, $15$ and $19$: the series is $3+7+11+15+19+23$.

$$S_6=\frac{n}{2}(a+l)=\frac62(3+23)=78$$

Check by adding: $3+7+11+15+19+23=78$.

@s 16
**Inverse of a matrix (4 marks).** $|A| = 2\times3-1\times5 = 1 \ne 0$, so $A^{-1}$ exists. For a $2\times2$ matrix, swap the diagonal elements, change the sign of the other two and divide by $|A|$:

$$A^{-1}=\frac{1}{1}\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-5&2\end{pmatrix}$$

*Verification:*

$$AA^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\5&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}3&-1\\-5&2\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}6-5&-2+2\\15-15&-5+6\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}=I$$

@s 17
**(a) Bijection (4 marks).** *One-to-one:* let $f(x_1)=f(x_2)$. Then $2x_1+3=2x_2+3$, so $x_1=x_2$. Different inputs give different outputs.

*Onto:* take any real $y$ and solve $y=2x+3$: $x=\dfrac{y-3}{2}$, a real number in the domain, and $f(x)=y$. So every element of the codomain has a pre-image: the range is all of $\mathbb R$.

Hence $f$ is a bijection. To find the inverse, put $y=2x+3$ and interchange $x$ and $y$: $x=2y+3$, so

$$f^{-1}(x)=\frac{x-3}{2}$$

**(b) Graph of $y=x^{2}-4x+3$ (4 marks).** Complete the square: $y=(x-2)^{2}-1$.
- *Vertex:* $(2,-1)$, the minimum point (the parabola opens upward, $a=1>0$).
- *Axis of symmetry:* $x=2$.
- *y-intercept:* $x=0$ gives $y=3$.
- *x-intercepts:* $y=0$: $(x-1)(x-3)=0$ gives $x=1$ and $x=3$.

@s 18
**(a) Geometric sequence (4 marks).** Let the terms be $\dfrac ar,\ a,\ ar$. The product is $a^{3}=1$, so $a=1$. The sum:

$$\frac1r+1+r=\frac{39}{10}$$
$$r+\frac1r=\frac{29}{10}$$
$$10r^{2}-29r+10=0$$

$$r=\frac{29\pm\sqrt{841-400}}{20}=\frac{29\pm21}{20}$$

So $r=\tfrac52$ or $r=\tfrac25$. The terms are $\tfrac25,\ 1,\ \tfrac52$ (or in the reverse order $\tfrac52, 1, \tfrac25$). Check: sum $=0.4+1+2.5=3.9$ and product $=\tfrac25\times1\times\tfrac52=1$.

**(b) Cramer's rule (4 marks).** The system is $2x+3y=13$ and $x-y=-1$.

$$D=\begin{vmatrix}2&3\\1&-1\end{vmatrix}=-2-3=-5$$
$$D_x=\begin{vmatrix}13&3\\-1&-1\end{vmatrix}=-13+3=-10$$
$$D_y=\begin{vmatrix}2&13\\1&-1\end{vmatrix}=-2-13=-15$$

Since $D\ne0$ the solution is unique: $x=\dfrac{D_x}{D}=\dfrac{-10}{-5}=2$ and $y=\dfrac{D_y}{D}=\dfrac{-15}{-5}=3$. Check: $2(2)+3(3)=13$ and $2-3=-1$.
