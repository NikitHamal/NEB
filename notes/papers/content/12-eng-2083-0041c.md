In a quiet corner of the bustling city, there stood an old, dusty library. While most people hurried past, its **unassuming** exterior hid an incredible secret. The librarian, an elderly woman named Ms. Abernathy, was not an ordinary librarian; she was the guardian of a magical library.

One sunny afternoon, Sarah, an eighth-grader with an **insatiable** love for books, stumbled upon the library. She had always been drawn to stories of magic and adventure, and something about the library's unassuming facade piqued her curiosity. As she stepped inside, a small bell above the door tinkled, announcing her arrival. Ms. Abernathy greeted her with a warm smile, sensing Sarah's keen interest in books.

Sarah soon discovered the true enchantment of the library. Its shelves held volumes that could transport readers to other worlds. She found herself **immersed** in tales of knights and dragons, enchanted forests, and talking animals. These books had a way of making her feel like she was right there, part of the adventure.

As the weeks passed, Sarah spent hours at the library, delving into one captivating story after another. She uncovered a book about a boy who could fly, and as she read, she felt her feet lifting off the ground. She found herself soaring over the city, experiencing the thrill of flight. Another day, she read about a time-traveling watch, and suddenly, she was witnessing historical events unfold before her eyes.

One day, as Sarah was browsing the shelves, she noticed a book titled "The Lost Amulet of Althoria." The cover featured a golden amulet with a shimmering blue gem. Ms. Abernathy explained that this was a unique book—it was unfinished. No one knew how the story ended because no reader had ever returned the book. Sarah decided to take on the challenge. She opened the book and was immediately drawn into a tale of a young adventurer, Alaric, on a **quest** to find the lost amulet. She felt herself journeying through dark forests, crossing treacherous rivers, and facing **mythical** creatures. The more she read, the deeper she became entangled in the story. It was as though she had become Alaric, feeling his determination and bravery.

As the days turned into weeks, Sarah's friends and family grew concerned. She had become so absorbed in the book that she neglected her usual activities. Ms. Abernathy gently reminded her to take breaks, but Sarah was determined to reach the end of the story.

Finally, one evening, as Sarah read the last page, she found herself standing in a clearing, facing an **ornate** door. Through the door, she saw a **dazzling** chamber with the lost amulet resting on a pedestal. With a deep breath, she opened the door and approached the amulet. As she touched it, a burst of blue light enveloped her, and she felt herself returning to the library.

When she opened her eyes, she was back among the dusty bookshelves, holding the book in her hands. Ms. Abernathy congratulated her on completing the unfinished story. Sarah had not only finished the tale but had also experienced an adventure unlike any other.

From that day on, Sarah continued to visit the magical library, understanding that the real magic was not just in the stories but in the way they made her feel and the adventures they allowed her to live.

@s 2
**(a) Neighbours.** In the young man's place, I would also feel deeply ashamed. He had judged the immigrant neighbours as noisy and different, yet they cared for his wife and cheered for his newborn child. I would weep, as he does, and then go out to thank them, apologise for my prejudice and share the joy with them. I would learn that kindness has nothing to do with language or class, and I would try to treat every neighbour as a friend from now on.

**(b) A Respectable Woman.** Mrs. Baroda had heard so much praise of her husband's friend that she expected Gouvernail to be a striking, impressive man, and she was ready to dislike him. In fact he was quiet, modest, and rather ordinary-looking, and he paid her little attention. Yet his calm and thoughtful manner slowly drew her towards him, until one moonlit night she felt a strong attraction. So the real Gouvernail was far from her expectation, and he disturbed her more than she had thought possible.

**Or — A Devoted Son.** Yes, in many ways Rakesh was a devoted son. A brilliant boy from a poor family, he topped the examination, became a doctor and never forgot his father Varma. He cared for him in old age with strict diets, medicines and injections, and refused to rest until his father was well. But his devotion was rigid. He treated his father as a patient and forbade the sweets and fried food Varma longed for, so the old man died feeling lonely. His duty was complete; his tenderness was not.

**(c) Every Morning I Wake.** The poet presents God as the great and loving protector of all. God is all-knowing and almighty; kings, scientists and the rich cannot save anyone from fear, trouble or death, but He can. He watches over the helpless people day and night, knows their secret weaknesses and their strengths, and judges them with mercy, not harshly. The poet therefore trusts in God's greatness and asks for his care and forgiveness for the ordinary people of the town.

**(d) On Libraries.** When Oliver Sacks was late for lunch or dinner as a boy, he could be found in the oak-panelled library of his family home. It was the quietest and most beautiful room of the house, with books on all four walls and a big table in the middle. He would be completely absorbed in a book there and forget the time.

**Or — Human Rights and the Age of Inequality.** The goal of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UN, 10 December 1948) is to set a common standard of basic rights for all human beings: freedom, equality, dignity, justice and respect, regardless of their race, religion or status. It aims to protect people from injustice, discrimination and abuse of power and to lay the foundation of freedom, peace and justice in the world. Samuel Moyn notes, however, that it does not deal with the gap between the rich and the poor.

**(e) Facing Death.** Yes, Strindberg shows despair powerfully and realistically. Mr. Durand is a poor man in exile, starving and burdened with debts, while creditors press him and his own daughters blame him for their poverty. Strindberg uses ordinary domestic talk, a cramped house and the desperate insurance plan to burn the home, so the reader feels the heavy, trapped atmosphere. When the father finally takes his own life, it seems a natural end of hopelessness, not an invented shock. This truth to ordinary suffering makes the despair believable and moving.

@s 3
**(a) A Very Old Man with Enormous Wings.** When Pelayo finds the very old man with huge wings lying in the mud of his courtyard, the neighbours first wonder at him, and a wise neighbour woman declares he is an angel who came for the sick child. Soon the crowd turns into a mob. The old man is locked in a chicken coop, people throw food and stones at him, pluck his feathers and even burn him with a branding iron. Pelayo and Elisenda charge admission and become rich. Father Gonzaga doubts that he is an angel and asks Rome, but does not help him. When a spider-woman fair arrives, people forget him completely.

The community's reaction shows how cruel, greedy and curious human beings can be. They see a suffering creature as a show or a business, not as someone in need of help, so the story criticises the lack of compassion and respect for anyone who is weak or different.

**Or — Marriage as a Social Institution.** Stephen L. Nock argues that marriage is much more than a private relationship between two persons. It is a social institution: it is defined by legal, moral and conventional assumptions, and it is linked with other institutions such as education, the economy and politics. Its rules come from outside the couple and give it "soft boundaries". Marriage creates social capital, a network of relatives and friends bound by trust, and it works as social control that guides men's and women's behaviour and ambitions. Nock holds that normative marriage is voluntary, mature, faithful and leads to parenthood.

I partly agree with him. Marriage does involve families, community and law, and it gives stability and responsibility to both partners. But I think that, today, equality, love and the free choice of the partners matter more than fixed roles such as the husband as the only earner. A good marriage should respect those personal choices while keeping its social duties.

**(b) 'A Day' (Emily Dickinson).** *Reference to the context.* These lines are from the poem 'A Day' by Emily Dickinson. In the poem the speaker describes a sunrise in a very simple and childlike way, as though she is telling someone how the sun rose "a ribbon at a time".

*Explanation.* In these lines the hills are personified as women who "untied their bonnets", that is, the mist and shade that covered them lifted as the sun came up. The bobolinks (songbirds) began to sing to welcome the dawn. The speaker then says softly to herself, with wonder, that the glowing light "must have been the sun". She does not announce it loudly; she is quietly amazed at the sudden beauty of morning.

*Significance.* The lines show the poet's joy in nature, her use of personification and her simple, musical language.

The Editor
The Kathmandu Post
Kathmandu

**Subject: Save nature to save the future**

Sir,

Through your esteemed newspaper, I wish to draw the attention of the public and the authorities to the growing problem of environmental pollution in our country.

Today the air of our cities is thick with smoke from vehicles, brick kilns and burning waste. Our rivers, such as the Bagmati, have turned into drains full of plastic and sewage. Noise from traffic and construction adds to the problem. The effects are serious: asthma and lung diseases among children and the elderly, dirty water that spreads diarrhoea and typhoid, and loss of fish, birds and farmland. Pollution also hastens climate change, which melts our Himalayan snow and causes floods and landslides.

We can still act. The government should enforce emission standards, ban single-use plastic and treat waste before it enters rivers. Citizens should use public transport, plant trees and segregate garbage. Schools and media should teach children to love nature.

If we save nature today, nature will save us tomorrow.

Yours faithfully,
Anita Gurung
Pokhara

**Model essay: Walking, a simple exercise for body, mind and society**

Walking is the simplest and cheapest form of physical activity, yet it brings benefits that few other exercises can match. It needs no equipment, no membership and no special training; anyone, young or old, can walk at any time and place. A person who walks regularly builds a healthier body, a calmer mind and better relations with other people.

Firstly, walking builds physical strength. A brisk walk of thirty minutes a day strengthens the heart, improves blood circulation and lowers blood pressure. It helps to control weight and blood sugar, and it protects against diabetes and heart disease. Walking also strengthens bones and muscles, improves balance and keeps joints flexible, which is very important as people grow older. Because it is gentle, there is little risk of injury.

Secondly, walking is good for mental health. When we walk, especially in a park or by a river, the body produces hormones that reduce stress and lift the mood. Many writers and scientists say that their best ideas came to them during a walk. Walking clears the head, improves concentration and memory and helps people sleep better. Doctors often advise walking to people who suffer from anxiety and mild depression.

Thirdly, walking develops social behaviour. Morning walkers meet neighbours, exchange greetings and make friends. Walking with family or friends gives time for conversation and strengthens bonds. Walking to school or to the market teaches children road discipline, patience and respect for others. It also encourages good citizenship, for a walker notices the dirt in the street and the needs of the community. Walking instead of driving reduces pollution and saves fuel, so it is a social service as well.

In conclusion, walking is much more than moving from one place to another. It strengthens the body, clears the mind and brings people together. If every one of us made walking a daily habit, we would have a healthier, happier and more friendly society.

**(a) noun.** "A beautiful present" has an adjective before it, and it is the thing given, so *present* is a noun (stress on the first syllable).
**(b) chide.** Dictionary order is by letters: *chide* (c-h-i) comes before *choice* (c-h-o-i). *Choose* (c-h-o-o), *chord* (c-h-o-r) and *chrome* (c-h-r) come after it.
**(c) looked after.** To look after means to take care of; the babysitter took care of the girl.
**(d) legal.** *il-* is used before words beginning with *l*: *legal* gives *illegal*.
**(e) chemical.** *Chaos* begins with the /k/ sound, as does *chemical*; *chance* begins with /tʃ/ and *circus* and *cinema* with /s/.
