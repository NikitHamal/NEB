### Group A

@s 1
Scarcity is the condition in which human wants are unlimited but the resources (land, labour, capital, time) that can satisfy them are limited and have alternative uses, so choice has to be made. It is the root problem of every economy.

@s 2
Total profit = TR − TC = (5Q² − 4Q + 10) − (4Q² − 6Q + 20) = **Q² + 2Q − 10**.

@s 3
Internal borrowing is the loan that a government raises from sources inside the country, such as treasury bills, development bonds, citizen saving bonds, the central bank and commercial banks, to finance a budget deficit. It is repaid in the domestic currency.

@s 4
Balance of trade records only the export and import of visible goods, while balance of payments records all economic transactions with the rest of the world (goods, services, transfers and capital movements). Hence balance of trade is only a part of balance of payments.

@s 5
Two measures to reduce poverty: (i) generating productive employment through agriculture, small industry and skill training; (ii) targeted programmes such as social security allowance, microfinance and free basic education and health for the poor.

@s 6
Two principles of the WTO: (i) non-discrimination (most-favoured-nation treatment and national treatment); (ii) freer and more predictable trade through the gradual lowering of tariffs and other trade barriers by negotiation. (Fair competition and special treatment for developing countries are also principles.)

@s 7
Two advantages of remittance in Nepal: (i) it is a large source of foreign exchange that finances the trade deficit and strengthens the balance of payments; (ii) it raises household income and consumption, which reduces poverty and pays for education, health and housing.

@s 8
Sustainable Development Goal 10, "Reduced Inequalities", aims to reduce inequality within and among countries.

@s 9
In the singular sense, statistics means a numerical measure or a single figure calculated from a sample or a set of observations, such as the mean, the standard deviation or the correlation coefficient. (In the plural sense it means the collection of numerical data.)

### Group B

@s 12
**Economic system adopted by Nepal (1 mark).** Nepal has adopted a **mixed economic system**, in which the private sector, the public sector and the cooperative sector work side by side. The constitution of Nepal (2072) names these three pillars of the national economy.

**Four features (4 marks).**
1. **Co-existence of three sectors.** Public, private and cooperative sectors all operate. The state owns electricity, water supply, the airline, the oil corporation and similar enterprises, while private firms run most industry, trade and services.
2. **Private property and freedom of choice.** Citizens may own property, start business and choose their occupation, and consumers choose what to buy, as in a market economy.
3. **Government regulation and planning.** The government guides the economy through periodic plans, the annual budget, monetary policy and regulatory bodies, and corrects market failures (for example it sets minimum wages and controls price of essentials).
4. **Role of the market together with welfare aims.** Prices are largely set by demand and supply, yet the state provides social security, public education and health, and tries to reduce poverty and inequality.
Other features: foreign aid and investment, and a large share of agriculture in employment.

**Or (Q12). Specialization (1 mark).** Specialization means that each person, firm, region or country concentrates on the work or production in which it is most efficient, and exchanges the surplus for what it does not produce. Division of labour is its commonest form.

**Four advantages (4 marks).**
1. **Higher productivity and output.** Repetition of one task raises speed and skill, so more is produced with the same resources.
2. **Lower cost of production.** Better use of machines and less wasted time reduce average cost and give the benefit of large scale.
3. **Better quality.** Workers who do one job repeatedly become experts and make fewer errors.
4. **Encourages invention and trade.** Simple repeated tasks are easy to mechanise, and specialisation creates the need for exchange between regions and countries, so trade expands (comparative advantage).

@s 13
**Ricardian theory of rent.** David Ricardo (1817) defined rent as *"that portion of the produce of the earth which is paid to the landlord for the use of the original and indestructible powers of the soil"*. According to him rent arises because land differs in fertility and is limited in supply, and it is a differential surplus.

**Assumptions.** (i) Land differs in fertility and situation; (ii) land is limited in supply; (iii) the law of diminishing returns operates; (iv) the market is perfectly competitive; (v) cultivation begins on the best land and then extends to inferior land as population grows; (vi) rent is determined only by the margin of cultivation.

**Explanation.** Suppose three grades of land A, B and C each receive the same dose of capital and labour. Grade A yields 40 quintals, B yields 30 and C yields 20. When only A is cultivated no rent is paid, since land is abundant. As demand for food rises, B is brought under the plough; the price is now set by the cost of producing on B, so A earns a surplus of 10. Later C is cultivated and becomes the **no-rent (marginal) land**: rent of A = 40 − 20 = 20 and rent of B = 30 − 20 = 10. Thus rent = produce of the given land − produce of the marginal land.

```figure caption="Ricardian rent: surplus of each grade over the no-rent marginal land (grade C)."

vals=[('Grade A',40),('Grade B',30),('Grade C (marginal)',20)]
for i,(n,v) in enumerate(vals):
    ax.bar(i,20,color='#cfd8e6',ec=INK,lw=0.8,width=0.55)
    if v>20: ax.bar(i,v-20,bottom=20,color='#fbe3c8',ec=INK,lw=0.8,width=0.55)
    ax.text(i,v+1,'output '+str(v),ha='center',fontsize=7.5)
    if v>20: ax.text(i,20+(v-20)/2,'rent '+str(v-20),ha='center',va='center',fontsize=7.5,color=ACCENT)
ax.text(2,10,'cost / no rent',ha='center',va='center',fontsize=7)

ax.set_ylabel('quintals per acre',fontsize=7.5); ax.set_ylim(0,48)
ax.tick_params(labelsize=7)
```

**Criticisms.** Rent also arises on the best land by scarcity; land does have some alternative uses; the "original and indestructible powers" are not truly indestructible; cultivation does not always start from the best land. Despite this, the theory explains well why rents differ between plots.

@s 15
The firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve (P = 200 − 8Q), so it is a monopolist and equilibrium is where **MR = MC** with MC cutting MR from below.

TR = P·Q = 200Q − 8Q², so MR = dTR/dQ = 200 − 16Q.
TC = 100 + 12Q², so MC = dTC/dQ = 24Q.

$$MR = MC \;\Rightarrow\; 200 - 16Q = 24Q$$
$$40Q = 200 \;\Rightarrow\; Q = 5$$
$$P = 200 - 8(5) = 160$$

**Check.** The slope of MC (24) is greater than the slope of MR (−16), so the second-order condition holds. TR = 160 × 5 = 800, TC = 100 + 12(25) = 400, so the profit is Rs. 400, the maximum. Equilibrium output is **5 units** and price is **Rs. 160**.

@s 16
A sound banking system acts as the circulatory system of an economy. Five roles in Nepal's development:
1. **Mobilisation of savings.** Banks collect small deposits from households in towns and villages through savings, fixed and recurrent accounts, and turn idle money into capital.
2. **Supply of credit for investment.** Loans to farmers, industries, hydropower and small business raise production and create jobs. The "deprived sector" lending rule and agricultural credit in Nepal are examples.
3. **Capital formation.** By channelling savings to productive investment, banks raise the rate of capital formation, the key to growth in a capital-scarce country.
4. **Promotion of trade and payment facilities.** Cheques, drafts, letters of credit, remittance transfer, mobile banking and ATMs make trade quick and safe, and provide foreign exchange for international business.
5. **Monetary policy and price stability.** The central bank (Nepal Rastra Bank) controls the money supply and credit through the bank rate, CRR, SLR and open market operations to maintain stable prices and the exchange rate.
Further roles: regional balance through branch expansion and microfinance, financial inclusion, and encouragement of entrepreneurship.

@s 17
Inequality of income, wealth and opportunity in Nepal is large (the Gini coefficient is about 0.3 to 0.4, and the richest group has a far higher share of income than the poorest). Five suggestions:
1. **Progressive taxation and redistribution.** Tax the rich at higher rates (income, property and inheritance taxes) and spend the revenue on the poor, through allowances and subsidies.
2. **Land reform and access to assets.** Distribute land to the landless and tenants, and give them credit and inputs, since unequal land holding is a major source of rural inequality.
3. **Employment generation.** Create productive jobs with the Prime Minister Employment Programme, agro-based and small industries and skills training so the poor earn regular income.
4. **Equal access to education and health.** Free and good-quality public schooling and basic health care raise the earning capacity of poor and marginalised groups.
5. **Inclusive and balanced regional development.** Roads, electricity and markets in remote hills and Madhesh, and reservation and special programmes for women, Dalits, indigenous people and the disabled, narrow regional and social gaps.
Better targeting of social security and the cooperative movement also help.

**Or (Q19). Paasche's index number (4 marks).** Paasche's method uses current-year quantities as weights:

@s 21
**Meaning.** Government expenditure is the spending by the central, provincial and local governments on administration, defence, social and economic services, development works and debt service. Classification arranges these into groups so the budget can be planned and judged.

**1. Recurrent and capital expenditure (Nepal's budget classification).**
- *Recurrent (current) expenditure* is the regular spending that does not create assets: salaries and allowances, office expenses, pensions, social security allowances, interest on loans, subsidies and operation and maintenance. It is a large share of the budget in Nepal because of the salary and social-security bill.
- *Capital expenditure* creates physical or financial assets and raises future production: roads, bridges, hydropower, airports, irrigation, buildings and purchase of shares. Capital expenditure is typically lower than the target because of weak project capacity.
- *Financing expenditure* (principal repayment of loans) is the third head in the Nepal budget.

**2. Development and non-development expenditure.** Development expenditure raises productive capacity (economic services such as agriculture, energy and transport, and social services such as education and health). Non-development expenditure covers administration, defence, law and order, interest payments and the like.

**3. Transfer and non-transfer (exhaustive) expenditure.** Transfer payments (old-age allowance, scholarships, subsidies, pensions) shift purchasing power without any good or service returned; non-transfer expenditure buys goods and services (salaries, construction).

**4. Productive and unproductive expenditure** (economic effect) and **revenue-yielding and non-yielding** expenditure are also used in public finance.

**5. Federal structure in Nepal.** Since federalism the government spends through three levels: the federal government, seven provinces and 753 local levels. Fiscal transfers (equalisation, conditional, complementary and special grants) move money from federal to provincial and local levels.

**Evaluation.** In Nepal recurrent expenditure usually exceeds 60% of the total budget while capital expenditure is low, which is a challenge. The aim is to raise the capital share and improve quality, so that spending contributes to growth, jobs and reduced poverty.

@s 22
**Introduction.** Nepal's foreign trade is the export and import of goods and services with other countries, mainly India (about 60 to 65% of trade), China and others. Imports far exceed exports, giving a chronic trade deficit (exports are only about one-tenth to one-fifth of imports).

**Major problems.**
1. **Huge and growing trade deficit.** Imports of petroleum, machinery, vehicles, gold and food are many times the value of exports (palm oil, soybean oil, textiles, carpets, tea, cardamom, herbs, and hydropower from this year), so the deficit has to be met by remittance and aid.
2. **Narrow export base and low value addition.** Exports are few and mostly primary or semi-processed (agricultural raw material), and have low demand and price in the world market.
3. **Dependence on India.** Most trade and transit is with one country, so changes in Indian policy, tariffs or non-tariff barriers (quality standards, quotas) hurt Nepal.
4. **Landlocked position and high transport cost.** Dependence on Kolkata/Haldia and the long road route raise the cost and time of trade; transit procedures are slow.
5. **Weak supply capacity and infrastructure.** Low productivity, power shortage in the past, poor roads and limited industrial base mean Nepali products cannot compete in price and quality.
6. **Non-tariff barriers and quality standards.** Strict sanitary and phytosanitary rules and testing facilities, certification and rules of origin stop many exports.
7. **Political instability, strikes and inefficient customs** also raise the cost of trade.
8. **Limited market diversification and preference loss.** After graduation from the LDC category Nepal will lose some trade preferences.

**Remedies (brief).** Diversify exports and add value to local products, build dry ports and transit links, improve quality standards, promote trade agreements and a trade-friendly policy, and increase production of import-substitute goods.

**Or (Q22). Advantages and disadvantages of foreign employment.**
**Introduction.** Foreign employment is work by Nepalis abroad, mostly in the Gulf, Malaysia, South Korea and India. Remittance inflow is about one-fourth of GDP, so it is an important part of the economy.

**Advantages.**
1. **Remittance and foreign exchange.** It finances the large trade deficit, raises the reserve and strengthens the balance of payments.
2. **Poverty reduction and higher living standard.** Remittance raises household income and spending on food, education and health; poverty fell sharply in the last two decades.
3. **Reduction of unemployment.** Many youths who cannot get jobs at home find employment, which eases pressure on the domestic labour market.
4. **Skills and experience.** Workers learn new skills and bring technology and savings back; some start enterprises on return.
5. **Capital for investment and consumption**, and growth of banking, real estate and transport services.

**Disadvantages.**
1. **Brain drain and labour shortage.** The young and skilled go abroad, so agriculture and industry face labour shortage and ageing in the villages.
2. **Social costs.** Family separation, effects on children and old people and mental stress.
3. **Risk, exploitation and deaths.** Workers face high recruitment fees, low pay, unsafe work, cheating by manpower agencies, and many return as coffins.
4. **Dependence and Dutch-disease effect.** Remittance is mostly spent on consumption and imports, not on productive investment; this makes the economy depend on foreign labour markets and weakens exports.
5. **Vulnerability to global shocks** such as the pandemic or a downturn in the Gulf.

**Conclusion.** Foreign employment benefits Nepal in the short run, but the government should make it safe, upgrade skills, channel remittance to productive use and create jobs at home so that people have a real choice.
