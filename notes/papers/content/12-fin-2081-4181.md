### Group A

@s 1
Two roles of a financial manager: **raising funds** at the lowest cost from suitable sources, and **investing the funds** wisely in profitable projects and current assets. (Others: managing working capital, deciding dividends, controlling risk.)

@s 2
Two components of an annual report: the **financial statements** (statement of financial position, income statement, cash flow statement) and the **directors' report with the auditor's report**. A chairman's letter and notes to the accounts are also included.

@s 3
**Current ratio** is determined to measure the **short-term solvency** of the firm, that is, its ability to pay current liabilities out of current assets. A ratio of about 2 : 1 is considered satisfactory.

@s 4
Two features of preferred stock: it carries a **fixed rate of dividend** which is paid before any dividend on common stock, and it has a **preference over common stock in repayment** of capital if the company is liquidated. It normally has no voting right.

@s 5
Two factors affecting capital structure: the **cost of capital** (debt is cheaper because interest is tax deductible) and the **stability of sales and earnings**. Control, risk, flexibility and the size of the firm also matter.

@s 6
Under the **zero-growth model** the dividend stays constant for ever, so the stock is valued like a perpetuity: $P_0 = D / r_e$, where $D$ is the constant annual dividend and $r_e$ the required rate of return.

@s 7
The **gross concept** of working capital means the **total of all current assets** (cash, bank, debtors, bills receivable, inventory, prepaid expenses). It stresses how funds are managed in each current asset.

@s 8
A **stock dividend** is a dividend paid by issuing additional shares (bonus shares) to existing shareholders in proportion to their holding, instead of cash. Total equity does not change; retained earnings are capitalised.

@s 9
**Foreign currency** is important to a multinational corporation because it is needed to **pay for imports, foreign investment and foreign loans**, and to **settle trade with foreign buyers and suppliers**. Its value also affects the profit on overseas operations.

@s 10
Long-term debt = 30% of Rs. 7,00,000 = Rs. 2,10,000, so short-term debt = Rs. 7,00,000 − Rs. 2,10,000 = **Rs. 4,90,000** (70% of total debt).

@s 11
Dollars required = Rs. 3,00,000 ÷ Rs. 135 per dollar = **USD 2,222.22**.

### Group B

@s 12
**Business finance** is the management of funds (raising, investing and distributing them), while **accounting** records, classifies and reports the financial transactions of the firm. The two are closely linked because the finance manager works from accounting data.

- **Accounting supplies the data.** The balance sheet, income statement and cash flow statement give the figures that the manager uses to judge liquidity, profitability and leverage.
- **Ratio analysis and planning.** Financial ratios, budgets and forecasts are built from accounting information, so a decision on borrowing or expansion rests on it.
- **Decisions are checked by accounting.** After funds are raised and invested, accounting records the cost, the return and the cash movement, so control and performance review are possible.
- **Cost and profit information.** Costing and break-even data from accounting guide pricing, capital budgeting and dividend decisions.
- **Differences.** Accounting works on the accrual basis and looks at the past, whereas finance works on cash flow and looks at the future, and it is concerned with value maximisation.

Thus accounting is the information system and finance is the decision system; neither can work well without the other.

@s 13
The **statement of financial position** (balance sheet) shows the assets, liabilities and owners' equity of a firm on a given date. It is prepared from the **adjusted trial balance** after the income statement is completed.

**Steps:** (1) Take the closing balances of all real and personal accounts from the ledger. (2) Group assets into **non-current** (land, building, machinery) and **current** (cash, bank, debtors, inventory) and list them in order of liquidity or permanence. (3) Group liabilities into **current** and **long-term**. (4) Add share capital, reserves and the retained earnings (opening balance plus net profit less dividends) as equity. (5) Check that **total assets = total liabilities + equity**.

**Example (Rs.):**

| Equity and liabilities | Rs. | Assets | Rs. |
|---|---:|---|---:|
| Share capital | 4,00,000 | Land and building | 3,00,000 |
| Retained earnings | 80,000 | Machinery | 2,00,000 |
| Long-term loan | 1,00,000 | Inventory | 60,000 |
| Creditors | 50,000 | Debtors | 40,000 |
| | | Cash | 30,000 |
| **Total** | **6,30,000** | **Total** | **6,30,000** |

The two sides agree at Rs. 6,30,000, which confirms that the statement has been prepared correctly.

@s 14
Credit terms **3/12, net 25** mean a 3% cash discount if paid within 12 days, otherwise the full amount is due on day 25. If the buyer skips the discount, it pays 13 extra days of credit (25 − 12) at the cost of the discount. Using a 360-day year:

$$\text{APC} = \frac{d}{100-d}\times\frac{360}{N-D} = \frac{3}{97}\times\frac{360}{25-12}$$

$$\text{APC} = 0.030928\times 27.692 = 0.8565 = \mathbf{85.65\%}$$

The effective annual rate compounds the 13-day cost over the number of periods in the year:

$$\text{EAR} = \left(1+\frac{3}{97}\right)^{360/13}-1 = (1.030928)^{27.692}-1 = 1.3245$$

So the **annual percentage cost is 85.65%** and the **effective annual rate is about 132.45%**. Such a high cost shows that forgoing the discount is a very expensive source of funds, and the buyer should rather borrow from a bank if it cannot pay in 12 days.

@s 15
At break-even, total revenue equals total cost, so contribution equals fixed cost.

$$\text{BEP (units)} = \frac{\text{Fixed cost}}{P - V}$$

Given $P = 40$, $V = 25$ and BEP = 10,000 units.

$$\text{BEP in rupees} = 10{,}000\times 40 = \mathbf{Rs.\ 4{,}00{,}000}$$

$$\text{Contribution per unit} = 40-25 = \text{Rs. } 15$$

$$\text{Fixed cost} = 10{,}000\times 15 = \mathbf{Rs.\ 1{,}50{,}000}$$

Check: at 10,000 units, sales Rs. 4,00,000 − variable cost Rs. 2,50,000 − fixed cost Rs. 1,50,000 = 0, so the firm just breaks even. Every unit sold beyond 10,000 adds Rs. 15 to profit.

@s 16
**Evaluation of an investment (capital budgeting) proceeds through the following steps:**

1. **Identification of investment proposals.** Ideas for expansion, replacement, new products or cost saving are generated from all levels of the firm.
2. **Estimating the cash flows.** The initial outlay (net cash outflow, NCO) and the yearly cash flows after tax (CFAT) are forecast; depreciation is added back and only incremental cash flows are counted.
3. **Fixing the required rate of return.** The cost of capital, adjusted for the risk of the project, is chosen as the discount rate.
4. **Applying evaluation techniques.** Non-discounting methods (payback period, accounting rate of return) and discounting methods (NPV, IRR, profitability index) are applied.
5. **Decision making.** Accept the project if NPV is positive (IRR above the required rate); for mutually exclusive projects choose the one with the highest NPV, and ration capital if funds are limited.
6. **Implementation.** Funds are released and the project is carried out on schedule.
7. **Post-audit and review.** Actual cash flows are compared with the forecast to find errors and improve later decisions.

@s 17
The requirement lines are not printed in the available copy of the paper; with marks [3+2] the usual parts are used: the **inventory conversion period** and the **operating cycle / cash conversion cycle** (350-day year).

$$\text{Inventory conversion period} = \frac{350}{10} = \mathbf{35\ days}$$

$$\text{Operating cycle} = 35 + 28 = \mathbf{63\ days}$$

$$\text{Cash conversion cycle} = 63 - 21 = \mathbf{42\ days}$$

Interpretation: cash remains tied up in the business for 42 days between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Shortening the inventory or receivable period, or lengthening the payable period, would reduce the funds needed for working capital.

@s 18
**Dividend payment schemes** (forms in which dividend is paid):

- **Cash dividend:** the most common form. Cash is paid per share. It needs enough cash in hand, and it reduces cash and retained earnings.
- **Stock (bonus share) dividend:** extra shares are issued free to shareholders in proportion to their holdings. Cash is saved, and retained earnings are converted into share capital.
- **Property dividend:** assets other than cash, such as goods or investments, are given to shareholders.
- **Scrip (note) dividend:** the firm issues promissory notes to be paid at a later date when it lacks cash today.
- **Liquidating dividend:** return of capital to the shareholders when the business is wound up.
- **Stock split:** each share is divided into more shares of lower par value to improve marketability; it is not a dividend but has a similar effect.

**Procedure:** the board declares the dividend on the *declaration date*, shareholders on the *record date* are entitled to it, the shares trade *ex-dividend* a few days before it, and payment is made on the *payment date*.

@s 19
**Domestic financial management** means the management of funds of a firm that operates within the home country and deals in the home currency. Its role in the development of the country is as follows.

- **Mobilising savings.** By issuing shares, debentures and accepting deposits, firms collect scattered savings and turn them into productive capital.
- **Efficient use of resources.** Sound investment decisions send funds to the most profitable projects, so scarce capital is not wasted.
- **Employment and income.** Growing firms create jobs and income and raise the standard of living.
- **Government revenue.** Profitable firms pay tax, customs and fees that finance public services.
- **Capital market growth.** Regular dividends and disclosure build investors' confidence and deepen the stock market.
- **Industrialisation and stability.** Proper working capital and risk management keep firms solvent, which supports production, exports and price stability.

**Or (Q19). Cross rate.** The question gives CAD 1.4661 per Euro (1 EUR = 1.4661 CAD) and CAD 0.0117 per yen (1 JPY = 0.0117 CAD). The **cross rate** is found by dividing one rate by the other so that the common currency (CAD) cancels:

$$\text{EUR/JPY} = \frac{\text{CAD per EUR}}{\text{CAD per JPY}} = \frac{1.4661}{0.0117} = 125.31$$

Therefore **1 Euro = about 125.31 Japanese Yen**, and the inverse is **1 JPY = 0.00798 EUR**. Because both currencies are quoted against the Canadian dollar, no direct EUR/JPY quote is needed.

@s 20
**Working figures.** Current assets Rs. 4,50,000, inventory Rs. 80,000, cash Rs. 20,000. Current liabilities = creditors Rs. 1,00,000 (the bank loan is treated as a long-term liability). Total debt = bank loan + creditors = Rs. 3,00,000. Equity = share capital + retained earnings = Rs. 7,50,000.

**a) Liquid ratio** $= \dfrac{\text{Current assets} - \text{Inventory}}{\text{Current liabilities}}$

$$= \frac{4{,}50{,}000-80{,}000}{1{,}00{,}000} = \mathbf{3.7:1}$$

**b) Debt-equity ratio** $= \dfrac{\text{Total debt}}{\text{Equity}}$

$$= \frac{2{,}00{,}000+1{,}00{,}000}{5{,}00{,}000+2{,}50{,}000} = \frac{3{,}00{,}000}{7{,}50{,}000} = \mathbf{0.4:1}$$

(If only the long-term bank loan is taken as debt, the ratio is 2,00,000 / 7,50,000 = 0.27.)

**c) Inventory turnover ratio** $= \dfrac{\text{Sales}}{\text{Inventory}}$

$$= \frac{18{,}00{,}000}{80{,}000} = \mathbf{22.5\ times}$$

(On a cost basis, cost of goods sold Rs. 10,80,000 gives 13.5 times; the sales basis is used because cost of goods sold is asked only in part h.)

**d) Fixed assets turnover** $= \dfrac{18{,}00{,}000}{6{,}00{,}000} = \mathbf{3\ times}$

**e) Return on total assets** $= \dfrac{\text{Net profit}}{\text{Total assets}} = \dfrac{1{,}20{,}000}{10{,}50{,}000} = 0.1143 = \mathbf{11.43\%}$

**f) Return on equity** $= \dfrac{1{,}20{,}000}{7{,}50{,}000} = 0.16 = \mathbf{16\%}$

**g) Net profit ratio** $= \dfrac{1{,}20{,}000}{18{,}00{,}000} = 0.0667 = \mathbf{6.67\%}$

**h) Gross profit** $= 40\% \times 18{,}00{,}000 = \mathbf{Rs.\ 7{,}20{,}000}$ (cost of goods sold = Rs. 10,80,000).

**Comment.** The liquid ratio of 3.7 shows very strong short-term solvency, the firm uses little debt (0.4), and the return of 16% on equity is satisfactory, but a net margin of only 6.67% suggests that operating expenses are high.

@s 21
**Meaning.** A **preference share** is a share that gives its holder a **preferential claim** over ordinary shareholders on dividend and on repayment of capital at liquidation. It is a hybrid security, having some features of debt (fixed dividend) and some of equity (ownership).

**Features of preference shares:**

1. **Fixed dividend.** A fixed rate of dividend, such as 10% of par value, is paid, whatever the profit.
2. **Preference in dividend.** The dividend is paid to preference holders first and only the balance goes to ordinary shareholders.
3. **Preference in liquidation.** When the company is wound up, their capital is refunded before ordinary shareholders, though after creditors.
4. **No voting rights.** Normally they cannot vote, except when dividend has remained unpaid for a long period or their rights are affected.
5. **Cumulative or non-cumulative.** Cumulative shares carry forward unpaid dividend to later years; non-cumulative shares lose it.
6. **Participating or non-participating.** Participating shares share in extra profit with ordinary shares after a set dividend.
7. **Redeemable or irredeemable and convertible.** The company may buy them back after a period, or the holder may convert them into ordinary shares.
8. **Dividend is not tax deductible,** unlike interest, so preference capital is costlier than debt, but failure to pay it does not push the company into bankruptcy.

They are useful for firms that want funds without diluting control or taking on the compulsory burden of interest.

**Or (Q21). a) Zero coupon bond.** No interest is paid, so the value is the present value of the par value to be received after 10 years at 12%:

$$V_0 = \frac{1{,}000}{(1.12)^{10}} = \frac{1{,}000}{3.1058} = \mathbf{Rs.\ 321.97}$$

**b) Constant-growth (Gordon) model.** $D_0 = 25$, $r = 15\%$, $g = 5\%$:

$$D_1 = D_0(1+g) = 25\times 1.05 = 26.25$$

$$P_0 = \frac{D_1}{r-g} = \frac{26.25}{0.15-0.05} = \mathbf{Rs.\ 262.50}$$

@s 22
**i) NPV at 12%** = present value of CFAT − net cash outlay. Present value factors at 12% (table values): 0.8929, 0.7972, 0.7118, 0.6355, 0.5674.

**Project M (uneven cash flows):**

| Year | CFAT (Rs.) | PV factor | Present value (Rs.) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 80,000 | 0.8929 | 71,432 |
| 2 | 1,20,000 | 0.7972 | 95,664 |
| 3 | 1,40,000 | 0.7118 | 99,652 |
| 4 | 1,60,000 | 0.6355 | 1,01,680 |
| 5 | 2,00,000 | 0.5674 | 1,13,480 |
| | | **Total PV** | **4,81,908** |

$$\text{NPV}_M = 4{,}81{,}908 - 5{,}00{,}000 = \mathbf{-Rs.\ 18{,}092}$$

**Project N (annuity of Rs. 1,40,000 for 5 years):** the annuity factor at 12% is $0.8929+0.7972+0.7118+0.6355+0.5674 = 3.6048$.

$$\text{PV} = 1{,}40{,}000\times 3.6048 = 5{,}04{,}672$$

$$\text{NPV}_N = 5{,}04{,}672 - 5{,}00{,}000 = \mathbf{+Rs.\ 4{,}672}$$

**ii) Decision.** Project M has a **negative NPV**, so it earns less than the 12% required return and would reduce shareholders' wealth; it should be rejected. Project N has a **positive NPV**, so it earns slightly more than 12% and adds value. **Project N is more profitable**, even though both projects cost the same and M returns more in total (Rs. 7,00,000 for each). N brings its cash earlier and evenly, and money received earlier is worth more because of the time value of money.
