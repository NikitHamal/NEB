**1. D.** Embryogenesis is the series of divisions and differentiation by which the zygote becomes the embryo (radicle, plumule and cotyledons) inside the developing seed. Fertilization forms the zygote, pollination is the transfer of pollen, and gametogenesis is the formation of gametes.

**2. A.** Thigmotropism (haptotropism) is the growth movement of a plant part in response to touch or contact, as in the tendrils of a pea or cucumber coiling round a support. Phototropism is response to light, geotropism to gravity and hydrotropism to water.

**3. C.** Crossing over is the exchange of corresponding segments between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes at the chiasmata in pachytene of prophase I of meiosis. It produces recombinants.

**4. B.** Sapwood (alburnum) is the outer, light-coloured part of the secondary xylem, which is still living and conducts water; with the cambium and phloem it forms the living part of the stem. Heartwood is dead, dark and non-conducting; the pith is the central parenchyma; the cortex lies outside the vascular tissue.

**5. B.** The light reaction (in the grana) includes photolysis of water, photophosphorylation (ATP formation) and reduction of NADP⁺ to NADPH. Reduction of CO₂ to carbohydrate happens in the dark (light-independent) reaction, the Calvin cycle in the stroma.

**12. B.** Tendons are made mainly of white collagen fibres, packed in parallel rows (dense regular connective tissue), which give high tensile strength. Yellow elastic fibres are abundant in ligaments and the walls of arteries.

**13. C.** A mesolecithal egg has a moderate amount of yolk, as in the frog; the yolk is concentrated in the vegetal pole. Large amounts are found in macrolecithal (centrolecithal or telolecithal) eggs like those of birds, and almost none in alecithal eggs like those of mammals.

**14. B.** The piamater is the innermost, thin and highly vascular layer of the meninges that adheres closely to the surface of the brain and spinal cord (the duramater is the outer, tough layer and the arachnoid is the middle layer). The cranium is the bony skull, not a meninx.

**15. B.** Candidiasis, caused by the yeast-like fungus *Candida albicans*, can spread by kissing (oral thrush) and by sexual contact (genital candidiasis). Tuberculosis and influenza spread by droplets and cholera by contaminated water and food.

**16. C.** Light passes through the transparent cornea, then the pupil (the opening in the iris), then the lens, and the image is focused on the retina. The iris is not a pathway: it is the diaphragm that makes the pupil. So Cornea → Pupil → Lens → Retina.

**17. A.** Insulin lowers blood glucose by promoting glucose uptake and its conversion to glycogen. Without enough insulin, glucose stays in the blood and the blood sugar level rises: hyperglycemia (diabetes mellitus).

### Section I: Botany — Group B

@s 6
**Simple permanent tissue** is a permanent tissue (cells that have lost the ability to divide) made up of only one type of cells, all similar in structure and function. Examples: parenchyma, collenchyma and sclerenchyma.

**Collenchyma** is a living simple permanent tissue with unevenly thickened primary cell walls.
*Structure:*
1. The cells are living, elongated, and have a protoplast with a nucleus and chloroplasts in some cells (so they can photosynthesise).
2. The primary wall is thickened with cellulose, hemicellulose and pectin at the corners (angular collenchyma), along the tangential walls (lamellar collenchyma) or around the intercellular spaces (lacunar collenchyma). The thickening is deposited unevenly and there is no lignin.
3. They are found in the hypodermis of dicot stems, in petioles and below the epidermis of leaf midribs; they are absent in roots and in monocots.
4. Intercellular spaces are small or absent in angular type.
*Functions:*
- It gives *mechanical support* and tensile strength to young growing organs while remaining flexible (it can stretch as the organ grows).
- It provides *elasticity* so that stems and leaves can bend in the wind without breaking.
- The chloroplast-containing cells carry out *photosynthesis* and store food.

@s 7
**(i)** Labels (read from the inside outwards, since A is nearest the microspore mother cells and D at the outer edge of the wall of the microsporangium):
**A = tapetum**, **B = middle layer(s)**, **C = endothecium**, **D = epidermis**.

**(ii) Formation of pollen grains.** The microsporangium of a young anther is filled with a mass of diploid (2n) *sporogenous tissue*. Its cells become *microspore mother cells* (pollen mother cells, PMCs).
1. Each diploid microspore mother cell divides by *meiosis* (reduction division): meiosis I gives two haploid cells and meiosis II gives four haploid cells, a **microspore tetrad**.
2. The four haploid microspores separate from each other, as the callose around the tetrad is dissolved by enzymes from the tapetum.
3. Each microspore develops a wall: an outer, tough, sporopollenin *exine* with germ pores and an inner, thin pectocellulosic *intine*. The microspore becomes a *pollen grain*.
4. The nucleus of the pollen grain divides by mitosis into a large *vegetative cell* and a small *generative cell*; the generative cell later divides into two male gametes. Thus one microspore mother cell produces four pollen grains, and the process is called *microsporogenesis*.

**(iii)** The **tapetum** (the innermost wall layer, A) nourishes the developing microspores and pollen grains; it supplies sporopollenin, enzymes and other nutrients.

@s 8
**Incomplete linkage.** Linkage is the tendency of genes located on the same chromosome to be inherited together. *Complete linkage* means the linked genes never separate (rare, seen in male *Drosophila*). **Incomplete linkage** is the condition in which the linked genes are sometimes separated by *crossing over* between non-sister chromatids of homologous chromosomes during prophase I of meiosis, so that besides the parental combinations a few new (recombinant) combinations also appear. The nearer two genes are, the less often they cross over; the farther apart, the more often.

*Example: Morgan's experiment on* Drosophila *(body colour and wing length).* Grey body (b⁺) and long wings (vg⁺) are dominant over black body (b) and vestigial wings (vg).
- Cross: grey, long (b⁺b⁺ vg⁺vg⁺) × black, vestigial (bb vgvg) → F₁ all grey, long (b⁺b vg⁺vg).
- Test cross: F₁ female × double recessive male (bb vgvg). Result:

| Phenotype | Type | Percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Grey, long | parental | 41.5 |
| Black, vestigial | parental | 41.5 |
| Grey, vestigial | recombinant | 8.5 |
| Black, long | recombinant | 8.5 |

The parental types are 83% (more than the 50% expected for independent assortment) and the recombinants 17%. Since the F₁ female produced 4 types of gametes unequally, the genes are linked, but the linkage is *incomplete* because of crossing over; the crossover value (recombination frequency) is 17%.

**Significance:** (i) the *recombination frequency* is used to prepare *genetic (linkage) maps*: 1% recombination = 1 map unit (1 centimorgan), so the position and the order of genes on a chromosome can be worked out; (ii) it produces new combinations of characters, so variation arises for natural selection and evolution; (iii) breeders can predict how often useful characters will be inherited together, and can combine desirable characters of two varieties.

**Or. Practical applications of vernalization.** Vernalization is the promotion of flowering by exposing germinating seeds or seedlings to a low temperature (0 to 5 °C) for some days.
1. **Spring-season sowing of winter varieties.** Seeds of winter wheat, barley and rye, which normally flower only after a winter, are chilled in moist condition and sown in spring, so a winter variety can be grown as a spring crop where winters are too severe or the seeds were missed in autumn.
2. **Earlier flowering and maturity.** Vernalized seeds shorten the vegetative period; the crop is harvested early, which can allow a second crop in the same field and avoids late-season stress from drought, frost or disease.
3. **Higher yield and better resistance.** Vernalization increases the yield of wheat, barley, millet and rice, and treated plants are more resistant to cold and some fungal diseases.
4. **Plant breeding and seed production.** Biennial plants (cabbage, carrot, beet, onion) normally flower in the second year; vernalization lets them flower in the first year, so seed can be produced quickly and several generations can be raised per year in breeding programmes.
5. **Horticulture.** Cold treatment of bulbs and seedlings (chrysanthemum, lily, tulip, hyacinth) induces uniform flowering at the desired time for the flower market, and crops can be grown in new regions or seasons.

@s 9
**Benefits of genetic engineering in medicine** (2 marks)
- *Recombinant therapeutic proteins:* human insulin (using *E. coli*), growth hormone, clotting factor VIII, interferon and erythropoietin are made in bacteria or yeast in unlimited, pure and safe amounts, replacing animal-derived products.
- *Vaccines:* recombinant vaccines such as the hepatitis-B vaccine (surface antigen HBsAg produced in yeast) are safe, since no infectious virus is handled.
- *Gene therapy and genome editing:* a defective gene can be replaced or corrected (ADA-deficiency SCID, haemophilia, sickle-cell disease by CRISPR-Cas9).
- *Diagnosis:* PCR, DNA probes and DNA fingerprinting detect genetic diseases and infections early, and monoclonal antibodies help in cancer diagnosis and therapy.

**Risks, and ethical and social concerns** (2 marks)
- *Biological risks:* off-target mutations, insertion of the gene at a wrong site (may switch on cancer genes), immune reactions to vectors, and the chance that a modified organism or vector escapes into the environment.
- *Ethical concerns:* changes to germ-line cells are *heritable*, so future generations that cannot consent are affected; "designer babies" (selection of intelligence, appearance) and a revival of *eugenics*; interference with nature; the status of human embryos used in the research.
- *Social concerns:* high cost, so rich people benefit and the gap with the poor widens; *genetic privacy and discrimination* by insurers or employers; patents and ownership of genes and cell lines; misuse in bio-weapons; the need for strict laws, ethics committees and informed consent.
Balanced use under regulation allows the cures while protecting human dignity and safety.

**Differences between aerobic and anaerobic respiration (3 marks).**

| Basis | Aerobic respiration | Anaerobic respiration |
|---|---|---|
| Oxygen | Requires free oxygen | Occurs in the absence of free oxygen |
| Site | Glycolysis in cytoplasm; Krebs cycle in mitochondrial matrix; ETC on the inner mitochondrial membrane | Entirely in the cytoplasm |
| Oxidation of glucose | Complete, to CO₂ and H₂O | Incomplete, to ethanol and CO₂ (or lactic acid) |
| Final electron acceptor | Oxygen | An organic compound such as pyruvate or acetaldehyde |
| Energy released | Large: 30–32 ATP (38 in older texts) per glucose, about 2870 kJ/mol | Small: only 2 ATP per glucose, about 210 kJ/mol |
| Products | CO₂ + H₂O + energy | Ethanol + CO₂ (yeast, fermentation) or lactic acid (muscle), + a little energy |
| Occurs in | Most plants and animals | Yeast, some bacteria, roots in waterlogged soil, muscles in heavy exercise |
| Equation | C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + 38 ATP | C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ + 2 ATP |

**Krebs cycle (citric acid cycle, TCA cycle) (5 marks).** It was described by Hans Krebs (1937). It occurs in the mitochondrial matrix and breaks down the two-carbon acetyl CoA into CO₂, with release of hydrogen carried by NADH and FADH₂.

*Link reaction:* pyruvate (from glycolysis) enters the mitochondrion and is oxidatively decarboxylated: pyruvate + CoA + NAD⁺ → acetyl CoA + CO₂ + NADH + H⁺.

*Steps of the cycle:*
1. **Condensation:** acetyl CoA (2C) joins oxaloacetate (4C) to give **citrate** (6C); enzyme citrate synthase; CoA is released.
2. **Isomerisation:** citrate is converted to **isocitrate** (6C) through cis-aconitate by aconitase.
3. **First oxidative decarboxylation:** isocitrate → **α-ketoglutarate** (5C) + CO₂, with NAD⁺ → NADH (isocitrate dehydrogenase).
4. **Second oxidative decarboxylation:** α-ketoglutarate → **succinyl CoA** (4C) + CO₂ + NADH (α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase).
5. **Substrate-level phosphorylation:** succinyl CoA → **succinate** + CoA, with GDP + Pi → GTP (≈ ATP) (succinyl-CoA synthetase).
6. **Dehydrogenation:** succinate → **fumarate**, FAD → FADH₂ (succinate dehydrogenase, in the inner mitochondrial membrane).
7. **Hydration:** fumarate + H₂O → **malate** (fumarase).
8. **Dehydrogenation:** malate → **oxaloacetate**, NAD⁺ → NADH (malate dehydrogenase). Oxaloacetate is regenerated and the cycle continues.

@s 11
**Allopolyploidy.** *Polyploidy* is the possession of more than two complete sets of chromosomes. **Allopolyploidy** (allopolyploid or amphidiploid) is polyploidy that arises from hybridization between two *different species* (genomes) followed by doubling of the chromosome number of the hybrid. The hybrid between two species is usually sterile because the chromosomes of the two parents are not homologous and cannot pair at meiosis; doubling of the chromosomes (by colchicine treatment or spontaneously) gives each chromosome a partner, and the amphidiploid is fertile.

*Example 1: Raphanobrassica (Karpechenko, 1927).*
- Radish *Raphanus sativus* (2n = 18, RR) × cabbage *Brassica oleracea* (2n = 18, BB)
- gametes R (9) + B (9) → F₁ hybrid RB (2n = 18): sterile, because the 9 R and the 9 B chromosomes do not pair
- chromosome doubling (colchicine or non-reduction of gametes) → **RRBB, 2n = 36**, fertile *Raphanobrassica*.
Karpechenko wished to combine the root of radish with the leaf of cabbage but got the leaf of radish and the root of cabbage, so it had no use; the experiment proved that a new species can be made.

*Example 2:* **Triticale** (×*Triticosecale*), a man-made cereal from durum wheat (*Triticum turgidum*, AABB, 2n = 28) × rye (*Secale cereale*, RR, 2n = 14); the F₁ (ABR, 21) is doubled to AABBRR (2n = 42). The bread wheat itself (*T. aestivum*, AABBDD, 2n = 42) is a natural allohexaploid of *T. turgidum* × *Aegilops tauschii* (DD).

**Benefits of polyploidy in agriculture and plant breeding.**
1. *Gigantism:* the larger cells make bigger organs, e.g. larger fruits, seeds, leaves, flowers (tetraploid grapes, apples, tomatoes, marigold).
2. *Hybrid vigour (heterosis) is fixed:* an allopolyploid is permanently heterozygous and keeps the vigour of the F₁.
3. *New crop species* combining characters of two parents: triticale combines the high yield of wheat with the hardiness and disease resistance of rye.
4. *Fertility is restored* in sterile species hybrids, and the crop can be propagated.
5. *Seedless fruits:* triploid (3n) watermelon, banana, grape (from 2n × 4n crosses).
6. Better disease, cold and stress tolerance, and richer protein or vitamin content in some polyploids; ornamental plants have bigger and longer-lasting flowers.

**Drawbacks.**
1. *Sterility:* odd-numbered polyploids (3n, 5n) cannot form balanced gametes and are sterile, so they must be propagated vegetatively; even auto-polyploids may have irregular meiosis and lower fertility.
2. Many polyploids grow *more slowly* and need more nutrients and water; seed set and germination may be low.
3. *Instability:* chromosome loss and unwanted traits may arise in later generations.
4. Chemicals such as colchicine are toxic and expensive; success depends on the species (not all respond).
5. Bigger organs may have lower sugar or a poor taste/quality; and polyploid breeding is not directly useful for animals (it is lethal in most animals).

**Or. Semi-conservative replication of DNA.**
*Definition:* in semi-conservative replication, each of the two daughter DNA molecules has one **old (parental) strand** and one **newly synthesised strand**. It was proposed by Watson and Crick (1953) and *proved by Meselson and Stahl* (1958) in *E. coli* grown in ¹⁵N medium and then transferred to ¹⁴N medium: after one generation all DNA was of intermediate density (¹⁵N–¹⁴N hybrid) and after two generations half was intermediate and half light (¹⁴N–¹⁴N), which fits only the semi-conservative model (a conservative model would give heavy and light bands, and a dispersive model a single band of falling density).

*Mechanism (in the S-phase; 5′→3′ synthesis, with enzymes):*
1. **Initiation:** replication starts at the *origin of replication* (ori), where initiator proteins bind. **Helicase** breaks the hydrogen bonds between the bases and unwinds the helix, making a Y-shaped *replication fork*; **single-strand binding proteins (SSBs)** hold the strands apart; **topoisomerase (gyrase)** removes the supercoiling ahead of the fork by cutting and rejoining.
2. **Priming:** DNA polymerase cannot start a chain; **primase** makes a short RNA primer (about 10 nucleotides) with a free 3′-OH on the template.
3. **Elongation:** **DNA polymerase III** adds deoxyribonucleotides, complementary to the template (A–T, G–C), to the 3′ end of the primer, always in the 5′→3′ direction. The *leading strand* is made continuously towards the fork; the *lagging strand* is made discontinuously as **Okazaki fragments** (1000–2000 bases in prokaryotes), each requiring its own primer.
4. **Primer removal and sealing:** **DNA polymerase I** removes the RNA primers (5′→3′ exonuclease) and fills the gap with DNA; **DNA ligase** joins the nicks by phosphodiester bonds, giving a continuous strand.
5. **Termination:** replication ends when forks meet (at *ter* sites in bacteria), and the two daughter molecules separate (in eukaryotes there are many origins and replicons, and replication is bidirectional).

**Significance in accurate transmission of genetic information.**
- The old strand is a *template*, so the base sequence of the new strand is fixed by base-pairing (A–T, G–C) and each daughter DNA is an exact copy of the parental DNA: the genetic message is conserved from cell to cell and generation to generation.
- *Proofreading* by DNA polymerase III (3′→5′ exonuclease) and the mismatch-repair system reduce the error rate to about one in 10⁹–10¹⁰ bases.
- Each daughter cell in mitosis gets the same genome as the parent, which maintains the identity of tissues and organisms; in the formation of gametes it passes the information to the offspring.
- Rare errors that escape are *mutations*, which are the raw material of variation and evolution.

**Organogenesis** is the process by which the three primary germ layers (ectoderm, mesoderm, endoderm) formed in gastrulation differentiate into the organs and organ systems of the body (e.g. nervous system from ectoderm, notochord, muscles and heart from mesoderm, gut lining from endoderm).

**J-shaped growth curve (exponential growth).** When a species is introduced into a new habitat with plenty of food, space and no predators or disease, its population grows *exponentially*: the growth rate depends only on the number of individuals present and keeps on increasing until a resource suddenly runs out or the environment changes. The curve is shaped like the letter J.

*Model:* the rate of change of population size N with time t is
$$\frac{dN}{dt} = rN \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad N_t = N_0\, e^{rt}$$
where N₀ is the starting number, r the intrinsic rate of natural increase (birth rate − death rate) and e = 2.718.

*Worked example.* Suppose 10 rabbits are released in a new habitat and r = 0.5 per year:

| Year (t) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| N = 10e^(0.5t) | 10 | 16 | 27 | 45 | 74 | 122 | 201 |

When the resources become scarce (carrying capacity K), either the numbers crash (a "boom and bust") or growth changes into the S-shaped (logistic) curve.

**Two activities to control population growth** (any two):
1. *Family planning and awareness:* making available contraceptives (condoms, pills, injections, implants, IUD), counselling and sterilisation services, and promoting the small family norm.
2. *Education and women's empowerment, and a higher age at marriage:* a higher level of female education, legal marriage age (20 years in Nepal), and creating employment lower the birth rate.

**Internal structure of the human heart (4 marks).**
The heart is a hollow, muscular, conical organ about the size of a fist (about 300 g) in the thoracic cavity between the lungs, tilted to the left. It is enclosed in a double-layered sac, the *pericardium*, which has pericardial fluid between the layers. The wall has three layers: the outer *epicardium*, the thick middle *myocardium* (cardiac muscle) and the inner *endocardium* (endothelium).
Internally the heart is divided into **four chambers** by septa:
1. **Right atrium** receives deoxygenated blood from the body by the *superior vena cava* (head, arms), *inferior vena cava* (trunk, legs) and *coronary sinus* (the heart wall).
2. **Right ventricle** receives blood from the right atrium through the *tricuspid valve* (three flaps) and pumps it through the *pulmonary trunk* to the lungs; it has a *pulmonary semilunar valve*.
3. **Left atrium** receives oxygenated blood from the lungs by *four pulmonary veins*.
4. **Left ventricle** has the thickest wall (to pump blood to the whole body); it receives blood through the *bicuspid (mitral) valve* (two flaps) and pumps it to the *aorta*, which has the *aortic semilunar valve*.
- The *interatrial septum* (with the *fossa ovalis*, a remnant of the foetal foramen ovale) and the *interventricular septum* separate right and left sides, so oxygenated and deoxygenated blood do not mix.
- The *atrioventricular valves* are held by thin *chordae tendineae* to the *papillary muscles* of the ventricular wall, which prevent them from turning inside out. Valves ensure the one-way flow of blood.

**Path of blood (3 marks).** The right atrium (deoxygenated blood from the body, by the vena cavae) → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary semilunar valve → pulmonary artery → lungs, where the blood takes up O₂ and gives off CO₂ (pulmonary circulation) → four pulmonary veins → left atrium → bicuspid valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → arteries → body tissues (systemic circulation) → vena cavae → right atrium. A double circulation is thus completed: the heart beats about 72 times a minute, each beat (cardiac cycle of 0.8 s) having atrial systole (0.1 s), ventricular systole (0.3 s) and joint diastole (0.4 s); the "lubb" sound is closure of the AV valves and "dupp" that of the semilunar valves. The beat is started by the *sinoatrial (SA) node*, the pacemaker, and carried by the AV node, bundle of His and Purkinje fibres.

**If the blood supply to the heart is suddenly interrupted (1 mark).** The heart muscle is supplied by the *coronary arteries*. If the blood supply is suddenly cut (a clot or a block in a coronary artery), the myocardial cells do not get oxygen and glucose, and the part of the muscle dies (**myocardial infarction, a heart attack**). The pumping becomes irregular or stops (arrhythmia, ventricular fibrillation, *cardiac arrest*), blood is not sent to the brain and other organs, and the person may die within minutes unless CPR, clot-dissolving drugs, angioplasty or bypass surgery are given.

**Or. Respiratory system of man.** The respiratory system brings air to the lungs for the exchange of gases. Air passes through a series of cavities and tubes:
1. **Nose and nasal cavity (conducting):** two nostrils open into the nasal cavity, divided by the nasal septum and lined by ciliated mucous epithelium with hairs; air is filtered, warmed and moistened, and the olfactory area detects smell. (Mouth can serve as an alternative.)
2. **Pharynx:** the common passage for air and food, behind the nose and mouth; air passes through the nasopharynx to the *glottis*.
3. **Larynx (voice box):** at the top of the trachea, made of cartilages (thyroid, cricoid, arytenoids) with the *vocal cords*; it is closed by the *epiglottis* during swallowing so that food does not enter the trachea.
4. **Trachea (windpipe):** a tube about 10–12 cm long, kept open by 16–20 C-shaped cartilage rings, lined with ciliated epithelium and goblet cells; mucus traps dust and the cilia sweep it up.
5. **Bronchi and bronchioles:** the trachea divides into right and left primary bronchi, which enter the lungs and divide into secondary and tertiary bronchi and then into many fine *bronchioles* (without cartilage, with smooth muscle).
6. **Alveoli (respiratory part):** each terminal bronchiole ends in alveolar ducts and clusters of tiny, thin-walled, air-filled sacs, the **alveoli** (about 300 million in the two lungs, with a surface area of about 70 m²), each surrounded by a network of capillaries; O₂ diffuses into the blood and CO₂ into the alveolus across the thin respiratory membrane. Alveolar cells make *surfactant*, which prevents their collapse.
7. **Lungs and pleura:** the right lung has three lobes and the left two (with a cardiac notch). Each lung is covered by a double *pleural membrane* with pleural fluid, which reduces friction. The lungs lie in the thoracic cavity, which is enclosed by ribs, the intercostal muscles and the *diaphragm*; breathing movements of the diaphragm and ribs inflate and deflate the lungs.

**Asthma attack (1 mark).** In an asthma attack (usually an allergic reaction, e.g. to dust, pollen, cold air or smoke), the smooth muscles of the bronchi and bronchioles *contract (bronchospasm)*, the lining becomes inflamed and swollen and extra mucus is secreted. The respiratory tubes therefore become *narrow*, air cannot pass out easily, and the patient has wheezing, shortness of breath, coughing and tightness of the chest. It is relieved by bronchodilators (salbutamol inhalers) and steroids.
