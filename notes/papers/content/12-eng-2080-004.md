@s 1
**(a)** The writer was in prison on Robben Island when he began writing the book. He started it in 1974, during his imprisonment there, and he had to write in secret because a prisoner was not allowed to write a book. The place was a prison island, so every page was written under guard, with the fear of being caught, and the work was possible only because he trusted a few fellow prisoners. He resumed work on the manuscript only after he was released from prison in 1990.

**(b)** His friends helped him in four ways. First, his old comrades Walter Sisulu and Ahmed Kathrada worked tirelessly to revive his memories of past events, and without them it is doubtful that the manuscript would ever have been completed. Second, his co-prisoners Mac Maharaj and Isu Chiba, using their unique calligraphic skills, made sure that the original manuscript safely reached its destination after the copy kept with him was seized. Third, after his release, dedicated colleagues, friends and professionals assisted him in finishing the work. Fourth, Kathrada spent long hours revising, correcting and giving accuracy to the story.

**(c)** The writer had to write secretly because he was a prisoner and the prison authorities would not have allowed him to write the book. If they found the pages, they would take them away, and the passage shows that this really happened: the copy he kept with him was discovered and confiscated. Writing in secret, and arranging for the original to be carried out safely by his co-prisoners, was the only way of saving the story of his life and struggle from being destroyed.

**(d)** After his release in 1990 the writer's schedule was crowded with numerous duties and responsibilities, which left him little free time for writing. He was no longer a prisoner with long empty hours; he was a public figure with many responsibilities, meetings and engagements that took up the whole day. For this reason he needed the help of colleagues, friends and professionals to finish the book, and he was able to complete it only after a long delay.

**(e)** The passage tells us that writing a book is a long, slow and difficult process, and that it is rarely the work of one person alone. A writer needs memory, and friends can revive it; he needs patience, since the book was begun in 1974 and finished many years later; he may meet obstacles such as confiscation of a manuscript; and he needs others to revise, correct and make the story accurate. Writing is therefore a team effort that needs time, patience, trust and sacrifice, and it can be resumed after interruption.

@s 2
**(a)** The poem is *Grand Mother* by the Mesquaki (American-Indian) poet Ray Young Bear. 'A voice coming from the rock' is a symbol: the rock stands for the land, the tradition and the roots of the tribe, and the voice is the grandmother, who represents the native identity of the Mesquaki people. Even if a voice came from a rock, the poet would know it to be hers. This shows that his bond with the homeland is so strong that its stones speak to him in her voice, and that the suppressed voice of the tribe still guides him like a light in the night.

**(b)** In Chekhov's story *About Love*, Alyohin is a university graduate, but he lives the hard, busy life of a farmer on a mortgaged estate and is always short of money. The Luganoviches, and at the end his listeners Burkin and Ivan, say that he is 'rushing around like a squirrel in a cage'. The proverb is significant because a squirrel in a cage is restless and active but goes nowhere. It describes Alyohin's wasted talent and his confinement by duty and reasoning. It also stands for his unfulfilled love for Anna, which he never openly expressed.

**(c)** In the essay by Lilla and Barry, the writers walk through a Sal forest in western Nepal and see bare, skeletal looking trees with hardly a leaf left, and hear people chopping branches from all directions to feed their cattle. These trees signify the ruthless exploitation of nature by people who need fodder. Yes, it does indicate the degrading condition of the environment: the villagers' reply that the animals must eat today shows that they think only of present needs, not of deforestation, soil erosion and the future. Poverty and lack of education make them harm the forest on which they depend.

**(d)** In Dylan Thomas's *A Story*, the charabanc stops outside the pub called the Mountain Sheep, and its landlord welcomes the members of the outing with great show. He is a long, lean man with a greased love-curl and 'pouncing eyes', and he greets them simpering, like a wolf welcoming a flock of sheep. This welcome is not friendly but cunning and greedy. He expects to make money from men who are going to drink heavily, and the party does drink the bar dry while the boy is left outside to guard the bus.

**(e)** In W. B. Yeats's play *Purgatory*, the Old Man kills his sixteen-year-old son with the same knife with which he once killed his drunken father. His intention is to end the family's line of sin and pollution that began with his mother, a lady of a great house who married beneath her, a drunken groom who wasted her estate. He believes the boy will continue the pollution and that his death will free his mother's soul from her repeated suffering in purgatory. He has no real achievement: at the end the sound of hoofbeats returns, which shows that the mother's ghost is still bound to relive her sin. The Old Man's murder is useless, and he can only pray to God to release her soul.

**(f)** In James Joyce's *The Boarding House*, Mrs. Mooney is a butcher's daughter who married her father's foreman. After her father's death Mr. Mooney drank, stole from the till, ran into debt and finally went for her with a cleaver. To get relief from this violent, drunken husband she went to the priest and obtained a formal decree of separation, keeping the children. This was an unpleasant and humiliating way in a Catholic city, because it exposed the family to gossip and left her with no shop and only a boarding house to run. She chose it because there was no other way to protect herself and her children.

@s 3
**(a)** *Women's Business* is an essay by Ilene Kantrov about American women entrepreneurs, from Lydia E. Pinkham in the nineteenth century to Margaret Rudkin, Jennie Grossinger, Elizabeth Arden, Helena Rubinstein and Annie Turnbo-Malone.

**How they differed from their male counterparts.** First, they used their image as women: they presented themselves as mothers, grandmothers, glamorous socialites or healers, and their gender became part of the brand, so customers trusted them as one of their own. Second, they claimed to sell more than a product. Pinkham, for example, offered advice on health, diet and exercise and distributed booklets, and she spoke for women's rights and temperance; Rubinstein and Arden promised beauty and self-improvement. Third, they presented themselves as social reformers and as saviours of their sex and race: they sponsored good causes and spoke of their business as service to women. Fourth, they mainly served women and often encouraged them to bypass male doctors and male businessmen, so they created a feminine space within a male-dominated commercial world. Fifth, they used personal stories, testimonials and the language of care rather than the cold language of business.

**How they resembled male entrepreneurs.** Like the businessmen of their day, they were driven by profit, and when altruism and profit clashed, profit won. Their advertising was often extravagant and even misleading: Pinkham's 'Vegetable Compound' was sold as a cure for every 'female weakness' although it contained a large share of alcohol, so she sold booze while championing temperance; some of Rubinstein's claims were challenged by the authorities. They used shrewd advertising, brand building and publicity, including claims of marriage to aristocrats, and they built large fortunes. They also worked within the law of their state and followed the same market methods as men.

**Conclusion.** The business women were different in method and image but the same in motive: they proved that women could succeed in commerce as well as men, but their philanthropy was often a clever marketing strategy for their own profit.

**Or (b).** *A Child is Born* is an essay by the feminist writer Germaine Greer, taken from her book *Sex and Destiny*. She compares the traditional (eastern) societies with the modern west.

*Pregnancy.* In a traditional society a pregnant woman is surrounded by her family and community. She follows customs, taboos and rituals handed down to her, so she is spared the mental burden of inventing her own way. She is never alone, and in several societies she goes to her mother's house for the last months. In the modern west pregnancy is largely a private matter: the woman must take care of herself, read manuals and depend on doctors and nurses, so she often feels isolated and anxious.

*Childbirth.* In traditional societies birth is a public event attended by women relatives and neighbours, and it is celebrated with feasting, singing and gifts. Greer admits that the risk is higher: infant and maternal mortality in traditional societies is high, and every such death is a tragedy to be prevented. In the modern west the mother gives birth in a hospital with the best medical equipment, which makes birth safer, but she may be alone among strangers, and the event is seen as a medical treatment rather than a family celebration.

*Child rearing.* In a traditional society the baby is looked after by the whole family: the mother is helped, the baby is passed from arm to arm, and the woman is respected as the mother of her first child, though she may lose her own name. In the modern west the mother, often with only her husband, carries the whole burden of care, the child is brought up in a small family, and the relation between mother and child is stronger but more tense.

*Conclusion.* The traditional way gives emotional security and shared responsibility but carries medical risk; the modern way gives medical safety but takes away social support. Greer suggests that the best arrangement would combine the care of the traditional community with the safety of modern medicine.

@s 11
**Model answer.** Studying abroad is a dream of many young Nepalis and, in my view, it has both benefits and costs. Its main advantages are that students receive a good quality education with modern laboratories, libraries and research facilities, learn about other cultures and languages, and build confidence and independence. A foreign degree may also help a student find a better job. However, the costs are heavy: the tuition fees and living expenses are very high, many families borrow money or sell land, and students often work long hours that interfere with their studies. They may suffer from loneliness and homesickness, and, worst of all, many do not return, which causes a 'brain drain' from Nepal. In my opinion, studying abroad is a good choice for a student who has a clear goal, the money and the plan to return, and who comes back to serve the country with the knowledge gained.

@s 12
**Modern Social Media: Boon and Bane**

Social media such as Facebook, Instagram, YouTube, TikTok and X have become a part of our daily lives, and they have a number of advantages. They connect people across the world in seconds, so family members living abroad can talk to each other on video for free. They are a fast source of news and information, and during emergencies such as the earthquake or floods, they help to spread alerts and organise relief. For students and teachers they provide free tutorials, lectures and discussion groups. For small business owners they give an inexpensive way to advertise and sell their goods. They also give young people a platform to express their opinions and show their creativity, and they have helped many people become known to the public.

On the other hand, social media have some serious disadvantages. First, they waste time: many students spend hours scrolling and neglect their studies, sleep and physical activity. Second, false news and rumours spread faster than facts and can cause panic, hatred and even violence. Third, privacy is at risk, since personal data and photos can be stolen or misused, and cyber-bullying and online fraud are rising. Fourth, constant comparison with other people's 'perfect' lives leads to anxiety, depression and low self-esteem, and real relationships are weakened because people prefer a screen to a face-to-face conversation.

In conclusion, social media are like a knife: useful when used wisely and harmful when misused. We should use them for learning, communication and creativity, limit our time online, check the facts before we share any news, and protect our personal information. If used with discipline and good sense, social media can make our lives richer, but if we let them rule us, they can destroy our time, our peace and our relationships.
