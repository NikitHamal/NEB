@s 19
**Reading of the question.** "Two opposite forces each of magnitude $P$ are added to $S_1$ and $S_2$" means that a force $P$ is added to $S_1$ at $M$ in its own direction and a force $P$ is added at $N$ in the *opposite* direction (so $S_2$ becomes $S_2 - P$). The total of the forces is then unchanged, and the problem is to find how far the resultant moves. (Taking the opposite assignment, $S_1 - P$ at $M$ and $S_2 + P$ at $N$, gives the same distance in the other direction.)

**Before.** The like parallel forces $S_1$ at $M$ and $S_2$ at $N$ have the resultant $R = S_1 + S_2$ acting at $C$ on $MN$, where the moments about $C$ balance: $S_1\cdot MC = S_2\cdot CN$. With $MC + CN = k$:
$$MC = \frac{S_2\,k}{S_1 + S_2}.$$

**After.** The forces at $M$ and $N$ are $S_1 + P$ and $S_2 - P$ (both still in the same sense, assuming $S_2 > P$). Their resultant is
$$R' = (S_1 + P) + (S_2 - P) = S_1 + S_2 = R,$$
acting at $C'$ with $(S_1 + P)\cdot MC' = (S_2 - P)\cdot C'N$. Hence
$$MC' = \frac{(S_2 - P)\,k}{(S_1 + P) + (S_2 - P)} = \frac{(S_2 - P)\,k}{S_1 + S_2}.$$

**Displacement of the line of action:**
$$CC' = MC - MC' = \frac{S_2k}{S_1+S_2} - \frac{(S_2 - P)k}{S_1+S_2} = \frac{Pk}{S_1 + S_2}.$$
The resultant keeps its magnitude $S_1 + S_2$ and its line of action shifts through $\dfrac{Pk}{S_1+S_2}$ towards $M$ (the end where the force was increased). Hence proved.

**(a)** L'Hospital's rule: if $\dfrac{f(x)}{g(x)}$ takes the form $\dfrac00$ or $\dfrac{\infty}{\infty}$ as $x\to a$, then $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$, provided the right-hand limit exists. The substitution method fails there (the value is not defined), so the rule is needed.

*Example.* $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x}$ is of the form $\dfrac00$. By the rule it equals $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\cos x}{1} = 1$.

*Caution.* If the form is **not** indeterminate the rule gives a wrong answer: $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{x+1}{x+2} = \frac12$ by direct substitution, whereas the ratio of derivatives is $1$. So the statement is justified: the rule is useful (and valid) only for indeterminate forms.
