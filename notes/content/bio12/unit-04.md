Embryology is the study of how a flowering plant makes the next generation —
how the anther builds pollen, how the ovule builds an egg, how the two meet, and
how a single fertilised cell becomes an embryo packed inside a seed. Everything
in this unit happens inside a flower you can pick from a field in Chitwan or
Jumla, and almost every stage has a standard labelled diagram that the board
asks for by name.

::: key What the examiner asks from this unit
Three things come again and again: (1) a **labelled diagram** — T.S. of an
anther, L.S. of an anatropous ovule, the mature embryo sac, or the *Capsella*
embryo; (2) **double fertilization** — who fuses with whom, and what ploidy each
product has; (3) **ploidy counting** of every part of a seed. Learn the
sequence *microspore mother cell → microspore → pollen grain → male gametes* and
its female mirror *megaspore mother cell → megaspore → embryo sac → egg*, and
most of the unit falls into place.
:::

## 4.1 Asexual and sexual reproduction in angiosperms

Angiosperms reproduce in two ways. In **asexual reproduction** a new plant grows
from a part of the parent without gametes and without fertilization, so the
offspring is genetically identical to the parent — a **clone**. In **sexual
reproduction** two gametes fuse, the chromosome sets of two parents are mixed,
and the offspring differs from both.

### Asexual reproduction

**Vegetative propagation** is asexual reproduction by a vegetative organ — root,
stem, leaf or bud.

| Organ | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Underground stem | rhizome | ginger *Zingiber officinale*, turmeric |
| | tuber | potato *Solanum tuberosum* (eyes = buds) |
| | bulb | onion *Allium cepa*, garlic |
| | corm | *Colocasia*, *Crocus* |
| Sub-aerial stem | runner | *Cynodon dactylon* (dubo) |
| | sucker | mint *Mentha*, chrysanthemum |
| | offset | water hyacinth *Eichhornia crassipes* |
| Leaf | adventitious buds on the margin | *Bryophyllum pinnatum* |
| Root | adventitious buds | sweet potato *Ipomoea batatas*, *Dahlia* |

**Artificial methods** used by growers are **cutting** (sugarcane, rose),
**layering** (jasmine, litchi), **grafting** and **budding** (mango, citrus,
apple in Mustang and Jumla) and **micropropagation** by tissue culture.

**Apomixis** is a special case: a seed is formed **without fertilization**, so
the embryo is a clone of the mother. In **nucellar polyembryony** in *Citrus*
and mango, extra embryos arise from the nucellus alongside the sexual one.
Apomixis is prized in breeding because it fixes a hybrid's vigour permanently.

| | Advantage | Disadvantage |
|---|---|---|
| Asexual | quick; keeps the parent's exact qualities; works for seedless kinds such as banana | no variation, so the whole crop can fall to one disease; weak strains accumulate |
| Sexual | creates variation, the raw material of breeding and evolution; seeds are easy to store and disperse | slower; needs pollinators; offspring are not identical to the parent |

### Sexual reproduction

The flower is the reproductive shoot. The **androecium** is made of stamens,
each a **filament** bearing an **anther**; the **gynoecium** is made of carpels,
each a **pistil** with **ovary**, **style** and **stigma**. The anther is the
**microsporangium** and produces **microspores** (pollen grains); the ovule
inside the ovary is the **megasporangium** and produces **megaspores**.
Because the two kinds of spore are different, angiosperms are **heterosporous**.

The plant body we see is the diploid **sporophyte**. The pollen grain is the
much-reduced male **gametophyte** and the embryo sac is the female
**gametophyte**; both are haploid, and both are permanently retained inside the
sporophyte. This is the **alternation of generations**, compressed almost out of
sight.

## 4.2 Pollination

::: definition Pollination
Pollination is the transfer of pollen grains from the anther of a flower to the
stigma of a pistil of the same kind of flower. It is the essential step before
fertilization, because the non-motile male gametes of angiosperms can reach the
egg only through a pollen tube grown from a grain that has landed on a stigma.
:::

**Self-pollination (autogamy)** is transfer within the same flower.
**Geitonogamy** is transfer between two flowers of the same plant — genetically
still self-pollination, though an agent is needed. **Cross-pollination
(xenogamy)** is transfer to a flower on a *different* plant of the same species,
and only this brings new genes.

Self-pollination is ensured by **homogamy** (anther and stigma mature together),
by **cleistogamy** — flowers that never open, as in *Commelina benghalensis*,
*Viola* and *Oxalis* — and by **bud pollination**, as in pea and wheat.

Cross-pollination needs an external agent.

| Agent | Name | Flower characters | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wind | anemophily | small, dull, no nectar or scent; versatile anthers, huge amounts of light dry pollen; feathery stigma | maize *Zea mays*, grasses, *Pinus*, *Cannabis* |
| Water | hydrophily | pollen and stigma unwettable; pollen often long and thread-like | *Vallisneria*, *Hydrilla*, *Zostera* |
| Insect | entomophily | large, coloured, scented; nectar; sticky spiny pollen; sticky stigma | *Salvia*, mustard, *Ficus* (with its wasp) |
| Bird | ornithophily | large, red, tubular, plenty of watery nectar, no scent | *Bombax ceiba* (simal), *Butea monosperma*, *Erythrina* |
| Bat | chiropterophily | large, dull, strong smell, open at night, tough | *Kigelia*, *Adansonia*, *Anthocephalus* |

Because continuous self-pollination narrows the gene pool, plants have evolved
**contrivances for cross-pollination**:

- **Dicliny (unisexuality)** — separate male and female flowers, either on one
  plant (monoecious: maize, castor) or on separate plants (dioecious: papaya,
  *Cannabis*), which makes selfing impossible in dioecious species.
- **Dichogamy** — the two sexes ripen at different times: **protandry** (anthers
  first) in sunflower *Helianthus annuus* and *Salvia*, **protogyny** (stigma
  first) in *Plantago* and *Michelia*.
- **Herkogamy** — a physical barrier, such as the pollinia of *Calotropis* or a
  stigma held above the anthers.
- **Heterostyly** — two or three style lengths in the same species, as in
  *Primula* and *Oxalis*, so that pollen is placed at the level of a different
  plant's stigma.
- **Self-incompatibility (self-sterility)** — a genetic block that prevents
  pollen from germinating or the tube from growing on the plant's own stigma, as
  in tobacco, crucifers and *Abutilon*.

| | Self-pollination | Cross-pollination |
|---|---|---|
| Agent | not needed | always needed |
| Pollen used | very little | very large amounts wasted |
| Variation | none | plenty |
| Purity of line | maintained | lost |
| Vigour | falls over generations (inbreeding depression) | hybrid vigour |
| Certainty | almost certain | uncertain, depends on the agent |

## 4.3 Fertilization

When a compatible pollen grain lands on a receptive stigma it absorbs water and
sugars and **germinates**. The intine grows out through a germ pore as the
**pollen tube**, which digests its way down through the style, guided
chemotropically by substances secreted by the synergids. The **tube nucleus**
leads at the tip; the generative cell divides (if it has not already) to give
**two male gametes** that follow behind.

The tube reaches the ovule and enters it in one of three ways:

| Route | Name | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Through the micropyle | **porogamy** | commonest, e.g. most angiosperms |
| Through the chalaza | **chalazogamy** | *Casuarina* |
| Through the integument or funicle | **mesogamy** | *Cucurbita*, *Populus* |

The tube then pushes into one **synergid**, whose **filiform apparatus** guides
it, and bursts, releasing both male gametes into the embryo sac. What follows is
unique to angiosperms.

::: definition Double fertilization
Double fertilization is the fusion of **one male gamete with the egg**
(**syngamy**, giving a diploid zygote) and of **the second male gamete with the
two polar nuclei of the central cell** (**triple fusion**, giving a triploid
primary endosperm nucleus) in the same embryo sac. It was discovered by
**S. G. Nawaschin in 1898** in *Lilium* and *Fritillaria*.
:::

Syngamy: male gamete (n) + egg (n) → **zygote (2n)** → embryo.
Triple fusion: male gamete (n) + two polar nuclei (n + n) → **primary endosperm
nucleus (3n)** → endosperm.

Triple fusion is called *triple* because **three** nuclei fuse, not because the
product is triploid — though it is. The significance of double fertilization is
that the food store is only laid down **after** the egg has actually been
fertilised, so no plant wastes reserves on an ovule that will never make a seed.

After fertilization the whole flower is rebuilt:

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Ovule | seed |
| Ovary | fruit |
| Ovary wall | pericarp |
| Integuments | seed coat (testa and tegmen) |
| Zygote | embryo |
| Primary endosperm nucleus | endosperm |
| Nucellus | usually consumed; if it persists, **perisperm** |
| Micropyle | micropyle of the seed (lets water in at germination) |
| Synergids and antipodals | degenerate |
| Calyx, corolla, stamens, style | usually fall off |

lobes, and each lobe contains two **microsporangia** (pollen sacs), so a

The wall of each microsporangium has four layers, from outside in:

| Layer | Nature | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Epidermis** | single, protective | protection |
| **Endothecium** | single, with fibrous thickenings on the inner and radial walls | hygroscopic; on drying it curls and **tears the anther open** at the stomium |
| **Middle layers** | 2–4 thin layers | ephemeral; crushed as pollen grows |
| **Tapetum** | innermost, cells often bi- or multinucleate, dense cytoplasm | **nourishes** the developing microspores; supplies **sporopollenin** for the exine, the pollenkitt and the enzymes and proteins of the pollen wall |

::: caution The tapetum is nutritive, not protective
A very common mistake is to give the tapetum the job of protection. Protection
is the epidermis; dehiscence is the endothecium; **nutrition of the microspores
is the tapetum**. If the tapetum fails, the pollen is sterile — which is exactly
how male-sterile lines are used in hybrid seed production.
:::

### Microsporogenesis and microgametogenesis

Inside each young sporangium the **sporogenous tissue** enlarges into
**microspore mother cells** (pollen mother cells, 2n). Each one divides by
**meiosis** to give a **tetrad of four haploid microspores**. This is
**microsporogenesis**. The tetrad then separates and each microspore matures
into a pollen grain — **microgametogenesis**.

The pollen grain has a two-layered wall: a hard, sculptured outer **exine** made
of **sporopollenin**, the most resistant organic material known (this is why
pollen survives as fossils), interrupted by thin **germ pores**; and a thin,
cellulose-and-pectin **intine** inside.

The microspore nucleus divides mitotically and very unequally, giving a large
**vegetative (tube) cell** with abundant food and a small, spindle-shaped
**generative cell** floating in its cytoplasm. In about 60 % of families the
pollen is shed at this **2-celled stage** and the generative cell divides later
inside the pollen tube; in the rest (Poaceae, Asteraceae, Brassicaceae) it
divides before shedding, so the grain is shed **3-celled**. Either way, the
mature male gametophyte is just **three cells** — one vegetative cell and two
non-motile **male gametes**.

**Problem.** An anther of a mustard plant produced **1 200 pollen grains**.

## 4.5 Development of female gametophyte

### The ovule

The ovule (megasporangium) is attached to the placenta of the ovary by a stalk,
the **funicle**. Its parts are the **hilum** (where funicle meets body), the
**chalaza** (the base of the body, opposite the micropyle), the **nucellus**
(the nutritive tissue), one or two **integuments** that enclose the nucellus
leaving a narrow pore, the **micropyle**, and, deep inside the nucellus, the
**embryo sac** (female gametophyte). In an **anatropous** ovule the funicle is
fused along one side as the **raphe**.

| Type of ovule | Body relative to funicle | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Orthotropous (atropous) | straight, micropyle in line with funicle | *Polygonum*, *Piper*, *Cycas* |
| **Anatropous** | completely inverted, micropyle beside the hilum — the commonest type, in about 80 % of families | mustard, sunflower, most dicots |
| Campylotropous | body curved, embryo sac slightly curved | *Brassica*, legumes |
| Amphitropous | body and embryo sac both strongly curved, horseshoe-shaped | *Poppy*, *Alisma* |
| Circinotropous | funicle grows right round the ovule | *Opuntia* |

### Megasporogenesis and megagametogenesis

A single hypodermal cell of the nucellus at the micropylar end enlarges as the
**archesporial cell**. In most plants it directly becomes the **megaspore mother
cell** (2n). This divides by **meiosis** to give a **linear tetrad of four
haploid megaspores** — that is **megasporogenesis**. Three of them, usually the
three towards the micropyle, degenerate; the **chalazal megaspore** survives as
the **functional megaspore**.

The functional megaspore now enlarges and its nucleus divides **mitotically
three times without any wall formation** — a free-nuclear stage — giving 2, then
4, then 8 nuclei. Four nuclei lie at each pole. Walls then form around them:
three cells at the micropylar end make the **egg apparatus** (one **egg cell**
flanked by two **synergids** whose **filiform apparatus** guides the pollen
tube), three at the chalazal end become the **antipodal cells**, and the
remaining two nuclei — one from each pole — move to the centre as the **polar
nuclei** inside the large **central cell**.

The result is the **Polygonum-type embryo sac**: **7 cells and 8 nuclei**. It is
called **monosporic** because it develops from a single megaspore, and it occurs
in over 80 % of angiosperms.

::: example Worked example 4.2 — counting divisions in the ovule
**Problem.** A pea pod contains **8 seeds**. Assuming no ovule was wasted,
(a) how many megaspore mother cells were needed, (b) how many megaspores were
formed and how many degenerated, (c) how many mitotic divisions took place in
forming the eight embryo sacs, and (d) how many pollen grains were needed at
the very least?

**Solution.**

(a) One seed comes from one ovule, and each ovule has one megaspore mother
cell → **8 megaspore mother cells**.

(b) Each meiosis gives 4 megaspores, so $8 \times 4 = 32$ megaspores were
formed and $8 \times 3 = 24$ degenerated.

(c) Each functional megaspore undergoes 3 rounds of division: 1 + 2 + 4 = **7
mitotic divisions** per embryo sac, so $8 \times 7 = 56$ mitotic divisions.

(d) Each pollen grain fertilises one ovule, so **at least 8 pollen grains** —
which would need $8 \div 4 = 2$ microspore mother cells.

::: example Worked example 4.3 — ploidy of the parts of a seed
**Problem.** Give the ploidy of: (a) nucellus, (b) megaspore mother cell,

(f) primary endosperm nucleus, (g) endosperm of a maize grain, (h) embryo,
(i) seed coat, (j) perisperm of black pepper.

**Solution.** Work outwards from the rule: everything belonging to the parent
sporophyte is **2n**, everything in the gametophyte is **n**, and only the two
products of double fertilization differ.

(a) nucellus **2n** — sporophyte tissue.
(b) megaspore mother cell **2n** — it has not yet undergone meiosis.
(c) synergid **n**, (d) antipodal **n** — both are embryo-sac cells.
(e) central cell has **two n polar nuclei**, so it is 2 × n but has *not* fused
with a gamete.
(f) primary endosperm nucleus **3n** — n + n + n from triple fusion.
(g) endosperm **3n**.
(h) embryo **2n** — from the zygote (n + n).
(i) seed coat **2n** — it is the integument, mother-plant tissue.
(j) perisperm **2n** — it is leftover nucellus.

## 4.6 Dicot and monocot embryo

### The dicot embryo — *Capsella bursa-pastoris*

The zygote rests for a while (letting the endosperm get ahead), then divides by
a **transverse** wall into a small **apical (terminal) cell** towards the chalaza
and a large **basal cell** towards the micropyle.

- The **basal cell** divides transversely again and again to make a filament of
  6–10 cells, the **suspensor**, which pushes the embryo down into the
  nourishing endosperm. Its lowest cell swells into a **vesicular haustorial
  cell** at the micropyle; its topmost cell, the **hypophysis**, later forms the
  tip of the radicle and the root cap.
- The **apical cell** divides by two vertical walls and one transverse wall into
  an **octant** of eight cells. The four **epibasal** cells make the plumule and
  the two cotyledons; the four **hypobasal** cells make the hypocotyl.
- Periclinal divisions then mark out the three primary tissue layers —
  **dermatogen** (future epidermis), **periblem** (cortex) and **plerome**
  (stele).
- The embryo passes through a **globular**, then a **heart-shaped** stage as the
  two cotyledon primordia bulge out, then a **torpedo** stage, and finally
  curves into the mature embryo.

### The monocot embryo

The first division is the same, but the embryo does not form two cotyledons.
The mature grass embryo, lying to one side of the endosperm, has:

- a single shield-shaped cotyledon, the **scutellum**, pressed against the
  endosperm; its **epithelial layer** secretes enzymes and absorbs the digested
  food;
- a **coleoptile**, a hollow protective sheath over the plumule, which pushes
  through the soil at germination;
- a **coleorhiza**, a similar sheath over the radicle;
- an **epiblast**, a small flap opposite the scutellum, often read as the
  vestige of the second cotyledon.

| Feature | Dicot embryo | Monocot embryo |
|---|---|---|
| Cotyledons | two, lateral | one, the scutellum, lateral |
| Position | central in the seed | to one side of the endosperm |
| Plumule | terminal, naked | lateral, covered by the **coleoptile** |
| Radicle | naked | covered by the **coleorhiza** |
| Epiblast | absent | usually present |
| Food store | usually in the cotyledons (non-endospermic) | in the endosperm (endospermic) |
| Example | *Capsella*, pea, gram | maize, wheat, rice |

## 4.7 Endosperm

::: definition Endosperm
The endosperm is the **triploid (3n) nutritive tissue** of an angiosperm seed,
formed from the primary endosperm nucleus after triple fusion. It feeds the
developing embryo and, in many seeds, the germinating seedling as well.
:::

The endosperm develops in one of three ways.

| Type | How it develops | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Nuclear** | the primary endosperm nucleus divides repeatedly **without wall formation**, giving a free-nuclear multinucleate mass; walls appear later, from the periphery inwards, or never | commonest type — maize, wheat, rice, *Capsella*, coconut (the "water" is free-nuclear endosperm and the white kernel is the later cellular part) |
| **Cellular** | a **wall follows every division** from the very first, so there is no free-nuclear stage | *Datura*, *Petunia*, balsam, *Adoxa* |
| **Helobial** | intermediate: the first division is followed by a wall that makes two unequal chambers, then the micropylar chamber goes free-nuclear | order Helobiales — *Alisma*, *Eremurus* |

In some seeds the seed coat grows inward in irregular folds, giving a
**ruminate endosperm**, as in betel nut *Areca catechu* and nutmeg
*Myristica fragrans*.

Seeds are grouped by what happens to the endosperm:

- **Endospermic (albuminous) seeds** keep the endosperm at maturity, and the
  cotyledons stay thin: maize, wheat, rice, castor *Ricinus communis*, coconut.
  In cereals the outermost endosperm layer is the protein-rich **aleurone
  layer**, which on germination secretes α-amylase to digest the starchy
  endosperm.
- **Non-endospermic (exalbuminous) seeds** have the endosperm completely
  absorbed by the cotyledons during seed development, so the fleshy cotyledons
  themselves are the food store: pea, gram, bean, groundnut, mustard.

If the **nucellus** also persists in the mature seed as a food store it is
called the **perisperm**, as in black pepper *Piper nigrum*, beet and cardamom.
A perisperm is 2n and is not endosperm at all.

::: tip Do not confuse the three food stores
**Endosperm** is 3n and comes from triple fusion. **Cotyledon** is 2n and is
part of the embryo. **Perisperm** is 2n and is leftover nucellus. Examiners ask
this as a one-mark difference every other year.
:::

The endosperm matters far beyond the plant: the endosperm of wheat, rice and
maize is the staple food of most of the world, including the rice grown in the
Terai and the maize grown on Nepal's hill terraces. Wheat flour is almost pure
endosperm, and the coconut's liquid endosperm, rich in cytokinins, is used in
plant tissue-culture media.

## Chapter summary

- **Asexual reproduction** (rhizome, tuber, bulb, runner, cutting, grafting,
  apomixis) gives clones — quick and uniform but with no variation; **sexual
  reproduction** gives variation.
- **Pollination** is the transfer of pollen from anther to stigma: autogamy,
  geitonogamy and xenogamy; agents are wind (anemophily), water (hydrophily),
  insect (entomophily), bird (ornithophily) and bat (chiropterophily).
- Cross-pollination is enforced by **dicliny, dichogamy (protandry and
  protogyny), herkogamy, heterostyly and self-incompatibility**.
- The **anther is dithecous and tetrasporangiate**; its wall is epidermis,
  endothecium (dehiscence), middle layers and **tapetum** (nutrition and
  sporopollenin).
- **Microsporogenesis**: microspore mother cell (2n) → meiosis → tetrad of
  microspores (n). **Microgametogenesis**: microspore → 2-celled pollen grain
  (vegetative + generative cell) → **two male gametes** in the pollen tube.
- **Megasporogenesis**: megaspore mother cell (2n) → meiosis → linear tetrad;
  three die, the chalazal one survives. Three free-nuclear mitoses then give the
  **Polygonum-type, monosporic, 7-celled and 8-nucleate embryo sac** — egg + 2
  synergids, 3 antipodals, and 2 polar nuclei in the central cell.
- **Double fertilization** (Nawaschin, 1898): syngamy gives the **2n zygote**
  and triple fusion gives the **3n primary endosperm nucleus**. Ovule → seed,
  ovary → fruit, integuments → seed coat, nucellus → perisperm if it persists.
- The **dicot embryo** (*Capsella*) passes through 2-celled, octant, globular,
  heart-shaped and torpedo stages, with a **suspensor** from the basal cell; the
  **monocot embryo** has one cotyledon, the **scutellum**, plus a **coleoptile**,
  a **coleorhiza** and an **epiblast**.
- **Endosperm** development is **nuclear** (commonest), **cellular** or
  **helobial**. Seeds are **endospermic** (maize, castor, coconut) or
  **non-endospermic** (pea, gram).

**Group B — Short answer (4 marks each)**

1. Draw a labelled diagram of a transverse section of a young anther and state the function of each wall layer. <span class="marks">[4]</span>
2. Describe the development of the male gametophyte from the microspore mother cell to the pollen tube. <span class="marks">[4]</span>
3. What is double fertilization? Write the two fusions with the ploidy of each product and state its significance. <span class="marks">[4]</span>
4. An anther contained 250 microspore mother cells. How many pollen grains, male gametes and ovules could it at most fertilise, and how many seeds could result? <span class="marks">[4]</span>
5. Differentiate between a dicot and a monocot embryo (any four points), and define coleoptile and coleorhiza. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

::: note Answers to Group B
**1.** The section is **tetrasporangiate** — two lobes, four microsporangia, a
central connective with a vascular bundle. Wall layers from outside in:
**epidermis** — protection; **endothecium** — fibrous thickenings that dry and
curl, tearing the anther open at the **stomium**; **middle layers** — 2–4 short-lived
layers, crushed as pollen grows; **tapetum** — innermost, multinucleate, supplies
nutrition, sporopollenin, pollenkitt and enzymes to the microspores.

**2.** Sporogenous tissue → **microspore mother cells (2n)** → **meiosis** →
**tetrad of four haploid microspores** (microsporogenesis). Each microspore
builds a two-layered wall — sporopollenin **exine** with germ pores, and cellulose
**intine**. Its nucleus then divides unequally by mitosis into a large
**vegetative (tube) cell** and a small **generative cell**; the grain is usually
shed at this 2-celled stage. On the stigma the grain germinates, the intine
grows out through a germ pore as the **pollen tube**, and the generative cell
divides into **two male gametes**, which travel down the tube behind the tube
nucleus.

**3.** Double fertilization is the occurrence of two fusions in the same embryo
sac. **Syngamy:** one male gamete (n) + egg (n) → **zygote (2n)** → embryo.
**Triple fusion:** the second male gamete (n) + two polar nuclei (n + n) →
**primary endosperm nucleus (3n)** → endosperm. It was discovered by Nawaschin
(1898). *Significance:* the food-storing endosperm is laid down **only after**
the egg is fertilised, so no reserves are wasted on an ovule that will not become
a seed; the triploid endosperm is also vigorous and gives the young seedling a
strong start. It is found only in angiosperms.

**4.** Each microspore mother cell gives 4 pollen grains:

$$ 250 \times 4 = 1000 \text{ pollen grains} $$

Each grain forms two male gametes: $1000 \times 2 = 2000$ **male gametes**. But
only **one** grain fertilises one ovule (the second gamete goes to the polar
nuclei), so these grains could fertilise at most **1000 ovules** and give at most
**1000 seeds**.

**5.**

| Feature | Dicot embryo | Monocot embryo |
|---|---|---|
| Number of cotyledons | two | one (scutellum) |
| Position of cotyledons | terminal, on either side of the axis | lateral, to one side |
| Plumule | naked | enclosed in a coleoptile |
| Radicle | naked | enclosed in a coleorhiza |
| Epiblast | absent | usually present |

The **coleoptile** is the hollow protective sheath over the plumule of a grass
embryo, and the **coleorhiza** is the protective sheath over its radicle.
:::

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. Describe the structure of a typical anatropous ovule with a labelled diagram,
   and explain the development of the female gametophyte from the megaspore
   mother cell to the mature embryo sac. <span class="marks">[8]</span>
2. What is pollination? Describe the different agents of cross-pollination with
   the flower characters and examples of each, and explain any four
   contrivances that plants use to ensure cross-pollination. <span class="marks">[8]</span>
3. Describe the development of a dicot embryo in *Capsella bursa-pastoris* with
   labelled diagrams, and compare it with a monocot embryo. What is endosperm,
   and how do the three types of endosperm development differ? <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Answers to Group C
**1. Outline.** (i) The ovule is attached to the placenta by the **funicle**;
the point of attachment to the body is the **hilum**, and in an anatropous ovule
the funicle is fused along one side as the **raphe**, so that the body is
completely inverted and the **micropyle** lies right beside the hilum. (ii) The
body consists of the **nucellus**, surrounded by one or two **integuments**
which leave the micropyle open at one end, and the **chalaza** at the opposite
end. The **embryo sac** lies within the nucellus. (iii) A hypodermal
**archesporial cell** at the micropylar end becomes the **megaspore mother cell
(2n)**. (iv) **Megasporogenesis:** meiosis gives a **linear tetrad of four
haploid megaspores**; the three nearer the micropyle degenerate and the
**chalazal megaspore** is functional. (v) **Megagametogenesis:** the functional
megaspore enlarges and its nucleus divides mitotically three times **without
wall formation**, giving 2, then 4, then 8 nuclei, four at each pole.
(vi) Walls then form: three cells at the micropylar end make the **egg
apparatus** (egg + two synergids with a filiform apparatus), three at the
chalazal end become **antipodal cells**, and one nucleus from each pole moves to
the centre as the two **polar nuclei** in the large **central cell**.
(vii) The mature sac is therefore **7-celled and 8-nucleate**, monosporic, and
is called the **Polygonum type**. A labelled diagram of the ovule and of the
embryo sac carries most of the marks.

**2. Outline.** (i) *Definition* — pollination is the transfer of pollen from
the anther to the stigma; self (autogamy), geitonogamy and cross (xenogamy).
(ii) *Anemophily* — wind: small, dull, scentless flowers, versatile anthers,
enormous amounts of light dry pollen, feathery stigmas — maize, grasses.
(iii) *Hydrophily* — water: unwettable pollen, often thread-like — *Vallisneria*,
*Hydrilla*, *Zostera*. (iv) *Entomophily* — insects: large, brightly coloured,
scented flowers with nectar, sticky spiny pollen and sticky stigma — *Salvia*,
mustard, *Ficus*. (v) *Ornithophily* — birds: large red tubular flowers with
copious watery nectar and no scent — *Bombax ceiba*, *Butea monosperma*.
(vi) *Chiropterophily* — bats: large, dull, strong-smelling, night-opening
flowers — *Kigelia*, *Adansonia*. (vii) *Contrivances* (any four): **dicliny**
(unisexual flowers, monoecious maize or dioecious papaya); **dichogamy**
(protandry in sunflower, protogyny in *Plantago*); **herkogamy** (a physical
barrier, as in *Calotropis*); **heterostyly** (different style lengths in
*Primula*); **self-incompatibility** (a genetic block, as in tobacco).

**3. Outline.** (i) The zygote divides transversely into a small **apical cell**
and a large **basal cell**. (ii) The basal cell forms the **suspensor**, whose
lowest cell becomes the vesicular haustorium and whose top cell, the
**hypophysis**, gives the radicle tip. (iii) The apical cell divides by two
vertical and one transverse wall into an **octant**; the four epibasal cells
give the plumule and cotyledons, the four hypobasal cells give the hypocotyl.
(iv) Periclinal divisions give **dermatogen, periblem and plerome**. (v) The
embryo passes through **globular → heart-shaped → torpedo → mature** stages,
ending with two cotyledons, a plumule and a radicle. (vi) *Comparison:* a
monocot embryo has one lateral cotyledon, the **scutellum**, with a

the cotyledons. (vii) *Endosperm* is the 3n nutritive tissue formed from the
primary endosperm nucleus. (viii) **Nuclear** type — repeated free-nuclear
divisions with walls laid down later or never (maize, coconut); **cellular**
type — a wall after every division from the first (*Datura*, *Petunia*);
**helobial** type — one wall makes two unequal chambers and the micropylar
chamber then goes free-nuclear (*Alisma*, Helobiales).
