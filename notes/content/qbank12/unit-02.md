Group A of the NEB Grade 12 Physics paper is eleven one-mark multiple-choice
questions. Eleven marks is not much, but they are the quickest marks in the
paper and, because the eleven items are spread across the whole syllabus, they
punish any unit you skipped. This chapter drills all twenty-five CDC units in
syllabus order. Each section opens with a **high-yield facts table**, then gives
eight to ten MCQs, then an answer key in which every answer carries a
**one-line reason** and, where a wrong option encodes a real misconception,
names it.

::: key What Group A actually tests
Group A rarely needs a long calculation. It tests four things: a **definition or
statement**, a **unit or dimension**, **one substitution into one formula**, or a
**misconception** offered as a distractor. The recent papers have also added
**graph-reading** items (displacement–time graph of SHM, $l$–$T^{2}$ graph, a
cyclic process on a $p$–$V$ diagram). If an item is eating more than ninety
seconds, re-read the question: you have probably missed a word such as
*maximum*, *minimum* or *same*.
:::

::: caution How the year tags in this chapter work
A tag such as **(NEB 2081)** is printed only on a question that we saw in that
year's board paper; **(NEB 2079 model)** means the official NEB model question
set for 2079, which we downloaded and read. Items tagged like this are
reproduced in substance, with the options shortened or reordered. The word
**data** after a tag means that the numbers come from a real past numerical
question whose stem has been turned into a one-mark item. The 2078
paper circulates online under two labels (board and model); we tag it
**(NEB 2078)** and note that its label is disputed. Every untagged question is
original and built to the same pattern — it is practice, not a leaked paper.
:::

## 2.1 Rotational dynamics

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Angular momentum | $L = I\omega = mvr$; unit $\text{kg m}^{2}\text{s}^{-1}$ |
| Rotational kinetic energy | $K = \tfrac12 I\omega^{2}$; also $L = \sqrt{2IK}$ |
| Torque | $\tau = I\alpha = \dfrac{dL}{dt}$; work $W = \tau\theta$; power $P = \tau\omega$ |
| Radius of gyration | $I = Mk^{2}$, so $k = \sqrt{I/M}$ |
| Rod, length $l$, perpendicular axis | centre: $\dfrac{Ml^{2}}{12}$; one end: $\dfrac{Ml^{2}}{3}$ |
| Equations of angular motion | $\omega = \omega_0+\alpha t$, $\theta = \omega_0t+\tfrac12\alpha t^{2}$, $\omega^{2} = \omega_0^{2}+2\alpha\theta$ |
| Conservation | no external torque $\Rightarrow$ $L$ constant $\Rightarrow$ $I_1\omega_1 = I_2\omega_2$ |

1. The product of the moment of inertia and the angular velocity of a body is its <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2079 model)
   (a) torque (b) linear momentum (c) angular momentum (d) force
2. The ratio of the moment of inertia of a uniform rod about an axis through one end to that about its centre (both perpendicular to the rod) is <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2082)
   (a) 2 (b) 3 (c) 4 (d) 6
3. A flywheel of moment of inertia $2\ \text{kg m}^{2}$ rotates at $10\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$. Its rotational kinetic energy is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $20\ \text{J}$ (b) $40\ \text{J}$ (c) $100\ \text{J}$ (d) $200\ \text{J}$
4. A skater spinning with arms outstretched pulls her arms in. Her angular velocity increases because <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) her angular momentum increases (b) her moment of inertia decreases while angular momentum is conserved (c) her kinetic energy is conserved (d) a torque acts on her
5. The radius of gyration of a body of mass $M$ and moment of inertia $I$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2078)
   (a) $\sqrt{MI}$ (b) $\sqrt{I/M}$ (c) $I/M$ (d) $\sqrt{M/I}$
6. A torque of $10\ \text{N m}$ acts on a wheel of moment of inertia $0.5\ \text{kg m}^{2}$. The angular acceleration is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $5\ \text{rad s}^{-2}$ (b) $20\ \text{rad s}^{-2}$ (c) $0.05\ \text{rad s}^{-2}$ (d) $2\ \text{rad s}^{-2}$
7. If the rotational kinetic energy of a body is $E$ and its moment of inertia is $I$, its angular momentum is <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2081)
   (a) $\sqrt{2IE}$ (b) $2IE$ (c) $\sqrt{E/2I}$ (d) $IE/2$
8. A motor applies a torque of $20\ \text{N m}$ to a shaft turning at $50\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$. The power delivered is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0.4\ \text{W}$ (b) $70\ \text{W}$ (c) $2.5\ \text{W}$ (d) $1000\ \text{W}$
9. A wheel starts from rest with $\alpha = 2\ \text{rad s}^{-2}$. The angular velocity after turning through $25\ \text{rad}$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $5\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$ (b) $10\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$ (c) $50\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$ (d) $100\ \text{rad s}^{-1}$

::: note Answers — 2.1
**1.** (c) — $L = I\omega$ by definition; torque is $I\alpha$, not $I\omega$.
**2.** (c) — $\frac{Ml^{2}/3}{Ml^{2}/12} = 4$. Students who quote "$1/3$ versus $1/12$" and answer 3 have forgotten to divide.
**3.** (c) — $\tfrac12(2)(10)^{2} = 100\ \text{J}$. Option (d) forgets the $\tfrac12$, option (a) forgets to square $\omega$.
**4.** (b) — no external torque, so $L = I\omega$ is fixed; smaller $I$ forces larger $\omega$. Kinetic energy is *not* conserved (option c): she does work pulling her arms in, so $K$ rises.
**5.** (b) — $I = Mk^{2}$.
**6.** (b) — $\alpha = \tau/I = 10/0.5 = 20$. Option (a) multiplies instead of dividing.
**7.** (a) — $E = L^{2}/2I$, so $L = \sqrt{2IE}$.
**8.** (d) — $P = \tau\omega = 20\times50 = 1000\ \text{W}$.
**9.** (b) — $\omega^{2} = 2\alpha\theta = 2(2)(25) = 100$, so $\omega = 10$.
:::

## 2.2 Periodic motion

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Defining condition of SHM | $a = -\omega^{2}x$ (directed to the mean position) |
| Speed | $v = \omega\sqrt{A^{2}-x^{2}}$; maximum $v_{max} = \omega A$ at the mean position |
| Energy | $E = \tfrac12 kA^{2} = \tfrac12 m\omega^{2}A^{2}$; constant |
| Spring–mass | $T = 2\pi\sqrt{m/k}$; independent of $g$ |
| Simple pendulum | $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}$; $l$–$T^{2}$ graph is a straight line of slope $4\pi^{2}/g$ |
| Spring cut into $n$ equal parts | each part has force constant $nk$ |
| Resonance | driving frequency = natural frequency; amplitude maximum |

1. In simple harmonic motion the acceleration is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) constant (b) proportional to displacement and directed towards the mean position (c) proportional to velocity (d) zero at the extreme position
2. A mass of $0.25\ \text{kg}$ hangs from a spring of force constant $100\ \text{N m}^{-1}$. Its time period is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0.31\ \text{s}$ (b) $0.63\ \text{s}$ (c) $3.14\ \text{s}$ (d) $6.28\ \text{s}$
3. At the mean position of a particle in SHM <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) kinetic energy is minimum (b) potential energy is maximum (c) speed is maximum and acceleration is zero (d) speed and acceleration are both maximum
4. A simple pendulum is taken to the Moon, where $g$ is one-sixth of its value on Earth. Its time period <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) becomes $6$ times (b) becomes $\sqrt{6}$ times (c) becomes $1/\sqrt{6}$ times (d) is unchanged
5. A particle of mass $0.5\ \text{kg}$ is attached to a spring of force constant $200\ \text{N m}^{-1}$ and oscillates with amplitude $0.1\ \text{m}$. The total energy is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0.5\ \text{J}$ (b) $1\ \text{J}$ (c) $2\ \text{J}$ (d) $10\ \text{J}$
6. A uniform spring is cut into two equal halves. The force constant and the frequency of each half (for the same mass) are <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2080)
   (a) halved; halved (b) doubled; multiplied by $\sqrt{2}$ (c) doubled; doubled (d) unchanged; unchanged
7. A simple pendulum bob of mass $0.40\ \text{kg}$ has potential energy $0.044\ \text{J}$ at an extreme point, taking the mean point as reference. Its kinetic energy at the mean point is <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2079 model)
   (a) $0.022\ \text{J}$ (b) $0.044\ \text{J}$ (c) $0.011\ \text{J}$ (d) $0.033\ \text{J}$
8. The time period of a simple pendulum inside an orbiting satellite is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) zero (b) the same as on Earth (c) infinite, because effective $g$ is zero (d) doubled
9. A particle of amplitude $0.02\ \text{m}$ oscillates in SHM with frequency $5\ \text{Hz}$. Its maximum speed is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0.10\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (b) $0.63\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (c) $6.3\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (d) $0.20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$
10. Resonance occurs when <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) the driving force is very large (b) damping is zero (c) the driving frequency equals the natural frequency of the system (d) the amplitude of the driver is maximum

::: note Answers — 2.2
**1.** (b) — $a = -\omega^{2}x$. At the extreme position acceleration is *maximum*, which is why (d) is wrong.
**2.** (a) — $T = 2\pi\sqrt{0.25/100} = 2\pi(0.05) = 0.314\ \text{s}$. Option (c) forgets the square root.
**3.** (c) — at $x = 0$: $v = \omega A$ (maximum), $a = 0$, $K$ maximum, $U$ minimum.
**4.** (b) — $T \propto 1/\sqrt{g}$, so $T$ increases by $\sqrt{6}$. Option (a) treats $T$ as proportional to $1/g$.
**5.** (b) — $E = \tfrac12kA^{2} = \tfrac12(200)(0.1)^{2} = 1\ \text{J}$. The mass does not enter.
**6.** (b) — a half-length spring is stiffer: $k' = 2k$, so $f' = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{2k/m} = \sqrt{2}f$.
**7.** (b) — energy is conserved at every point of the swing, so $K_{mean} = U_{extreme} = 0.044\ \text{J}$; the mass, time period and amplitude are red herrings.
**8.** (c) — in free fall the effective $g$ is zero, so $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l/g}\to\infty$: the pendulum does not oscillate. (A spring–mass system would be unaffected, because $T$ does not contain $g$.)
**9.** (b) — $v_{max} = \omega A = 2\pi(5)(0.02) = 0.63\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Option (d) uses $\omega = f$.
**10.** (c) — resonance is a *frequency match*; amplitude of the driver or absence of damping is not the condition (zero damping would only make the peak infinite).
:::

## 2.3 Fluid statics

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Pressure at depth | $P = P_0+\rho gh$; gauge pressure $\rho gh$ |
| Surface energy | $E = T\,\Delta A$; soap bubble has two surfaces |
| Capillary rise | $h = \dfrac{2T\cos\theta}{r\rho g}$; $h\propto 1/r$, $h\propto 1/g$ |
| Stokes' law; terminal velocity | $F = 6\pi\eta rv$; $v_t = \dfrac{2r^{2}(\rho-\sigma)g}{9\eta}\propto r^{2}$ |
| Poiseuille | $\dfrac{V}{t} = \dfrac{\pi Pr^{4}}{8\eta l}$ |
| Continuity; Bernoulli | $A_1v_1 = A_2v_2$; $P+\tfrac12\rho v^{2}+\rho gh$ = constant |
| Floating body | $\dfrac{\text{submerged volume}}{\text{total volume}} = \dfrac{\rho_{body}}{\rho_{liquid}}$ |

1. Two capillary tubes of radii $4\ \text{mm}$ and $2\ \text{mm}$ are dipped in the same liquid. The ratio of the heights of rise in them is <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2081)
   (a) $1:2$ (b) $2:1$ (c) $1:4$ (d) $4:1$
2. If the height of capillary rise in a tube on the Earth is $h$, the height on the Moon ($g_{Moon} = g/6$) is <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2078)
   (a) $h/6$ (b) $h$ (c) $6h$ (d) $36h$
3. Two identical raindrops, each falling at a terminal velocity of $0.15\ \text{m s}^{-1}$, combine into one drop. The new terminal velocity is about <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2082)
   (a) $0.15\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (b) $0.24\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (c) $0.30\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (d) $0.60\ \text{m s}^{-1}$
4. The work done in blowing a soap bubble of radius $2\ \text{cm}$ (surface tension $0.03\ \text{N m}^{-1}$) is about <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $1.5\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}$ (b) $3.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}$ (c) $6.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}$ (d) $3.0\times10^{-2}\ \text{J}$
5. Water flows at $2\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ through a pipe of cross-section $200\ \text{cm}^{2}$ into a section of $50\ \text{cm}^{2}$. The speed in the narrow section is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0.5\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (b) $2\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (c) $4\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (d) $8\ \text{m s}^{-1}$
6. A stream of air is blown horizontally underneath one pan of a beam balance. That pan <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2078)
   (a) goes down (b) goes up (c) stays level (d) oscillates about the level
7. If the radius of a horizontal capillary tube is doubled, the rate of flow of a liquid under the same pressure difference becomes <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $2$ times (b) $4$ times (c) $8$ times (d) $16$ times
8. A block floats in water with three-quarters of its volume submerged. Its relative density is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0.25$ (b) $0.50$ (c) $0.75$ (d) $1.33$
9. The gauge pressure at a depth of $10\ \text{m}$ in water ($\rho = 1000\ \text{kg m}^{-3}$, $g = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$) is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $9.8\times10^{3}\ \text{Pa}$ (b) $9.8\times10^{4}\ \text{Pa}$ (c) $1.98\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ (d) $1.01\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$

::: note Answers — 2.3
**1.** (a) — $h\propto 1/r$, so the wider tube gives the smaller rise: $\dfrac{h_{4}}{h_{2}} = \dfrac{r_{2}}{r_{4}} = \dfrac{2}{4} = 1:2$. Option (b) inverts the ratio.
**2.** (c) — $h\propto 1/g$; weaker gravity means the column climbs six times higher.
**3.** (b) — volume doubles so $r' = 2^{1/3}r$; $v_t\propto r^{2}$ gives $v' = 2^{2/3}(0.15) = 0.24\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Option (c) wrongly assumes $v_t\propto$ volume.
**4.** (b) — a bubble has two surfaces: $W = T\times2\times4\pi r^{2} = 0.03\times2\times4\pi(0.02)^{2} = 3.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}$. Option (a) counts one surface only.
**5.** (d) — $A_1v_1 = A_2v_2$ gives $v_2 = 200\times2/50 = 8\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Option (a) divides the wrong way.
**6.** (a) — the air moving under the pan has lower pressure (Bernoulli), so the still air above pushes the pan **down**. Students who answer (b) picture the moving air "lifting" the pan.
**7.** (d) — $V/t\propto r^{4}$, so $2^{4} = 16$.
**8.** (c) — fraction submerged $=\rho_{body}/\rho_{water} = 0.75$.
**9.** (b) — $\rho gh = 1000\times9.8\times10 = 9.8\times10^{4}\ \text{Pa}$; option (d) is the atmospheric pressure, which is not part of the *gauge* value.
:::

## 2.4 First Law of Thermodynamics

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| First law | $Q = \Delta U + W$; $\Delta U$ of an ideal gas depends on temperature only |
| Work | $W = \int P\,dV$ = area under the $p$–$V$ curve; isobaric $W = P\Delta V$ |
| Isothermal | $PV$ = constant; $W = nRT\ln\dfrac{V_2}{V_1}$; $\Delta U = 0$ |
| Adiabatic | $Q = 0$; $PV^{\gamma}$ = constant; $W = \dfrac{nR(T_1-T_2)}{\gamma-1}$ |
| Isochoric | $W = 0$; $Q = \Delta U = nC_V\Delta T$; $P\propto T$ |
| Heat capacities | $C_P - C_V = R$; $\gamma = C_P/C_V$ ($\tfrac53$ monatomic, $\tfrac75$ diatomic) |
| Work for the same expansion, same initial state | isobaric $>$ isothermal $>$ adiabatic $>$ isochoric ($=0$) |

1. The internal energy of an ideal gas depends on <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2079 model)
   (a) volume only (b) pressure only (c) temperature only (d) both pressure and volume
2. A gas expands from the same initial state through the same volume change by four different processes. The work done by the gas is greatest in the <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2079 model)
   (a) isothermal process (b) isobaric process (c) adiabatic process (d) isochoric process
3. A gas in a rigid vessel is heated from $127\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$ to $227\ ^{\circ}\text{C}$. The ratio of initial to final pressure is <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2081)
   (a) $127:227$ (b) $4:5$ (c) $5:4$ (d) $1:1$
4. In an adiabatic process <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2082)
   (a) temperature stays constant (b) no heat enters or leaves the system (c) pressure stays constant (d) volume stays constant
5. A gas at constant pressure $2\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ expands from $1\ \text{L}$ to $3\ \text{L}$. The work done by the gas is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $200\ \text{J}$ (b) $400\ \text{J}$ (c) $600\ \text{J}$ (d) $4\times10^{5}\ \text{J}$
6. A system absorbs $500\ \text{J}$ of heat and does $200\ \text{J}$ of work. Its internal energy <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) rises by $700\ \text{J}$ (b) rises by $300\ \text{J}$ (c) falls by $300\ \text{J}$ (d) is unchanged
7. One mole of an ideal gas expands isothermally at $300\ \text{K}$ to double its volume. The heat absorbed is about ($R = 8.31\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$) <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $1.2\ \text{kJ}$ (b) $1.7\ \text{kJ}$ (c) $2.5\ \text{kJ}$ (d) $3.4\ \text{kJ}$
8. A monatomic ideal gas ($\gamma = \tfrac53$) is compressed adiabatically to one-eighth of its volume. The pressure becomes <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $8$ times (b) $16$ times (c) $32$ times (d) $64$ times
9. Which relation between the molar heat capacities of an ideal gas is correct? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $C_V - C_P = R$ (b) $C_P + C_V = R$ (c) $C_P - C_V = R$ (d) $C_P/C_V = R$

::: note Answers — 2.4
**1.** (c) — for an ideal gas there are no intermolecular forces, so $U$ is purely the kinetic energy, which depends on $T$ alone. Option (d) is the classic confusion with a real gas.
**2.** (b) — between the same two volumes the isobaric path lies highest on the $p$–$V$ diagram, the isochoric path has zero area. (The item is about expansion from the same state; with different end-points the ranking can change.)
**3.** (b) — $P\propto T$ at constant volume with $T$ in **kelvin**: $400:500 = 4:5$. Option (a) uses Celsius temperatures.
**4.** (b) — $Q = 0$. Temperature changes in an adiabatic process (it is the *isothermal* one that holds $T$ constant).
**5.** (b) — $W = P\Delta V = 2\times10^{5}\times2\times10^{-3} = 400\ \text{J}$. Option (d) forgets to convert litres to cubic metres.
**6.** (b) — $\Delta U = Q - W = 500 - 200 = +300\ \text{J}$. Option (a) adds instead of subtracting.
**7.** (b) — isothermal, so $Q = W = RT\ln2 = 8.31\times300\times0.693 = 1.73\ \text{kJ}$.
**8.** (c) — $P_2/P_1 = (V_1/V_2)^{\gamma} = 8^{5/3} = 32$. Option (a) treats it as isothermal ($\gamma = 1$).
**9.** (c) — Mayer's relation. $C_P$ exceeds $C_V$ because at constant pressure the gas also does expansion work.
:::

## 2.5 Second Law of Thermodynamics

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Kelvin–Planck | no engine can convert all the heat it takes from a single reservoir into work |
| Clausius | heat cannot flow by itself from a colder to a hotter body |
| Efficiency | $\eta = \dfrac{W}{Q_1} = 1-\dfrac{Q_2}{Q_1}$; Carnot $\eta = 1-\dfrac{T_2}{T_1}$ (kelvin) |
| Refrigerator | $\text{COP} = \dfrac{Q_2}{W} = \dfrac{Q_2}{Q_1-Q_2} = \dfrac{T_2}{T_1-T_2}$ |
| Otto cycle | $\eta = 1-\dfrac{1}{r^{\gamma-1}}$, $r$ = compression ratio |
| Diesel vs petrol | diesel has the higher compression ratio (about 15–22 against 6–10) |
| Entropy | $\Delta S = \dfrac{Q}{T}$ for a reversible step; an isolated system never loses entropy |

1. The efficiency of a Carnot engine working between the steam point and the ice point is about <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2082)
   (a) $10\%$ (b) $27\%$ (c) $73\%$ (d) $100\%$
2. A heat engine takes $500\ \text{J}$ from the source and rejects $400\ \text{J}$ to the sink. Its efficiency is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $80\%$ (b) $25\%$ (c) $20\%$ (d) $10\%$
3. The coefficient of performance of an ideal refrigerator working between $273\ \text{K}$ and $300\ \text{K}$ is about <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2078)
   (a) $0.1$ (b) $1.1$ (c) $10$ (d) $27$
4. The efficiency of an Otto engine with compression ratio $8$ and $\gamma = 1.4$ is about <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $43\%$ (b) $57\%$ (c) $75\%$ (d) $88\%$
5. A Carnot engine has a sink at $300\ \text{K}$ and an efficiency of $50\%$. The source temperature is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $450\ \text{K}$ (b) $600\ \text{K}$ (c) $900\ \text{K}$ (d) $150\ \text{K}$
6. The Kelvin–Planck statement of the second law says that <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) heat flows from hot to cold (b) no cyclic engine can be $100\%$ efficient when working from a single reservoir (c) energy is conserved (d) entropy of the universe is constant
7. Which of the following has the higher compression ratio? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) petrol engine (b) diesel engine (c) both the same (d) steam engine
8. The entropy of an isolated system undergoing an irreversible process <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) decreases (b) remains constant (c) increases (d) becomes zero
9. A refrigerator absorbs $200\ \text{J}$ from the cold space using $50\ \text{J}$ of work. The heat rejected to the room is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $150\ \text{J}$ (b) $250\ \text{J}$ (c) $200\ \text{J}$ (d) $4\ \text{J}$

::: note Answers — 2.5
**1.** (b) — $\eta = 1 - 273/373 = 0.268$. Option (c) is $T_2/T_1$, the *rejected* fraction.
**2.** (c) — $\eta = 1 - 400/500 = 20\%$; option (a) is $Q_2/Q_1$.
**3.** (c) — $\text{COP} = 273/(300-273) = 10.1$. The refrigerator works between a cold space at the ice point and the room; a value above 1 is normal for a refrigerator, unlike an engine.
**4.** (b) — $\eta = 1 - 8^{-0.4} = 1 - 0.435 = 0.565$.
**5.** (b) — $0.5 = 1 - 300/T_1$ gives $T_1 = 600\ \text{K}$.
**6.** (b) — this is the statement itself; (a) is Clausius's statement in weakened form, (c) is the first law.
**7.** (b) — a diesel engine compresses air alone, so it can run at a high ratio without pre-ignition; a petrol engine compresses fuel vapour and would knock.
**8.** (c) — the direction of spontaneous change is the direction in which entropy of the isolated system rises.
**9.** (b) — energy balance: $Q_1 = Q_2 + W = 200 + 50 = 250\ \text{J}$. Option (a) subtracts.
:::

## 2.6 Wave motion

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Wave speed | $v = f\lambda = \omega/k$ |
| Travelling wave along $+x$ | $y = A\sin(\omega t - kx)$, $k = 2\pi/\lambda$ |
| Phase difference | $\Delta\phi = \dfrac{2\pi}{\lambda}\Delta x$ |
| Two consecutive particles in the same phase | $\lambda$ apart |

## 2.24 Radioactivity and nuclear reaction

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Decay law | $N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$; $A = \lambda N$ |
| Half-life; mean life | $T_{1/2} = \dfrac{0.693}{\lambda}$; $\tau = \dfrac{1}{\lambda} = 1.44\,T_{1/2}$ |

| Units | becquerel ($1\ \text{s}^{-1}$); curie $= 3.7\times10^{10}\ \text{Bq}$ |

1. The percentage of the original radioactive atoms remaining after $4$ half-lives is <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2079 model)

   (a) s (b) $\text{s}^{-1}$ (c) Bq (d) $\text{J s}^{-1}$

   (a) fluorescence (b) ionization of the gas inside it (c) heating of its wall (d) chemical change

   (a) a helium nucleus (b) a fast electron emitted from the nucleus (c) an electromagnetic wave (d) a proton

   (a) $\alpha$ (b) $\beta$ (c) $\gamma$ (d) cathode rays

::: note Answers — 2.24
**1.** (b) — $(\tfrac12)^{4} = 1/16 = 6.25\%$. Option (d) is four times $5\%$, an arithmetic rather than geometric decay.
**2.** (c) — $\tau = T_{1/2}/0.693 = 72\ \text{years}$. Option (b) confuses mean life with half-life.
**3.** (a) — $\lambda = 0.693/(5700\times3.156\times10^{7}) = 3.85\times10^{-12}\ \text{s}^{-1}$; $A = \lambda N = 3.9\times10^{10}\ \text{Bq}$. The year must be converted to seconds.

## 2.25 Recent trends in physics

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Focus (hypocentre) | the point inside the Earth where the rupture begins and seismic waves start |
| Epicentre | the point on the surface vertically above the focus |
| P-wave | longitudinal, fastest, travels through solids and liquids |
| S-wave | transverse, slower, solids only |

| Gravitational waves | ripples of space-time; first detected by LIGO in 2015 |
| Higgs boson | discovered at CERN in 2012; linked to the origin of mass |

1. The point within the Earth where seismic waves start is called the <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2078, 2082, 2082 model)
   (a) epicentre (b) focus (hypocentre) (c) seismograph (d) fault line
2. The point on the Earth's surface directly above the focus is the <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) focus (b) epicentre (c) node (d) antinode
3. A P-wave passes from solid rock into a liquid. Its speed <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2081)
   (a) increases (b) decreases (c) is unchanged (d) becomes zero and the wave stops
4. Which seismic wave cannot travel through a liquid? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) P-wave (b) S-wave (c) sound wave (d) pressure wave
5. What causes earthquakes? <span class="marks">[1]</span> (NEB 2079 model)
   (a) the flow of magma only (b) the rubbing and sudden slipping of the Earth's plates (c) tsunamis (d) the Moon's pull
6. The most destructive seismic waves near the epicentre are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) P-waves (b) S-waves (c) surface waves (Rayleigh and Love) (d) sound waves
7. Love waves are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) surface waves with horizontal shear motion (b) longitudinal body waves (c) electromagnetic waves (d) deep-Earth waves
8. Gravitational waves were first directly detected by <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) the Hubble telescope (b) LIGO (c) the Large Hadron Collider (d) a seismograph in Kathmandu
9. The Higgs boson is associated with <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) gravity (b) the mass of elementary particles (c) electric charge (d) radioactivity

::: note Answers — 2.25
**1.** (b) — the focus is underground; the epicentre is its surface projection. Mixing the two is the commonest error.
**2.** (b) — the epicentre is where the damage is usually greatest.
**3.** (b) — a liquid has a much smaller rigidity and is more compressible than solid rock, so the wave slows.
**4.** (b) — a liquid has no rigidity (shear modulus $=0$), so a shear wave cannot propagate in it; this is how seismologists detected the liquid outer core.
**5.** (b) — stress builds up along plate boundaries and is released as slip. Magma flow causes some volcanic tremors but not most earthquakes.
**6.** (c) — they arrive last but carry the most energy and shake the ground horizontally and in rolling motion.
**7.** (a) — Love waves shear the ground side to side; Rayleigh waves roll it in an ellipse.
**8.** (b) — the two LIGO detectors in the USA recorded the merger of two black holes on 14 September 2015.
**9.** (b) — the Higgs field gives elementary particles their mass; its quantum is the Higgs boson.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Group A of the NEB Grade 12 Physics paper is eleven one-mark items drawn from across all twenty-five units; no unit is safe to skip, which is why this chapter gives every unit its own facts table, its own MCQs and its own reasoned key.
- Recent papers repeat a small core of Group A facts: the angular momentum product $I\omega$; energy conservation in SHM; capillary rise and raindrop terminal velocity ($v_t\propto r^{2}$); the work ranking of thermodynamic processes; Carnot efficiency in kelvin; node–antinode spacing; Laplace's adiabatic correction; thermocouple $\theta_i = 2\theta_n-\theta_c$; Lenz's law and energy; the peak of the $220\ \text{V}$ supply ($311\ \text{V}$); the focus of an earthquake.
- Most lost marks are unit slips, not physics slips: $\text{cm}^{2}$ left unconverted, Celsius used in gas laws, kelvin under a square root, $\text{eV}$ and joule mixed, the $\tfrac12$ omitted in $\tfrac12LI^{2}$ and $\tfrac12I\omega^{2}$.
- Formula pairs that are confused: closed-pipe $v/4L$ against open-pipe $v/2L$; observer-moving against source-moving Doppler; Malus' $\cos^{2}\theta$ against $\cos\theta$; $B$ at the centre of a coil ($\mu_0NI/2r$) against a straight wire ($\mu_0I/2\pi d$); $I^{2}Rt$ against $IRt$.
- Graph items are now common: read the slope of $l$–$T^{2}$ ($4\pi^{2}/g$), of $K_{max}$–$f$ ($h$), the area of a $p$–$V$ loop (work) and of a hysteresis loop (energy loss per cycle).
- Year tags mark questions we actually saw; untagged items are practice built to the same pattern.
