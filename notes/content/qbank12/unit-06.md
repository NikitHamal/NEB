Group B (eight questions of five marks) and Group C (three questions of eight marks) together carry **64 of the 75 marks**, and they are where a Chemistry result is decided. This chapter takes the twenty-one units of the CDC syllabus in order and, for each, sets short-answer questions in the real five-mark splits (1+4, 2+3, 1+1+3, 1+1+1+2) and long-answer questions in the eight-mark splits (2+2+2+2, 3+3+2, 5+3). **Every question is followed by a complete model answer**, worded and laid out the way a full-mark answer is written in the answer book. Numericals show the data, the formula, the substitution and the answer with its unit.

::: caution Year tags and re-set data

## 6.1 Volumetric Analysis

CDC allots **8 teaching hours**. The examinable core is equivalent weight, the concentration units, primary and secondary standards, the normality equation, and the redox and acid–base titrations with their calculations. The unit has supplied one full Group B or Group C question in almost every sitting.

**Short answer questions [5 marks each]**

1. (a) Write any two differences between a primary standard and a secondary standard substance. [2] (b) Give two examples of each. [2] (c) Why is oxalic acid crystal preferred to NaOH for making a standard solution? [1] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2083)
2. 3.15 g of oxalic acid crystals, H₂C₂O₄·2H₂O, are dissolved in water to make 500 mL of solution. (a) Find the normality of the acid. [1] (b) The whole 500 mL is titrated with a KMnO₄ solution (equivalent weight 31.5) and becomes permanently pink after 400 mL is added. What mass of KMnO₄ is present in one litre of that solution? [2] (c) Why is the solution warmed and acidified with dilute H₂SO₄ before titration? [2] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2083; the volume of acid titrated is taken as the whole 500 mL)
3. 2.16 g of Na₂CO₃ is added to 400 mL of decinormal H₂SO₄. (a) How is completion of the neutralisation detected in the titration of Na₂CO₃ against H₂SO₄? [1] (b) Is Na₂CO₃ a primary standard? Why? [1] (c) Calculate the further volume of decinormal H₂SO₄ needed to neutralise the carbonate fully. [3] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2078 model)
4. An aqueous solution of a dibasic acid contains 17.7 g of the acid per litre (molar mass 118 g mol⁻¹) and its density is 1.0077 g mL⁻¹. Calculate (a) the molarity [2] (b) the molality [2] (c) the normality [1]. <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2079)
5. (a) A bottle of HCl is labelled 2 M. What does this mean? [1] (b) How does molarity differ from molality? [1] (c) State the significance of the normality factor in preparing a standard solution. [1] (d) Liquor ammonia is 25 % (w/w) NH₃ and has specific gravity 0.91. Find its molarity. [2] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2079 model)
6. (a) Define a primary standard substance. [1] (b) Why is a titration between a weak acid and a weak base not commonly done? [1] (c) 10 mL of N/10 oxalic acid (factor f = 1.01) neutralises 10 mL of x % (w/v) NaOH solution. Find x. [3] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB model set B)

::: note Model answers — short (1–3)
**1.** (a)

| | Primary standard | Secondary standard |
|---|---|---|
| Preparation | prepared by weighing the pure substance directly | its strength is found by titrating against a primary standard |
| Purity and stability | very pure, stable in air, not hygroscopic, exact formula | not pure or not stable; absorbs CO₂, moisture or is volatile |
| Molar mass | high, so the weighing error is small | may be low or variable |

(b) Primary: oxalic acid crystals, anhydrous Na₂CO₃ (also K₂Cr₂O₇). Secondary: NaOH, HCl (also KMnO₄, Na₂S₂O₃).
(c) NaOH is hygroscopic and absorbs CO₂, so the weighed mass is not the true mass of NaOH; oxalic acid crystals have a fixed composition, are stable and need no correction.

**2.** (a) Equivalent weight of oxalic acid crystals = 126/2 = 63.
$$ N = \frac{3.15/63}{0.5} = \frac{0.05}{0.5} = 0.10\ \text{N} $$
(b) Apply the normality equation at the end point, N₁V₁ = N₂V₂, with N₁ = 0.10 N, V₁ = 500 mL, V₂ = 400 mL:
$$ N_{2} = \frac{0.10\times500}{400} = 0.125\ \text{N} $$
Mass per litre = normality × equivalent weight = 0.125 × 31.5 = **3.94 g L⁻¹**.
(c) Dilute H₂SO₄ supplies the H⁺ that the half-reaction MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O needs; without it the brown MnO₂ forms. HCl would be oxidised and HNO₃ would itself oxidise, so neither can be used. The mixture is warmed to 60–70 °C because the reaction between MnO₄⁻ and oxalate is slow at room temperature; once Mn²⁺ forms it catalyses the reaction.

**3.** (a) In the titration of Na₂CO₃ against H₂SO₄ **methyl orange** is used: it turns from yellow to orange-pink at the end point, when all the carbonate has become H₂CO₃.
(b) Yes. Anhydrous Na₂CO₃ is pure, stable, non-hygroscopic when dry and has a known formula, so a standard solution can be made by direct weighing.
(c) Equivalent weight of Na₂CO₃ = 106/2 = 53.
$$ \text{equivalents of Na}_{2}\text{CO}_{3} = \frac{2.16}{53} = 0.04075 = 40.75\ \text{meq} $$
$$ \text{meq of acid present} = 400\times0.1 = 40.0 $$
$$ \text{excess carbonate} = 40.75-40.0 = 0.75\ \text{meq} $$
$$ V = \frac{0.75}{0.1} = 7.5\ \text{mL of decinormal H}_{2}\text{SO}_{4} $$

**4.** (a) Molarity = (17.7/118) mol per litre = **0.15 M**.
(b) Mass of 1 L of solution = 1000 × 1.0077 = 1007.7 g. Mass of solvent = 1007.7 − 17.7 = 990.0 g = 0.990 kg.
$$ m = \frac{0.15}{0.990} = 0.152\ \text{mol kg}^{-1} $$
(c) A dibasic acid has two replaceable H, so its n-factor is 2: N = 0.15 × 2 = **0.30 N**.
:::

::: note Model answers — short (5–6)
**5.** (a) One litre of the solution contains 2 mol (73 g) of HCl.
(b) Molarity is moles of solute per litre of **solution**, and it changes with temperature because the volume changes. Molality is moles of solute per kilogram of **solvent**, and it does not change with temperature because mass does not.
(c) The normality factor is the ratio of the actual strength of the solution to the strength it was meant to have. It lets the student use a solution that is not exactly as planned: the volume is simply multiplied by the factor, so there is no need to remake the solution.
(d) Take 1 L of liquor ammonia. Mass = 1000 × 0.91 = 910 g. Mass of NH₃ = 0.25 × 910 = 227.5 g. Moles = 227.5/17 = 13.4.
$$ M = \frac{10\times0.91\times25}{17} = 13.4\ \text{mol L}^{-1} $$

**6.** (a) A primary standard is a substance pure enough, and stable enough, that its standard solution can be made by dissolving an accurately weighed mass in a known volume, with no need to standardise it afterwards.
(b) In a weak acid–weak base titration the pH changes gradually near the equivalence point instead of jumping, so no indicator gives a sharp colour change and the end point cannot be seen.
(c) True normality of oxalic acid = 0.1 × 1.01 = 0.101 N. At the end point N₁V₁ = N₂V₂:
$$ N_{2} = \frac{0.101\times10}{10} = 0.101\ \text{N} $$
Strength of NaOH = 0.101 × 40 = 4.04 g L⁻¹ = 0.404 g per 100 mL, so **x = 0.404 %**.
:::

**Long answer questions [8 marks each]**

1. (a) Deduce the normality equation N₁V₁ = N₂V₂. [3] (b) A decinormal oxalic acid solution was titrated with KMnO₄ solution. Burette readings for 10 mL of the acid: (1) 0.0 → 11.5 mL, (2) 0.0 → 11.0 mL, (3) 0.0 → 11.0 mL. (i) Name the titration. [1] (ii) Find the equivalent weight of KMnO₄ in acidic medium. [1] (iii) Find the normality of the KMnO₄. [1] (iv) Why is dilute H₂SO₄ added to the flask? [1] (v) Identify the titrant and the titrand. [1] <span class="marks">[8]</span> (NEB 2082)
2. Sodium hydroxide solution can be standardised by titration against a standard solution of oxalic acid. (a) Is the NaOH solution a primary standard? Why? [2] (b) Differentiate between the equivalence point and the end point. [2] (c) 1.26 g of oxalic acid crystals is dissolved to make 250 mL of solution, and 25.0 mL of it neutralises 20.0 mL of the NaOH solution. Find the normality and the strength in g L⁻¹ of the NaOH. [4] <span class="marks">[8]</span> (NEB 2081; data re-set)

::: note Model answers — long
**1.** (a) **Law of equivalence:** at the end point, equal numbers of gram equivalents of the two reactants have reacted. For a solution of normality N and volume V (in mL), the number of milli-equivalents is N × V.
$$ \text{meq of solution 1} = \text{meq of solution 2} $$
$$ N_{1}V_{1} = N_{2}V_{2} $$
(b) (i) **Redox (permanganate) titration** of oxalic acid against KMnO₄.
(ii) MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O, so the n-factor is 5 and E = 158/5 = **31.6**.
(iii) The first reading (11.5 mL) is a rough value; the concordant readings are 11.0 mL and 11.0 mL, so the mean is 11.0 mL.
$$ N_{\text{KMnO}_{4}} = \frac{N_{\text{oxalic}}V_{\text{oxalic}}}{V_{\text{KMnO}_{4}}} = \frac{0.1\times10}{11.0} = 0.091\ \text{N} $$
(iv) Dilute H₂SO₄ gives the H⁺ the permanganate half-reaction needs and keeps the medium acidic, so that Mn²⁺ and not brown MnO₂ is the product. Neither HCl nor HNO₃ can be used, because the first is oxidised by MnO₄⁻ and the second is itself an oxidant.
(v) **Titrant** (in the burette): KMnO₄ solution. **Titrand** (in the conical flask): oxalic acid solution. KMnO₄ acts as its own indicator, so no other indicator is added.

**2.** (a) **No.** NaOH is hygroscopic and absorbs CO₂ from air, so its mass cannot be relied on when weighed out; its solution must be standardised against a primary standard, which makes NaOH a secondary standard.
(b) The **equivalence point** is the theoretical stage at which the reactants have combined in exactly equivalent amounts. The **end point** is the stage at which the indicator changes colour and the titration is stopped. A well-chosen indicator makes the two almost coincide, but they are not identical and the small difference is the titration error.
(c) Equivalent weight of oxalic acid crystals = 63.
$$ N_{\text{acid}} = \frac{1.26/63}{0.25} = \frac{0.02}{0.25} = 0.08\ \text{N} $$
$$ N_{\text{NaOH}} = \frac{0.08\times25.0}{20.0} = 0.10\ \text{N} $$
$$ \text{Strength} = 0.10\times40 = 4.0\ \text{g L}^{-1} $$
:::

**Most repeated in Volumetric Analysis:** primary and secondary standards (2083, model set B, 2081, 2079); the KMnO₄–oxalic acid titration and the equivalent weight of KMnO₄ (2082, 2079, 2083); the normality equation (2082); molarity, molality and normality conversions (2079, 2079 model).

Write the **n-factor from the reaction**, not from the formula: KMnO₄ is 158/5 in acid, and H₃PO₃ is 82/2 whatever the number of hydrogens. In every titration answer name the titrant and the titrand, state the indicator and its colour change, and state the equation N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ before substituting. ## 6.2 Ionic Equilibrium

CDC allots **10 teaching hours**, the second largest of the general and physical units. The examiner draws on it every year: a pH calculation, Ostwald's dilution law, and a solubility product or precipitation question.

**Short answer questions [5 marks each]**

1. The ionic product of water at 25 °C is 1 × 10⁻¹⁴. (a) Define the ionic product of water. [1] (b) Deduce the relation pH + pOH = 14. [1] (c) Calculate [OH⁻] of 0.01 M HCl at 25 °C. [2] (d) What is the effect of temperature on the ionic product of water? [1] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2079)
2. (a) Define the pH of a solution. [1] (b) Calculate the hydroxyl-ion concentration of a solution whose pH is 4.7. [2] (c) Find the mass of NaOH that would give this concentration of OH⁻ in one litre. [2] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2081)
3. (a) Differentiate between the Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis concepts of bases. [2] (b) Give one example of a Lewis acid and one of a Lewis base. [1] (c) Identify the conjugate acid–base pairs in NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. [2] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2082 for (a))
4. Predict whether a precipitate will form on mixing 20 mL of 1.0 × 10⁻³ M NaCl solution with 80 mL of 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ M AgNO₃ solution. Ksp of AgCl = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰. <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2083; data re-set)
5. What is a buffer solution? Derive the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation for an acidic buffer, and calculate the pH of a buffer containing 0.2 M ethanoic acid and 0.1 M sodium ethanoate (pKa = 4.74). <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — short (1–3)
**1.** (a) The **ionic product of water** is the product of the molar concentrations of H⁺ and OH⁻ ions in pure water (or in any dilute aqueous solution) at a given temperature: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25 °C.
(b) Take logarithms of Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] and change sign:
$$ -\log K_{w} = -\log[\text{H}^{+}]-\log[\text{OH}^{-}] $$
$$ \text{p}K_{w} = \text{pH}+\text{pOH} = 14 $$
(c) HCl is a strong acid, so [H⁺] = 0.01 M.
$$ [\text{OH}^{-}] = \frac{K_{w}}{[\text{H}^{+}]} = \frac{10^{-14}}{10^{-2}} = 1\times10^{-12}\ \text{mol L}^{-1} $$
(d) The ionisation of water is endothermic, so raising the temperature shifts the equilibrium to the right and **Kw increases** (about 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 50 °C); the pH of pure water falls below 7 although it stays neutral.

**2.** (a) The **pH** of a solution is the negative logarithm to the base 10 of its hydrogen-ion concentration, pH = −log[H⁺].
(b) pOH = 14 − 4.7 = 9.3.
$$ [\text{OH}^{-}] = 10^{-9.3} = 5.0\times10^{-10}\ \text{mol L}^{-1} $$
(c) NaOH is a strong base, so 1 mol NaOH gives 1 mol OH⁻ (the water's own OH⁻ is ignored, as the question intends).
$$ \text{mass} = 5.0\times10^{-10}\times40 = 2.0\times10^{-8}\ \text{g} $$

**3.** (a)

| | Brønsted–Lowry base | Lewis base |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | a proton (H⁺) acceptor | an electron-pair donor |
| Needs a proton? | yes | no — it may react with any electron-pair acceptor |
| Example | NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ | NH₃ donating to BF₃ or Ag⁺ |

Every B–L base is a Lewis base, but the Lewis concept is wider: it includes reactions that involve no proton.
(b) Lewis acid: BF₃ (or AlCl₃, H⁺). Lewis base: NH₃ (or OH⁻, H₂O).
(c) NH₃ (base) / NH₄⁺ (its conjugate acid); H₂O (acid) / OH⁻ (its conjugate base).

**4.** After mixing, the total volume is 100 mL and each concentration is diluted.
$$ [\text{Cl}^{-}] = \frac{20\times1.0\times10^{-3}}{100} = 2.0\times10^{-4}\ \text{M} $$
$$ [\text{Ag}^{+}] = \frac{80\times1.0\times10^{-4}}{100} = 8.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{M} $$
$$ Q_{sp} = [\text{Ag}^{+}][\text{Cl}^{-}] = 2.0\times10^{-4}\times8.0\times10^{-5} = 1.6\times10^{-8} $$
Since Qsp (1.6 × 10⁻⁸) is **greater than** Ksp (1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰), the solution is supersaturated and **AgCl precipitates**.

**5.** A **buffer solution** resists a change in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added. An acidic buffer is a weak acid with its salt, for example CH₃COOH + CH₃COONa.
For HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA], so
$$ [\text{H}^{+}] = K_{a}\frac{[\text{HA}]}{[\text{A}^{-}]} $$
Take −log of both sides. The salt is fully ionised, so [A⁻] ≈ [salt]; the acid is weakly ionised and suppressed by the common ion, so [HA] ≈ [acid]:
$$ \text{pH} = \text{p}K_{a}+\log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]} $$
$$ \text{pH} = 4.74+\log\frac{0.1}{0.2} = 4.74-0.30 = 4.44 $$
:::

**Long answer questions [8 marks each]**

1. (a) What is a weak electrolyte? [1] (b) Derive Ostwald's dilution law and hence α = √(Ka/C). [3] (c) State two limitations of the law, and say whether strong electrolytes obey it. [2] (d) A 0.1 M solution of ethanoic acid is 1.34 % ionised. Calculate its dissociation constant. [2] <span class="marks">[8]</span> (NEB 2083, 2079 model, model set A)
2. (a) Write the applications of the solubility product principle and the common ion effect in qualitative salt analysis. [3] (b) Differentiate between the Brønsted–Lowry and Lewis concepts of bases. [2] (c) Calculate the degree of ionisation, the [H⁺] and the pH of 0.01 M HCN (Ka = 4.8 × 10⁻¹⁰). [3] <span class="marks">[8]</span> (NEB 2082)
3. (a) Differentiate between the ionic product and the solubility product. [2] (b) State one limitation of Ostwald's dilution law. [1] (c) Why is an aqueous solution of sodium chloride neutral? [1] (d) Ksp of BaSO₄ is 1 × 10⁻¹⁰. Will a precipitate form when equal volumes of (i) 2 × 10⁻³ M BaCl₂ and 2 × 10⁻⁴ M Na₂SO₄ are mixed? (ii) 2 × 10⁻⁸ M BaCl₂ and 2 × 10⁻³ M Na₂SO₄ are mixed? [2+2] <span class="marks">[8]</span> (NEB model set B)
4. (a) Is Zn(OH)₂ precipitated when 100 mL of a solution containing 0.22 g NaOH is added to 1 L of saturated Zn(OH)₂ solution (Ksp = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁴)? Give reasons. [5] (b) A solution of KOH has pH 8. It is diluted 1000 times. What is the pH of the diluted solution? [3] <span class="marks">[8]</span> (NEB 2078 model)

::: note Model answers — long (1–2)
**1.** (a) A **weak electrolyte** is a substance that ionises only partly in solution, so that ions and unionised molecules are in equilibrium (ethanoic acid, NH₄OH).
(b) Let 1 mol of weak acid HA be dissolved in V litres, and let α be the degree of ionisation.

| | HA | ⇌ H⁺ | + A⁻ |
|---|---|---|---|
| At start | 1 | 0 | 0 |
| At equilibrium | 1 − α | α | α |

Concentrations are these amounts divided by V, where C = 1/V:
$$ K_{a} = \frac{(\alpha/V)(\alpha/V)}{(1-\alpha)/V} = \frac{\alpha^{2}}{(1-\alpha)V} = \frac{C\alpha^{2}}{1-\alpha} $$
This is **Ostwald's dilution law**. For a weak electrolyte α is very small, so 1 − α ≈ 1:
$$ K_{a} = C\alpha^{2}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \alpha = \sqrt{\frac{K_{a}}{C}} $$
Since α ∝ 1/√C, the degree of ionisation **increases on dilution**. For a weak base replace Ka by Kb.
(c) **Limitations:** (i) it fails for strong electrolytes, which are completely ionised, so the "constant" changes with concentration; (ii) it fails for weak electrolytes at high concentration (inter-ionic attractions); (iii) it does not apply without modification when a common ion is present. **Strong electrolytes do not obey it.**
(d) α = 1.34 % = 0.0134 and C = 0.1 M.
$$ K_{a} = \frac{C\alpha^{2}}{1-\alpha} = \frac{0.1\times(0.0134)^{2}}{1-0.0134} $$
$$ K_{a} = \frac{1.80\times10^{-5}}{0.987} = 1.8\times10^{-5} $$

**2.** (a) **Solubility product.** In group II the sulphides of Pb²⁺, Cu²⁺, Cd²⁺ and others have very small Ksp, so even the tiny [S²⁻] from H₂S in acid is enough to precipitate them, while the more soluble ZnS and NiS (larger Ksp) stay in solution. In group IV, NH₄OH/NH₄Cl is added so that [S²⁻] is large enough to exceed Ksp of ZnS, NiS, CoS and MnS. A precipitate forms only when the ionic product exceeds Ksp.
**Common ion effect.** (i) Dilute HCl is added in group II: H⁺ is the common ion for H₂S ⇌ 2H⁺ + S²⁻, so [S²⁻] is kept low and ZnS and NiS do not precipitate. (ii) In group III, NH₄Cl is added with NH₄OH: the common ion NH₄⁺ suppresses the ionisation of NH₄OH, so [OH⁻] is small and only Fe(OH)₃, Al(OH)₃ and Cr(OH)₃ (very small Ksp) precipitate, not Mg(OH)₂. (iii) In group V, (NH₄)₂CO₃ supplies CO₃²⁻ for Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺.
(b) See the table in short answer 3 above: a Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton; a Lewis base donates an electron pair, and is not limited to reactions involving H⁺.
(c) α = √(Ka/C) = √(4.8 × 10⁻¹⁰/0.01) = √(4.8 × 10⁻⁸) = **2.2 × 10⁻⁴**.
[H⁺] = Cα = 0.01 × 2.19 × 10⁻⁴ = **2.2 × 10⁻⁶ M**, and pH = −log(2.19 × 10⁻⁶) = **5.66**.
:::

::: note Model answers — long (3–4)
**3.** (a)

| | Ionic product | Solubility product |
|---|---|---|
| Applies to | any solution, saturated or not | a saturated solution only, at equilibrium |
| Value | can have any value | constant at a fixed temperature |
| Use | compared with Ksp to decide precipitation | defines the solubility of a sparingly soluble salt |

(b) It fails for strong electrolytes (they are completely ionised).
(c) NaCl is the salt of a strong acid (HCl) and a strong base (NaOH). Neither Na⁺ nor Cl⁻ reacts with water, so [H⁺] = [OH⁻] and the solution is neutral.
(d) (i) After mixing equal volumes each concentration is halved: [Ba²⁺] = 1 × 10⁻³ M and [SO₄²⁻] = 1 × 10⁻⁴ M.
$$ Q_{sp} = 1\times10^{-3}\times1\times10^{-4} = 1\times10^{-7} $$
1 × 10⁻⁷ > 1 × 10⁻¹⁰, so **BaSO₄ precipitates**.
(ii) [Ba²⁺] = 1 × 10⁻⁸ M and [SO₄²⁻] = 1 × 10⁻³ M.
$$ Q_{sp} = 1\times10^{-8}\times1\times10^{-3} = 1\times10^{-11} $$
1 × 10⁻¹¹ < 1 × 10⁻¹⁰, so **no precipitate** forms.

**4.** (a) **Saturated Zn(OH)₂.** Zn(OH)₂ ⇌ Zn²⁺ + 2OH⁻, so Ksp = (s)(2s)² = 4s³.
$$ s = \left(\frac{1.8\times10^{-14}}{4}\right)^{1/3} = 1.65\times10^{-5}\ \text{mol L}^{-1} $$
So in the saturated solution [Zn²⁺] = 1.65 × 10⁻⁵ M and [OH⁻] = 3.30 × 10⁻⁵ M.
**Added alkali.** 0.22 g NaOH = 0.22/40 = 5.5 × 10⁻³ mol of OH⁻. After mixing, the volume is 1.1 L.
$$ [\text{OH}^{-}] = \frac{5.5\times10^{-3}+3.3\times10^{-5}}{1.1} = 5.03\times10^{-3}\ \text{M} $$
$$ [\text{Zn}^{2+}] = \frac{1.65\times10^{-5}}{1.1} = 1.50\times10^{-5}\ \text{M} $$
$$ Q_{sp} = [\text{Zn}^{2+}][\text{OH}^{-}]^{2} = 1.50\times10^{-5}\times(5.03\times10^{-3})^{2} = 3.8\times10^{-10} $$
Since 3.8 × 10⁻¹⁰ **exceeds** Ksp = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁴, **Zn(OH)₂ is precipitated**. The added OH⁻ is a common ion, which has raised the ionic product far beyond the solubility product.
(b) pH 8 means pOH = 6, so [OH⁻] = 10⁻⁶ M. A 1000-fold dilution would give 10⁻⁹ M, but that is far less than the 10⁻⁷ M OH⁻ that pure water already supplies, so water dominates.
$$ [\text{OH}^{-}] = \frac{10^{-9}+\sqrt{10^{-18}+4\times10^{-14}}}{2} = 1.005\times10^{-7}\ \text{M} $$
pOH = 6.998, so **pH = 7.002**, just above 7: a base, however much diluted, cannot give an acidic solution.
:::

## 6.3 Chemical Kinetics

CDC allots **7 teaching hours**. A kinetics numerical has appeared in Group B or Group C in every paper from 2078 to 2083: determining the order from a table of initial rates, finding k with its unit, and using the first-order integrated equation.

**Short answer questions [5 marks each]**

1. Define the instantaneous rate of reaction. Write the rate expressions for 2N₂O₅(g) → 4NO₂(g) + O₂(g). If 2.24 L of O₂ at NTP is produced in 30 minutes, find the rate of disappearance of N₂O₅. <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2082)

3. (a) Derive the integrated equation for a first-order reaction, k = (2.303/t) log[a/(a − x)], and show that t½ is independent of the initial concentration. [3] (b) A first-order reaction is 50 % complete in 10 s. Calculate the time for 90 % completion. [2] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2078 model)
4. (a) Define half-life. [1] (b) Deduce that for a zero-order reaction the half-life is directly proportional to the initial concentration. [2] (c) The rate constant of a zero-order reaction is 0.010 mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹. Find t½ when the initial concentration is 0.50 mol L⁻¹. [2] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2081 for (a) and (b))

[1] (c) By how many times does the rate increase if the concentrations of both A and B are doubled? [2] (d) What is the effect on the rate of raising the temperature by 20 °C? [1] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB model set A)

::: note Model answers — short (1–3)
**1.** The **instantaneous rate** is the rate of the reaction at one particular moment: the slope of the tangent to the concentration–time curve at that time, rate = ±d[C]/dt as Δt → 0.
For 2N₂O₅ → 4NO₂ + O₂ each rate is divided by the stoichiometric coefficient:
$$ \text{rate} = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{d[\text{N}_{2}\text{O}_{5}]}{dt} = +\frac{1}{4}\frac{d[\text{NO}_{2}]}{dt} = +\frac{d[\text{O}_{2}]}{dt} $$
Moles of O₂ formed = 2.24/22.4 = 0.10 mol in 30 min. From the equation 1 mol O₂ is formed when 2 mol N₂O₅ react, so N₂O₅ used = 0.20 mol.
$$ -\frac{\Delta n(\text{N}_{2}\text{O}_{5})}{\Delta t} = \frac{0.20\ \text{mol}}{30\ \text{min}} = 6.7\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol min}^{-1}\ (1.1\times10^{-4}\ \text{mol s}^{-1}) $$
Dividing by the volume of the vessel gives the rate in mol L⁻¹ min⁻¹.

**2.** (a) The **rate law** is the expression that relates the rate of a reaction to the molar concentrations of the reactants, each raised to the power that is found by experiment: rate = k[P]ᵐ[Q]ⁿ.
(b) The powers m and n are **not** the stoichiometric coefficients; they depend on the mechanism, in particular on the slowest step, and so can only be found by measuring how the rate changes when the concentrations are changed.
(c) The **rate constant** k is the rate of the reaction when every reactant concentration is 1 mol L⁻¹. It depends on temperature and catalyst, not on concentration.
(d)

| | Order | Molecularity |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | sum of the powers in the experimental rate law | number of molecules colliding in one elementary step |
| Value | zero, fractional, whole or negative | always a whole number: 1, 2 or 3 |
| Applies to | the overall reaction | a single elementary step |

**3.** (a) For A → products, let the initial concentration be a and let x have reacted in time t. The rate is proportional to the concentration left:
$$ \frac{dx}{dt} = k(a-x) $$
$$ \int\frac{dx}{a-x} = k\int dt\;\Rightarrow\;-\ln(a-x) = kt+I $$
At t = 0, x = 0, so I = −ln a.
$$ kt = \ln\frac{a}{a-x}\;\Rightarrow\;k = \frac{2.303}{t}\log\frac{a}{a-x} $$
At t = t½, x = a/2, so a − x = a/2:
$$ t_{1/2} = \frac{2.303}{k}\log 2 = \frac{0.693}{k} $$
The initial concentration a has cancelled, so **t½ is independent of a**.
(b) k = 0.693/10 = 0.0693 s⁻¹. For 90 % completion, a − x = 0.1a:
$$ t = \frac{2.303}{0.0693}\log\frac{a}{0.1a} = \frac{2.303}{0.0693}\times1 = 33.2\ \text{s} $$
:::

::: note Model answers — short (4–5)
**4.** (a) The **half-life** is the time taken for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half of its initial value.
(b) For zero order, rate = k[A]⁰ = k, so −d[A]/dt = k. Integrating: a − [A] = kt. At t = t½, [A] = a/2:
$$ a-\frac{a}{2} = kt_{1/2}\;\Rightarrow\;t_{1/2} = \frac{a}{2k} $$
so t½ ∝ a, the initial concentration.
(c)
$$ t_{1/2} = \frac{0.50}{2\times0.010} = 25\ \text{s} $$

**5.** (a) The **order** is the sum of the powers to which the concentrations are raised in the experimental rate law.
(b) rate = k[A]¹[B]⁰ = k[A].
(c) Only A appears in the rate law. Doubling [A] doubles the rate; doubling [B] has no effect. The rate increases **2 times**.
(d) The rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise, so a 20 °C rise multiplies it by 2² = **4 times**.
:::

## 6.4 Thermodynamics

CDC allots **8 teaching hours**. Enthalpy calculations from Hess's law (formation and combustion data) are an annual Group B or Group C item, and the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation appears in the objective section and in numerical form.

**Short answer questions [5 marks each]**

1. (a) Define Hess's law of constant heat summation. [1] (b) What is meant by the enthalpy of a reaction? [1] (c) The standard enthalpies of formation of H₂O₂(l) and H₂O(l) are −188 and −286 kJ mol⁻¹. Find ΔH for 2H₂O₂(l) → 2H₂O(l) + O₂(g). [3] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2079)
2. Define the standard heat of formation. Calculate the heat of formation of acetic acid from: C(s) + O₂ → CO₂, ΔH = −99 kcal mol⁻¹; H₂(g) + ½O₂ → H₂O(l), ΔH = −68 kcal mol⁻¹; CH₃COOH(l) + 2O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O(l), ΔH = −208 kcal mol⁻¹. [1+4] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2082)
3. The Gibbs equation is ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. (a) Define the Gibbs free energy change. [1] (b) Predict the spontaneity when ΔH and ΔS are both positive. [2] (c) For a reaction ΔH = 50 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = 300 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Is it spontaneous at 15 °C? [2] <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB model set B)
4. Define the heat of formation. Calculate the heat of formation of ethyl alcohol, given the heats of combustion: carbon −94 kcal, hydrogen −68.5 kcal, ethyl alcohol −330 kcal (all per mole). <span class="marks">[5]</span> (NEB 2078 model, model set A)

::: note Model answers — short
**1.** (a) **Hess's law:** the total enthalpy change of a reaction is the same whether it takes place in one step or in several steps, provided the initial and final conditions are the same; it depends only on the initial and final states.
(b) The **enthalpy of a reaction** is the heat change at constant pressure when the number of moles of reactants shown in the balanced equation react completely.
(c) ΔH = Σ ΔH_f (products) − Σ ΔH_f (reactants); ΔH_f of an element (O₂) is zero.
$$ \Delta H = [2(-286)+0]-[2(-188)] = -572+376 = -196\ \text{kJ} $$
The reaction is exothermic: −196 kJ for 2 mol H₂O₂.

**2.** The **standard heat of formation** is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states at 298 K and 1 atm.
Formation reaction: 2C(s) + 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → CH₃COOH(l). The three given equations are used as follows:
(i) 2C + 2O₂ → 2CO₂, ΔH = 2(−99) = −198; (ii) 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, ΔH = 2(−68) = −136; (iii) 2CO₂ + 2H₂O → CH₃COOH + 2O₂, ΔH = +208 (the combustion equation reversed).
Adding (i), (ii) and (iii) gives 2C + 2H₂ + O₂ → CH₃COOH.
$$ \Delta H_{f} = -198-136+208 = -126\ \text{kcal mol}^{-1} $$

**3.** (a) The **Gibbs free energy change** ΔG is the part of the enthalpy change that is available to do useful work at constant T and P: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS; a process is spontaneous when ΔG is negative.
(b) ΔH positive makes ΔG more positive, and ΔS positive makes −TΔS negative. At **low temperature** the ΔH term dominates, so ΔG > 0 and the process is **non-spontaneous**; at **high temperature** the TΔS term dominates, so ΔG < 0 and the process becomes **spontaneous**. (Melting of ice and evaporation of water follow this pattern.)
(c) T = 15 + 273 = 288 K and ΔS = 0.300 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.
$$ \Delta G = 50-288\times0.300 = 50-86.4 = -36.4\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1} $$
ΔG is negative, so the reaction **is spontaneous at 15 °C**.

**4.** The **heat of formation** is the enthalpy change when one mole of a compound is formed from its elements in their standard states.
Formation: 2C + 3H₂ + ½O₂ → C₂H₅OH. The heats of combustion of carbon and hydrogen are the heats of formation of CO₂ and H₂O. Combustion: C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O, ΔH_c = −330.
$$ \Delta H_{c} = [2\Delta H_{f}(\text{CO}_{2})+3\Delta H_{f}(\text{H}_{2}\text{O})]-\Delta H_{f}(\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{OH}) $$
$$ -330 = [2(-94)+3(-68.5)]-\Delta H_{f} = -393.5-\Delta H_{f} $$
$$ \Delta H_{f}(\text{C}_{2}\text{H}_{5}\text{OH}) = -393.5+330 = -63.5\ \text{kcal mol}^{-1} $$
:::

$$ K = 10^{5.73} = 5.4\times10^{5} $$
The reaction is spontaneous at 298 K even though ΔS is negative, because the exothermic ΔH outweighs the −TΔS term.

::: note Model answers — short
**1.** A **d–d transition** is the excitation of an electron from a lower-energy d-orbital to a higher-energy d-orbital of the same metal ion (for an octahedral complex, from t₂g to e_g) when the ion absorbs a photon of visible light of energy equal to the crystal-field splitting Δo. The complementary colour is transmitted, so the compound is coloured.
**Characteristics of transition metals:**
1. **Variable oxidation states**, because the (n−1)d and ns electrons have similar energy (Mn shows +2 to +7).
2. **Coloured compounds**, from d–d transitions in ions with a partly filled d subshell.
3. **Complex formation**, because the ions are small, highly charged and have vacant d-orbitals ([Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺, [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻).
4. **Catalytic activity**, from variable oxidation state and the ability to adsorb reactants on the surface (Fe in the Haber process, V₂O₅ in the contact process).
5. **Paramagnetism**, from unpaired d electrons; also high melting points, hardness, high density and the formation of alloys and interstitial compounds.

**2.** (a) **Assumptions of crystal field theory:**
1. The ligands are treated as **point negative charges** (or dipoles), and the bond between metal and ligand is purely **electrostatic**.
2. In the free ion the five d-orbitals have the same energy; the approaching ligands repel the d electrons, and the orbitals **split** into sets of different energy according to their orientation relative to the ligands.
3. The difference in energy, the crystal field splitting Δ, depends on the ligand and on the geometry, and electrons fill the split orbitals according to Δ and the pairing energy.
(b) See the figure: in an octahedral complex the three t₂g orbitals are lowered by 0.4Δo and the two e_g orbitals are raised by 0.6Δo.

**3.** (a) Cu²⁺ is d⁹: the t₂g set is full and the e_g set holds three electrons, with unequal occupancy of d_z² and d_x²−y². Unequal repulsion on the ligands along z and in the xy plane makes the complex **elongate along the z-axis** (Jahn–Teller distortion), which lowers the total energy.
(b) In [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ the d–d transition absorbs light in the red-orange region, so the transmitted (complementary) colour is **blue**.
(c) **Fe³⁺** (d⁵) is more stable than Fe²⁺ (d⁶) because a half-filled d subshell gives extra exchange energy and symmetry.
(d) They have **variable oxidation states** and vacant d-orbitals, so they form intermediate compounds with reactants and offer a surface for adsorption.

**4.** (a) Cr: [Ar] 3d⁵4s¹ (not 3d⁴4s²) and Cu: [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s¹ (not 3d⁹4s²). A half-filled (d⁵) or fully filled (d¹⁰) subshell has extra stability from symmetry and exchange energy, so one 4s electron moves into the 3d subshell.
(b) Zn is [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s² and forms only Zn²⁺ (3d¹⁰): its d subshell is full in both the atom and its common ion, so it does not meet the definition of a transition element (an ion with a partly filled d subshell).
(c) Sc³⁺ is 3d⁰ and Zn²⁺ is 3d¹⁰, so there is no partly filled d subshell and **no d–d transition** is possible.
(d) Cr³⁺ is [Ar] 3d³: **three** d electrons.
:::

**1.** (a) **A = copper (Cu)**, in the sulphate CuSO₄. With a little NH₄OH a blue precipitate **B = Cu(OH)₂** forms; excess ammonia dissolves it to the deep blue **C = tetraamminecopper(II) sulphate, [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄**.
CuSO₄ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + (NH₄)₂SO₄
Cu(OH)₂ + (NH₄)₂SO₄ + 2NH₄OH → [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄ + 4H₂O
(b) Cu: [Ar] 3d¹⁰4s¹.
(c) **Electrolytic refining** (after poling): impure copper is the anode, pure copper the cathode, and acidified CuSO₄ is the electrolyte; 99.95 % pure copper deposits.

**2.** (a) See the figure: the chief ore is copper pyrite, CuFeS₂, and the roasted ore is smelted with coke and silica in a small water-jacketed blast furnace. (b) The reactions that occur in the furnace:
FeO + SiO₂ → FeSiO₃ (slag; silica is the flux that removes iron as a fusible silicate)
Cu₂O + FeS → Cu₂S + FeO
The FeO formed in the second reaction is slagged by silica. The molten **matte** (Cu₂S + FeS, about 45 % Cu) sinks below the lighter slag and is tapped off for the Bessemer converter. Matte forms because copper has a greater affinity for sulphur than iron has, so the sulphur stays with copper and the iron passes into the slag as FeSiO₃.

**3.** **Preparation:** (i) directly, by heating mercury in excess chlorine, Hg + Cl₂ → HgCl₂; (ii) industrially, by heating mercury(II) sulphate with sodium chloride and subliming the product, HgSO₄ + 2NaCl → HgCl₂↑ + Na₂SO₄ (a little MnO₂ is added to oxidise any mercurous salt).
**Properties:** white crystalline, poisonous, sublimes on heating, sparingly soluble in water and largely un-ionised (covalent). Reactions: (i) with KI, a scarlet precipitate that dissolves in excess KI: HgCl₂ + 2KI → HgI₂↓ + 2KCl; HgI₂ + 2KI → K₂[HgI₄]; (ii) with SnCl₂, a white precipitate that turns grey: 2HgCl₂ + SnCl₂ → Hg₂Cl₂↓ + SnCl₄, then Hg₂Cl₂ + SnCl₂ → 2Hg + SnCl₄; (iii) with NH₃, a white precipitate: HgCl₂ + 2NH₃ → Hg(NH₂)Cl↓ + NH₄Cl.
**Uses:** as an antiseptic and disinfectant (1 : 1000), to preserve wood and anatomical specimens, to make Nessler's reagent and calomel, and as a catalyst (with HgSO₄) for the hydration of ethyne.

**4.** (a) Dissolve zinc (or ZnO or ZnCO₃) in dilute H₂SO₄ and crystallise below 30 °C: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂; the solution gives ZnSO₄·7H₂O.
(b) At red heat (about 800 °C) it loses its water and then decomposes: 2ZnSO₄ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂ + O₂.
(c) A **double salt** is a compound formed by the crystallisation of a solution containing two simple salts in equimolar amounts; it exists in the solid state but dissociates into its ions in water. Example: potash alum, K₂SO₄·Al₂(SO₄)₃·24H₂O (or Mohr's salt).
(d) By mixing solutions of zinc sulphate and barium sulphide: ZnSO₄ + BaS → ZnS↓ + BaSO₄↓. The mixed white precipitate of ZnS and BaSO₄ is **lithopone**, a white pigment.
(e) The zinc atom ([Ar] 3d¹⁰4s²) and its only ion Zn²⁺ (3d¹⁰) both have a completely filled d subshell, so zinc does not show the properties of a typical transition element (variable oxidation states, coloured ions, paramagnetism).

::: note Model answers — short (5–7)
**5.** (a) The **open hearth process** is a method of making steel from pig iron in a shallow, saucer-shaped furnace heated by producer gas and pre-heated air (the Siemens regenerative principle); the impurities are oxidised by haematite in the charge.
(b) Fe₂O₃ + 3C → 2Fe + 3CO↑; 2Fe₂O₃ + 3Si → 4Fe + 3SiO₂; Fe₂O₃ + 3Mn → 2Fe + 3MnO; 5Fe₂O₃ + 6P → 10Fe + 3P₂O₅. Lime is added as a flux and the SiO₂ and P₂O₅ pass into the slag (CaSiO₃, Ca₃(PO₄)₂).
(c) **Spiegeleisen** is an alloy of iron, manganese and carbon. It is added at the end to restore the required amount of carbon and, through manganese, to remove dissolved oxygen (as MnO), since excess oxygen makes steel brittle.
(d) **Stainless steel:** about 74 % Fe, 18 % Cr, 8 % Ni (and about 0.1 % C).

**6.** (a) M is **zinc**, and its main ore is **zinc blende, ZnS**.
(b) Zn²⁺ is 3d¹⁰ and the atom has a full d subshell, so there is no partly filled d subshell in either; it is a non-typical transition (d-block) element.
(c) **Vertical retort:** a tall, narrow column of fireclay or silicon carbide heated from outside. Briquettes of roasted ore (ZnO) and coke are fed in at the top, and zinc vapour and CO leave from the top to a condenser. **Roasting:** 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂. **Reduction:** ZnO + C → Zn + CO at about 1673 K; the zinc distils as vapour (b.p. 907 °C).
(d) In moist air a thin protective layer of basic zinc carbonate forms, 2Zn + O₂ + CO₂ + H₂O → ZnCO₃·Zn(OH)₂, which stops further corrosion (the basis of galvanising).

**7.** **Rusting** is the slow corrosion of iron in moist air to form hydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O, a brown flaky solid.
**Electrochemical theory.** The impure iron surface with water containing dissolved CO₂ and O₂ acts as a tiny galvanic cell.
- **Anode** (oxygen-poor region): Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻.
- **Cathode** (oxygen-rich region): O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O. The electrons flow through the metal and the ions through the moisture.
- Fe²⁺ is further oxidised by air and water: 4Fe²⁺ + O₂ + 4H₂O → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8H⁺, and the oxide hydrates to rust, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O.

**Prevention:** (i) painting, oiling or plastic coating to keep out air and water; (ii) galvanising (coating with zinc, which also acts as a sacrificial anode); (iii) alloying to stainless steel; (iv) cathodic protection with magnesium blocks.

**Long answer questions [8 marks each]**

1. Describe the extraction of copper from copper pyrite, giving the reactions of each stage: concentration, roasting, smelting, Bessemerisation and refining. <span class="marks">[8]</span> (NEB 2078 model, 2079 model, 2081, 2083)
2. Describe the manufacture of steel by (a) the Basic Oxygen Process [4] and (b) the Open Hearth Process [3], and state one advantage of the open hearth process. [1] <span class="marks">[8]</span> (NEB 2079 for (b))
3. Describe the extraction of silver from argentite by the cyanide process. Write the reactions, explain why air is blown through the solution and how silver is refined. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Model answers — long
**1.** **Ore:** copper pyrite, CuFeS₂ (2–3 % Cu).
1. **Concentration by froth flotation.** The powdered ore is agitated with water, pine oil and sodium ethyl xanthate while air is blown through; the sulphide particles are wetted by oil and rise in the froth, the earthy gangue sinks.
2. **Roasting** in a reverberatory furnace in a limited supply of air at about 1070 K, which drives off moisture, As and Sb as oxides, and burns part of the sulphur:
2CuFeS₂ + O₂ → Cu₂S + 2FeS + SO₂; 2FeS + 3O₂ → 2FeO + 2SO₂; 2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂.
3. **Smelting** of the roasted ore with coke and silica in a water-jacketed blast furnace: FeO + SiO₂ → FeSiO₃ (slag), Cu₂O + FeS → Cu₂S + FeO. The molten product is **copper matte**, Cu₂S + FeS (about 45 % Cu).
4. **Bessemerisation.** Molten matte is poured into a **Bessemer converter** lined with silica; hot air is blown through. 2FeS + 3O₂ → 2FeO + 2SO₂, FeO + SiO₂ → FeSiO₃; 2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂; and then **auto-reduction**: 2Cu₂O + Cu₂S → 6Cu + SO₂↑. The product is **blister copper** (98 % pure; the escaping SO₂ makes blisters).
5. **Refining.** First poling (green wood poles reduce residual Cu₂O), then **electrolytic refining**: anode of impure copper, cathode of pure copper, electrolyte CuSO₄ + dilute H₂SO₄. Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻; cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The pure copper (99.95 %) deposits and the impurities fall as **anode mud** (Ag, Au).

**2.** (a) **Basic Oxygen Process (LD process).** Vessel: a pear-shaped converter lined with basic dolomite (CaO·MgO). Charge: molten pig iron, up to 30 % scrap, and quicklime as flux. A water-cooled lance blows 99.5 % pure oxygen at about 10 atm for 20 minutes; no fuel is needed because the oxidation of the impurities supplies the heat.
C: 2C + O₂ → 2CO↑ (burns at the mouth); Si: Si + O₂ → SiO₂, then SiO₂ + CaO → CaSiO₃ (slag); Mn: 2Mn + O₂ → 2MnO, then MnO + SiO₂ → MnSiO₃; P: 4P + 5O₂ → 2P₂O₅, then P₂O₅ + 3CaO → Ca₃(PO₄)₂ (slag); S: S + O₂ → SO₂↑. When the carbon is down to the required level, ferro-manganese or other alloying metals are added.
(b) **Open Hearth Process.** Vessel: shallow, saucer-shaped hearth with a basic (dolomite) lining for phosphoric iron; heated by producer gas and pre-heated air through regenerators. The oxidising agent is haematite in the charge: Fe₂O₃ + 3C → 2Fe + 3CO; 2Fe₂O₃ + 3Si → 4Fe + 3SiO₂; 5Fe₂O₃ + 6P → 10Fe + 3P₂O₅; Fe₂O₃ + 3Mn → 2Fe + 3MnO. Lime takes SiO₂ and P₂O₅ into the slag. A charge takes 8–10 hours.
(c) **Advantage:** because it is slow, samples can be analysed and the composition adjusted, so alloy and special steels of accurately controlled composition can be made.

**3.** **Ore:** argentite (silver glance), Ag₂S.
1. **Concentration** by froth flotation.
2. **Leaching.** The concentrate is agitated with a dilute (0.1–0.5 %) NaCN solution while **air is blown** through:
Ag₂S + 4NaCN ⇌ 2Na[Ag(CN)₂] + Na₂S
The reaction is reversible. The air oxidises the sodium sulphide as it forms, 2Na₂S + 2O₂ + H₂O → Na₂S₂O₃ + 2NaOH, which **removes a product and drives the equilibrium to the right** (Le Chatelier's principle). The silver dissolves as sodium dicyanoargentate(I).
3. **Precipitation.** Zinc dust displaces silver because zinc is more electropositive:
2Na[Ag(CN)₂] + Zn → Na₂[Zn(CN)₄] + 2Ag↓
4. **Refining** by the **Moebius electrolytic process**: anode of impure silver, cathode of pure silver, electrolyte of AgNO₃ with about 1 % HNO₃. Pure silver (99.9 %) collects as crystals at the cathode and falls into a bag below the anode; gold and platinum remain in the anode mud.
