## 2.1 Classification systems, taxonomic hierarchy and binomial nomenclature

**Taxonomy** is the science of identifying, naming and classifying organisms (the
word was coined by A. P. de Candolle, 1813); **systematics** adds their
evolutionary relationships. A **taxon** is a group of any rank — a species, a
family, a kingdom.

### From two kingdoms to three domains

| System | Proposed by | Groups | Basis / what it fixed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Two kingdoms | **Carolus Linnaeus** (1758) | Plantae, Animalia | presence of a cell wall and photosynthesis vs locomotion and ingestion |
| Three kingdoms | Ernst Haeckel (1866) | + **Protista** | unicellular forms separated |
| Four kingdoms | H. F. Copeland (1956) | **Monera**, Protista, Plantae, Animalia | prokaryotes separated |
| **Five kingdoms** | **R. H. Whittaker (1969)** | Monera, Protista, **Fungi**, Plantae, Animalia | cell structure, body organisation, **mode of nutrition**, reproduction, phylogeny |
| Six kingdoms | Carl Woese (1977) | Monera split into Archaebacteria and Eubacteria | rRNA differences |
| **Three domains** | **Carl Woese, O. Kandler and M. Wheelis (1990)** | **Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya** (above kingdoms) | sequences of **16S/18S ribosomal RNA** |

**Why two kingdoms failed.** It put prokaryotes (bacteria, blue-green algae) and
eukaryotes in one kingdom; it lumped heterotrophic fungi, which have chitin
walls, with green plants; it lumped unicellular and multicellular forms
together; and it had no place for *Euglena* (green, yet wall-less and able to
feed like an animal), slime moulds (an animal-like feeding stage and a plant-like
spore stage), lichens and viruses.

| Feature | Monera | Protista | Fungi | Plantae | Animalia |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cell type | **prokaryotic** | eukaryotic | eukaryotic | eukaryotic | eukaryotic |
| Cell wall | non-cellulosic (peptidoglycan) | present in some | present, **chitin** | present, **cellulose** | absent |
| Nuclear membrane | absent | present | present | present | present |
| Body organisation | cellular | cellular (unicellular) | multicellular or loose tissue | tissue and organ | tissue, organ and organ system |
| Nutrition | autotrophic (photo- or chemosynthetic) and heterotrophic | photosynthetic and heterotrophic | heterotrophic (**absorptive** — saprophytic, parasitic) | autotrophic (photosynthetic) | heterotrophic (holozoic) |
| Examples | bacteria, cyanobacteria, *Mycoplasma* | diatoms, dinoflagellates, *Euglena*, slime moulds, *Amoeba* | yeast, *Mucor*, *Agaricus*, *Penicillium* | algae, mosses, ferns, pines, flowering plants | *Hydra*, earthworm, frog, humans |

::: caution What stays outside the five kingdoms
Whittaker's scheme has no place for **viruses, viroids, prions and lichens** —
they are discussed separately. A question that offers "Monera" as the kingdom
of viruses is a trap: viruses are acellular.
:::

| Feature | Archaea | Bacteria | Eukarya |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nuclear envelope | absent | absent | present |
| Peptidoglycan in wall | **absent** (some have pseudomurein) | **present** | absent |
| Membrane lipids | **ether-linked**, branched chains | ester-linked, unbranched | ester-linked, unbranched |
| Histones | present in some | absent | present |
| First amino acid of a protein | methionine | **formyl-methionine** | methionine |
| Growth inhibited by streptomycin, chloramphenicol | no | yes | no |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 70S | 80S (70S in organelles) |

**Archaea** live in extreme habitats: **methanogens** (*Methanobacterium*, in
marshes and the rumen of cattle — they make the methane of biogas/gobar-gas
plants), **halophiles** (*Halobacterium*, in salt lakes) and
**thermoacidophiles** (*Sulfolobus*, *Thermoplasma*, in hot sulphur springs).

### Taxonomic hierarchy

Linnaeus arranged the ranks in a fixed order, the **taxonomic hierarchy**. Moving
up from species to kingdom, each rank contains more organisms that share fewer
characters. **Species** is the basic unit: a group of similar individuals that
interbreed in nature to give fertile offspring and are reproductively isolated
from other such groups (Ernst Mayr's biological species concept, 1942; the word
"species" was first used by John Ray).

| Rank | Ending in plants | Mango | Wheat | Human |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kingdom | — | Plantae | Plantae | Animalia |
| Division (plants) / Phylum (animals) | -phyta | Angiospermae | Angiospermae | Chordata |
| Class | -opsida, -phyceae (algae), -mycetes (fungi) | Dicotyledonae | Monocotyledonae | Mammalia |
| Order | **-ales** | Sapindales | Poales | Primata |
| Family | **-aceae** | Anacardiaceae | Poaceae | Hominidae |
| Genus | — | *Mangifera* | *Triticum* | *Homo* |
| Species | — | *indica* | *aestivum* | *sapiens* |

::: memory The ranks, top to bottom
"**K**ing **D**avid **C**ame **O**ver **F**or **G**ood **S**oup" — Kingdom,
Division, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species. (Animals: Phylum for Division.)
:::

### Binomial nomenclature

Every species has a two-word scientific name: the **generic name** followed by
the **specific epithet**. Gaspard Bauhin used two-word names (1623), but
**Linnaeus** made the system universal in *Species Plantarum* (**1753**) for
plants and *Systema Naturae* (10th edition, **1758**) for animals. Plant names
are governed by the **ICN** (International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi
and plants, formerly ICBN), animal names by the **ICZN**, bacteria by the ICNP
and viruses by the ICTV.

1. Names are in **Latin** or are Latinised, whatever their origin.
2. The generic name starts with a **capital** letter; the specific epithet is
   all **small** letters — *Rhododendron arboreum* (lali gurans), *Azadirachta
   indica* (neem).
3. Printed names are in *italics*; handwritten names are **underlined, each word
   separately**.
4. The author's name may follow in roman type, often abbreviated: *Mangifera
   indica* L. (L. = Linnaeus).
5. **Priority**: the earliest validly published name is the correct one (for
   plants, counted from 1 May 1753).
6. **Tautonyms** (genus and species the same word) are **not allowed in
   plants**, though zoology allows them — *Naja naja* (cobra), *Rattus rattus*.
7. A subspecies or variety gives a **trinomial** — *Homo sapiens sapiens*.

fluorescens*), **amphitrichous** (at both ends — *Spirillum*), **peritrichous**
(all over the surface — *E. coli*, *Salmonella typhi*).

### Gram staining

Hans Christian **Gram (1884)**: stain with **crystal violet** → fix with **iodine**
(mordant) → wash with **alcohol** (decolouriser) → counterstain with
**safranin**. Gram-positive cells stay **violet**; Gram-negative cells lose the
violet and take up the **pink-red** safranin.

### Nutrition

| Mode | Source of energy and carbon | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Photoautotrophic** (anoxygenic) | light; H₂S or organic acids as H donor, so **no O₂** is released; bacteriochlorophyll | green sulphur (*Chlorobium*), purple sulphur (*Chromatium*), purple non-sulphur (*Rhodospirillum*) bacteria |
| **Photoautotrophic** (oxygenic) | light; H₂O is split, **O₂** released; chlorophyll *a* | cyanobacteria |
| **Chemoautotrophic** | energy from oxidising inorganic substances; CO₂ fixed | *Nitrosomonas* (NH₃ → NO₂⁻), *Nitrobacter* (NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻), *Thiobacillus* and *Beggiatoa* (H₂S → S → SO₄²⁻), iron bacteria (Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺) |
| **Saprophytic** | dead organic matter | *Bacillus*, *Clostridium*, *Pseudomonas* — decomposers |
| **Parasitic** | living host, causing disease | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, *Vibrio cholerae*, *Xanthomonas* |
| **Symbiotic** | living host, mutual benefit | *Rhizobium* in legume root nodules; *E. coli* in the colon (makes vitamin K) |

Anoxygenic photosynthesis by a green sulphur bacterium:
6CO₂ + 12H₂S → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6H₂O + 12S. Sulphur, not oxygen, is the by-product.

### Reproduction and growth

Bacteria reproduce asexually by **binary fission** — the DNA replicates, the cell
elongates and a septum divides it into two identical cells. It is **amitotic**:
no spindle, no chromosome condensation. Under ideal conditions *E. coli* divides
every **20 minutes**. **Endospores** help survival, not multiplication (one cell
→ one spore → one cell). There is no true sexual reproduction, but genes are
recombined by **transformation** (uptake of free DNA; Griffith, 1928),
**conjugation** (transfer through a sex pilus; Lederberg and Tatum, 1946) and
**transduction** (transfer by a bacteriophage; Zinder and Lederberg, 1952).

```photo id="bio11/staph" caption="Spherical bacteria (cocci) of *Staphylococcus*, in the irregular grape-like clusters that give the genus its name, seen in a stained smear." width="74"
```


**Bacterial growth** means an increase in the number of cells. In the log phase
the number doubles every generation, so after $n$ generations

$$ N = N_0 \times 2^{n}, \qquad n = \frac{t}{g} = \frac{\log N - \log N_0}{\log 2} $$

where $N_0$ is the starting number, $t$ the time and $g$ the **generation
(doubling) time**.

| Phase | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Lag** | cells adapt to the new medium, grow in size and make enzymes; numbers hardly change |
| **Log (exponential)** | division at the maximum constant rate; generation time is shortest; cells are most uniform and most sensitive to antibiotics |
| **Stationary** | nutrients run out and wastes build up; division rate = death rate; secondary metabolites (antibiotics) and endospores are formed |
| **Decline (death)** | deaths exceed divisions; numbers fall exponentially |

### Cyanobacteria (blue-green algae)

Cyanobacteria are photosynthetic **prokaryotes** of the kingdom Monera, about
3.5 billion years old (stromatolites); their oxygen built up Earth's
atmosphere, and a cyanobacterium engulfed by an early eukaryote became the
chloroplast.

- **Cell**: prokaryotic, with a Gram-negative-type peptidoglycan wall, usually
  inside a **mucilage sheath**; no nucleus, no chloroplast, no mitochondria.
- **Pigments**: **chlorophyll *a*** (no chlorophyll *b*), carotenoids, and the
  **phycobilins** — **c-phycocyanin** (blue) and c-phycoerythrin (red) — held in
  phycobilisomes on free thylakoids. The blend gives the blue-green colour.
- **Photosynthesis** is **oxygenic**, with both photosystems, like green plants.
- **Reserve food**: **cyanophycean starch** (glycogen-like) and cyanophycin
  (protein) granules.
- **No flagella** at any stage; some filaments glide or oscillate (*Oscillatoria*).
- **Gas vacuoles** (pseudovacuoles) keep planktonic forms afloat.
- **Heterocysts**: large, pale, thick-walled cells of *Nostoc* and *Anabaena* that
  **fix nitrogen**. They lack photosystem II, so they make no O₂ that would
  destroy nitrogenase.
- **Reproduction**: only vegetative and asexual — fission, fragmentation into
  **hormogonia**, and thick-walled resting **akinetes**. **No sexual reproduction**
  and no motile cells.
- **Forms**: unicellular (*Chroococcus*), colonial (*Gloeocapsa*, *Microcystis*),
  filamentous (*Oscillatoria*, *Nostoc*, *Anabaena*, *Spirulina*).
- **Importance**: nitrogen fixation in rice fields (*Anabaena*, *Nostoc*,
  *Aulosira*); *Anabaena azollae* inside the water fern *Azolla* (biofertiliser);
  *Nostoc* as the partner of many lichens and in the coralloid roots of *Cycas*;
  *Spirulina* as protein-rich single-cell protein; *Microcystis* forms toxic
  water blooms.

::: caution "Blue-green algae" are not algae
Algae are eukaryotes with chloroplasts. Cyanobacteria are prokaryotes in Monera.
Options that give them "chlorophyll *b*", "a nucleus", "flagellated zoospores"
or "sexual reproduction by gametes" are all wrong.
:::

## 2.3 Viruses

### Discovery and characteristics

| Year | Scientist | Discovery |
|---|---|---|
| 1892 | **Dmitri Ivanowsky** | sap of mosaic-diseased tobacco stays infectious after passing a bacteria-proof filter |
| 1898 | **M. W. Beijerinck** | called the agent "**contagium vivum fluidum**" (contagious living fluid); named it virus |
| 1898 | Loeffler and Frosch | foot-and-mouth disease virus — the first animal virus |
| 1915–17 | F. W. Twort, F. d'Hérelle | **bacteriophages** — viruses of bacteria |
| 1935 | **W. M. Stanley** | crystallised TMV (Nobel Prize 1946) |
| 1936 | Bawden and Pirie | TMV is a nucleoprotein (protein + RNA) |
| 1971 | **T. O. Diener** | **viroids** — potato spindle tuber disease |
| 1982 | **Stanley Prusiner** | **prions** — infectious proteins (Nobel Prize 1997) |

A **virus** is an acellular, ultramicroscopic (about 20–300 nm),
**obligate intracellular parasite** made of nucleic acid in a protein coat. It
has no metabolism of its own and multiplies only inside a living host cell,
using the host's ribosomes, enzymes and energy.

| Like non-living things | Like living things |
|---|---|
| no cell, cytoplasm or cell membrane | possess genetic material (DNA or RNA) |
| no metabolism, respiration or growth outside a host | multiply inside a host and pass on their characters |
| can be **crystallised** and stored like chemicals | **mutate** and evolve |
| inert outside the host | infect specific hosts and cause disease |

### Structure and chemical composition

- The complete infectious particle is the **virion**.
- **Capsid** — the protein coat, built of identical subunits called
  **capsomeres**; it protects the nucleic acid and decides the host by
  attaching to it. Capsid + nucleic acid = **nucleocapsid**.
- **Nucleic acid** — DNA **or** RNA, **never both**; single- or
  double-stranded, linear or circular. It is the infective part.
- **Envelope** — present in some viruses (HIV, influenza, herpes,
  SARS-CoV-2): a lipid bilayer taken from the host membrane, studded with
  viral glycoprotein **spikes (peplomers)**. Viruses without it are **naked**
  (TMV, adenovirus, poliovirus, most phages).
- **Enzymes** in some: **reverse transcriptase** in retroviruses (HIV);
  lysozyme in the tail of T4; neuraminidase in influenza.

Chemically, a virus is mostly **protein**: TMV is about **95 % protein and 5 %
RNA**.

| Feature | **Tobacco mosaic virus (TMV)** | **T4 bacteriophage** |
|---|---|---|
| Host | tobacco (plant) | *Escherichia coli* (bacterium) |
| Shape and symmetry | rigid rod, about 300 × 18 nm; **helical** | tadpole-like; **complex** — polyhedral head + helical tail |
| Nucleic acid | **single-stranded RNA**, about 6400 nucleotides, coiled helically inside the capsid | **double-stranded DNA**, linear, packed in the head |
| Capsid | **2130 identical capsomeres**, each binding 3 nucleotides | head, collar, contractile **tail sheath** round a hollow core, **base plate** with six **tail fibres** |
| Envelope | none | none |


### Types of viruses

| Basis | Types and examples |
|---|---|
| **Host** | **plant viruses** — mostly ssRNA (TMV, potato virus X; cauliflower mosaic virus is dsDNA); **animal viruses** — DNA or RNA (polio, influenza, HIV, herpes, pox); **bacteriophages** — mostly dsDNA (T2, T4, λ; φX174 has ssDNA); mycophages (fungi) and cyanophages |
| **Nucleic acid** | dsDNA (T4, pox, herpes, adenovirus, hepatitis B); ssDNA (φX174, parvovirus); dsRNA (reovirus, rotavirus, wound tumour virus of plants); ssRNA (TMV, polio, influenza, HIV, SARS-CoV-2) |
| **Symmetry** | **helical** (TMV, influenza nucleocapsid); **polyhedral / icosahedral** — 20 triangular faces (adenovirus, poliovirus, herpes); **complex** (T4 phage, poxvirus) |
| **Envelope** | enveloped (HIV, influenza, herpes) or naked (TMV, adenovirus, polio) |
| **Life cycle (phages)** | **virulent** — lytic cycle only, the host bursts (T4); **temperate** — can also integrate into the host DNA as a **prophage** and be copied silently (lysogenic cycle; λ phage) |

**Sub-viral agents.** **Viroids** are tiny, naked, circular **single-stranded
RNA** molecules (about 250–400 nucleotides) with no protein coat that infect
plants (potato spindle tuber, citrus exocortis). **Prions** are **infectious
proteins with no nucleic acid** that cause scrapie in sheep, mad cow disease
(BSE) and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease in humans.

If an option gives a virus both DNA and RNA, ribosomes, a cell wall,
independent metabolism, or sensitivity to penicillin — strike it out. If it calls
a virus a member of Monera — strike it out. (Some question banks claim that the
"leukoviruses" contain both; they are retroviruses whose genome is RNA, copied
into DNA only inside the host cell.)

::: example Bacterial exponential growth and generation count
**Problem.** An *Escherichia coli* culture with a doubling time of 20 minutes starts with $5 \times 10^3$ cells mL$^{-1}$. How many cells mL$^{-1}$ are present after 2 hours of log-phase growth?

**Solution.** Two hours $= 120$ minutes gives $n = 120 / 20 = 6$ generations. Therefore $N = N_0 \times 2^n = 5 \times 10^3 \times 2^6 = 5 \times 10^3 \times 64 = \mathbf{3.2 \times 10^5\text{ cells mL}^{-1}}$.
:::

::: example Decoding viral nucleic acid type from base composition
**Problem.** Chemical analysis of a purified plant virus reveals 28 % adenine, 22 % uracil, 26 % guanine, and 24 % cytosine. What type of genome does this virus possess?

**Solution.** The presence of uracil (and absence of thymine) proves the genome is **RNA**. Because $A \neq U$ (28 % vs 22 %) and $G \neq C$ (26 % vs 24 %), Chargaff's base-pairing equality fails, so the genome is **single-stranded RNA (ssRNA)**, typical of TMV.
:::

::: example Gram stain outcome and cell-wall chemistry
**Problem.** During a Gram-staining practical, a student forgets the iodine mordant step and proceeds straight from crystal violet to ethanol decolourisation and safranin counterstain. How will *Staphylococcus aureus* (a Gram-positive coccus) appear?

**Solution.** Without Gram's iodine ($\text{I}_2\text{/KI}$), the large crystal-violet–iodine (CV–I) complex never forms inside the thick peptidoglycan mesh. Ethanol washes the uncomplexed crystal violet out, and safranin stains the cells **pink/red** (a false Gram-negative result).
:::

## Chapter summary

- Linnaeus (1758) gave two kingdoms; Whittaker (1969) gave five — Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia — chiefly on cell structure, body organisation and mode of nutrition; Woese (1990) placed three domains — Archaea, Bacteria, Eukarya — above them, on rRNA sequences.
- The hierarchy runs kingdom, division, class, order (-ales), family (-aceae), genus, species; species is the basic unit.
- Binomial names (Linnaeus, 1753): Latin, genus capitalised, epithet small, italicised or underlined separately; tautonyms are not allowed in plants.
- Bacteria have a peptidoglycan wall, 70S ribosomes, a nucleoid and often plasmids; they are cocci, bacilli, vibrios or spirilla.
- Gram-positive walls have thick peptidoglycan and stay violet; Gram-negative walls are thin with an outer lipopolysaccharide membrane and stain pink.
- Bacteria are photoautotrophic (anoxygenic, except cyanobacteria), chemoautotrophic (*Nitrosomonas*, *Nitrobacter*), saprophytic, parasitic or symbiotic.
- Binary fission doubles the population each generation ($N = N_0 \times 2^n$); the growth curve has lag, log, stationary and decline phases.
- Cyanobacteria are oxygenic prokaryotes with chlorophyll *a* and phycobilins, cyanophycean starch, heterocysts for N₂ fixation, and no flagella or sexual reproduction.
- Viruses are acellular obligate parasites with DNA or RNA (never both) in a protein capsid; TMV is a helical ssRNA rod, T4 a complex dsDNA phage.
- Viroids are naked RNA; prions are infectious proteins.

### Model answer D1: five-kingdom classification and bacteria (10 marks)

**Introduction.** Classification groups organisms by their similarities. Linnaeus gave two kingdoms. **R. H. Whittaker (1969)** proposed five kingdoms on the basis of cell structure, body organisation, mode of nutrition, reproduction and phylogeny.

| Kingdom | Cell type | Nutrition | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monera | prokaryotic | autotrophic or heterotrophic | bacteria, cyanobacteria, archaea |
| Protista | eukaryotic, unicellular | photosynthetic or heterotrophic | Amoeba, Euglena, diatoms |
| Fungi | eukaryotic, cell wall of chitin | absorptive heterotrophs | Mucor, yeast, mushrooms |
| Plantae | eukaryotic, cellulose wall | photosynthetic | mosses to flowering plants |
| Animalia | eukaryotic, no wall | ingestive heterotrophs | sponges to mammals |

**Merits and limits.** The system separates prokaryotes, and it separates fungi from plants. But viruses are not included, and the Archaea were later put in a separate domain by Carl Woese (three domains: Bacteria, Archaea and Eukarya).

**Bacteria.**

1. **Structure.** A cell wall of **peptidoglycan**, a plasma membrane, a nucleoid region of circular DNA (no nuclear membrane), plasmids, $70\text{S}$ ribosomes, and sometimes a capsule, pili and flagella.
2. **Shapes.** Cocci (spherical), bacilli (rod), vibrio (comma) and spirilla (spiral).
3. **Gram staining** distinguishes Gram-positive (thick peptidoglycan, purple) and Gram-negative (thin wall, outer membrane, pink) bacteria.
4. **Nutrition.** Photoautotrophs, chemoautotrophs (nitrifying bacteria), saprotrophs and parasites.
5. **Reproduction.** Binary fission (every $20$ min for *E. coli*), and genetic recombination by **conjugation**, **transformation** and **transduction**. Endospores resist heat.
6. **Importance.** Nitrogen fixation (*Rhizobium*, *Azotobacter*), decomposition, curd formation (*Lactobacillus*), antibiotics, genetic engineering and sewage treatment. Harmful: typhoid, cholera, tuberculosis, tetanus and food spoilage.

**Cyanobacteria** (blue-green algae) such as *Nostoc* and *Anabaena* carry out oxygenic photosynthesis, and the heterocysts fix nitrogen. *Azolla* with *Anabaena* is a biofertiliser in rice fields.

**Numerical.** One bacterium dividing every $20$ minutes produces $2^{n}$ cells after $n$ generations. In $2$ hours $n = 6$ and the number is $2^6 = 64$, and in $4$ hours $n = 12$ with $4096$ cells.

**Conclusion.** The five-kingdom system rests on cell type, organisation and nutrition, and bacteria are the prokaryotes with both great harm and great benefit.

### Model answer D2: viruses (10 marks)

**Introduction.** **Viruses** are non-cellular, obligate intracellular parasites of the size of $20$ to $300\ \text{nm}$. They show the characters of both living (multiplication, mutation) and non-living things (crystallisation, no metabolism outside a host). W. Stanley crystallised TMV in 1935, and Ivanovsky and Beijerinck discovered them in tobacco mosaic disease.

**Structure.** A **nucleic acid core** (either DNA or RNA, never both) surrounded by a protein coat, the **capsid**, made of capsomeres. Some animal viruses have an envelope.

- **Bacteriophage** T4: icosahedral head with DNA, a tail sheath and tail fibres.
- **TMV:** helical rod of single-stranded RNA.
- **HIV:** an enveloped retrovirus with RNA and reverse transcriptase.

**Multiplication.**

1. **Lytic cycle:** adsorption, penetration, synthesis of viral nucleic acid and protein, assembly and lysis (release).
2. **Lysogenic cycle:** the phage DNA is integrated into the host chromosome as a **prophage** and is replicated with it, and later may enter the lytic cycle.
3. **Retroviruses** make DNA from RNA by reverse transcriptase.

**Diseases.**

| Host | Disease |
|---|---|
| Human | influenza, polio, rabies, dengue, COVID-19, hepatitis B, AIDS |
| Plant | tobacco mosaic, potato leaf roll |
| Animal | foot-and-mouth, bird flu |
| Bacteria | phage infection |

Control: vaccination, hygiene and antivirals. Antibiotics do not work on viruses. Other agents: **viroids** (naked RNA, potato spindle tuber) and **prions** (infectious proteins, mad cow disease).

| Feature | Virus | Bacterium |
|---|---|---|
| Cell structure | non-cellular | prokaryotic cell |
| Nucleic acid | DNA or RNA | both |
| Reproduction | only in host cells | independent binary fission |
| Ribosomes | absent | present |
| Antibiotics | no effect | effective |

**Numerical.** A phage burst size of $100$ means a single infected cell releases $100$ phages. After three cycles one phage could give $100^3 = 10^6$ phages (in ideal conditions). A virus of $100\ \text{nm}$ is $100\times10^{-9} = 10^{-7}\ \text{m}$, much smaller than a bacterium ($1$ to $5\ \mu\text{m}$).

**Conclusion.** Viruses are acellular parasites that take over the host machinery, so they can be controlled by vaccines, not by antibiotics.
