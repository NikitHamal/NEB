Basic Components of Life is the first unit of the Botany section and carries **2 of
the 40 Botany questions**. Both are almost always straight recall or one-step
reasoning: which bond joins the glucose units of cellulose, which sugar is
non-reducing, which mineral sits in chlorophyll or in nitrogenase, what a
competitive inhibitor does to $K_m$, which class an enzyme belongs to. The
chapter is short to read and quick to score — every fact below has been asked
in some form by entrance setters, so learn the tables.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- **Carbohydrate classes** — mono-, di- and polysaccharides; aldose or ketose; reducing or non-reducing (sucrose is the non-reducing one).
- **Glycosidic bonds** — α-1,4 (starch, glycogen), α-1,6 (their branch points), β-1,4 (cellulose, chitin); inulin is a fructose polymer.
- **Lipids** — triglyceride = glycerol + 3 fatty acids (ester bonds, 3 H₂O lost); saturated vs unsaturated; phospholipids, steroids, waxes.
- **Mineral roles and deficiency signs** — Mg in chlorophyll, Mo in nitrogenase, Mn and Cl in water splitting, Ca in the middle lamella; mobile elements show symptoms in old leaves first.
- **Protein structure** — peptide bond, four levels and the bonds that hold each, fibrous vs globular, simple vs conjugated.
- **Enzymes** — cofactors, lock-and-key vs induced fit, $K_m$, the effect of temperature, pH and inhibitors, and the six IUB classes with one example each.
:::

## 1.1 Carbohydrates and lipids: structure, types and biological role

### Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are **polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones**, or substances that give
these on hydrolysis. Most fit the formula Cₙ(H₂O)ₙ — "hydrates of carbon" — but
the formula is only a guide: deoxyribose (C₅H₁₀O₄) is a carbohydrate that does
not fit it, and acetic acid (C₂H₄O₂) fits it but is not a carbohydrate. A sugar
with a free aldehyde group (–CHO) is an **aldose**; one with a ketone group
(>C=O) is a **ketose**.

| Class | Number of carbons | Aldose example | Ketose example | Where it matters |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Triose | 3 | glyceraldehyde (PGAL) | dihydroxyacetone (DHAP) | first sugars of the Calvin cycle and glycolysis |
| Tetrose | 4 | erythrose | erythrulose | erythrose-4-phosphate in the Calvin cycle |
| Pentose | 5 | ribose, deoxyribose, xylose | ribulose | RNA, DNA; RuBP is the CO₂ acceptor |
| Hexose | 6 | glucose, galactose, mannose | fructose | respiratory fuel; fruit sugar |
| Heptose | 7 | glucoheptose | sedoheptulose | Calvin cycle |

**Glucose** (dextrose, grape sugar) is an aldohexose; **fructose** (laevulose,
fruit sugar) is a ketohexose and the sweetest natural sugar. In water, hexoses
close into rings. In **α-glucose** the –OH on carbon 1 lies below the plane of
the ring; in **β-glucose** it lies above. That single difference decides whether
the polymer is a digestible fuel (starch, α) or an indigestible fibre (cellulose, β).

Two sugars join by a **glycosidic bond**, formed by condensation (loss of one
H₂O) and broken by hydrolysis. **Oligosaccharides** have 2–10 sugar units.

| Disaccharide | Units | Bond | Reducing? | Source / role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sucrose | glucose + fructose | α-1 → β-2 | **No** | cane, beet; the transport sugar of the phloem |
| Maltose | glucose + glucose | α-1,4 | Yes | germinating barley (malt); starch digestion |
| Lactose | galactose + glucose | β-1,4 | Yes | milk sugar |
| Cellobiose | glucose + glucose | β-1,4 | Yes | breakdown product of cellulose |
| Trehalose | glucose + glucose | α-1,1 | No | fungi; the blood sugar of insects |

Sucrose is non-reducing because the bond joins the reducing carbons of both units
(C-1 of glucose and C-2 of fructose), so no free carbonyl group is left. Its
hydrolysis by **invertase** (sucrase) gives an equal mixture of glucose and
fructose called **invert sugar** — the optical rotation changes from
dextro to laevo, hence the name. **Raffinose** (galactose + glucose + fructose) is
a common non-reducing trisaccharide of seeds.

**Polysaccharides** (glycans) are long chains of hundreds to thousands of units.
They are insoluble, non-sweet and non-reducing, which is exactly why they make
good stores.

| Polysaccharide | Monomer | Bonds | Shape | Iodine colour | Role and occurrence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Amylose (≈20–25 % of starch) | α-glucose | α-1,4 | unbranched helix | blue-black | plant food store |
| Amylopectin (≈75–80 % of starch) | α-glucose | α-1,4 + α-1,6 branches every ~24–30 units | branched | red-violet | plant food store |
| Glycogen | α-glucose | α-1,4 + α-1,6 branches every ~8–12 units | highly branched | red-brown | store of animals and **fungi** ("animal starch") |
| Cellulose | β-glucose | β-1,4 | straight chains in microfibrils | none | plant cell wall; the most abundant organic compound on Earth |
| Chitin | N-acetylglucosamine | β-1,4 | straight | none | fungal cell wall, insect exoskeleton |
| Inulin | **fructose** | β-2,1 | short chains | none | stored in the tuberous roots of *Dahlia* and dandelion |
| Pectin | galacturonic acid | α-1,4 | — | none | middle lamella (as calcium pectate) |
| Peptidoglycan (murein) | NAG + NAM with short peptides | β-1,4 + peptide cross-links | net-like sac | none | bacterial cell wall |
| Agar, carrageenan | galactose (partly sulphated) | — | — | none | red algae (*Gelidium*, *Gracilaria*) |

::: caution Three favourite traps
1. **Sucrose is non-reducing**; maltose and lactose are reducing. "All disaccharides reduce Benedict's solution" is false.
2. **Inulin is a fructose polymer**, not glucose — and glycogen, not starch, is the store of fungi.
3. Cellulose and starch are both glucose. They differ only in α versus β linkage, and humans lack the enzyme (cellulase) that splits the β-1,4 bond between glucose units; cattle digest grass only with the help of cellulase-making microbes in the rumen.
:::

| Test | Reagent | Positive result | Detects |
|---|---|---|---|
| Molisch's | α-naphthol + conc. H₂SO₄ | violet ring at the junction | any carbohydrate |
| Benedict's / Fehling's | alkaline Cu²⁺ (Benedict's: CuSO₄, sodium citrate, Na₂CO₃) | brick-red Cu₂O on boiling | reducing sugars |
| Barfoed's | copper acetate in acetic acid | red precipitate within ~2 min | **mono**saccharides (disaccharides react slowly) |
| Seliwanoff's | resorcinol + HCl | quick cherry-red | **ketoses** (fructose) |
| Iodine | I₂ in KI | blue-black | starch (amylose) |

**Biological roles.** Carbohydrates are the main respiratory fuel (glucose gives
about 17 kJ g⁻¹); storage (starch, glycogen, inulin); structure (cellulose,
hemicellulose, pectin, chitin, peptidoglycan); the sugar part of nucleotides
(ribose, deoxyribose) and of ATP, NAD, FAD and coenzyme A; and recognition
markers on cell surfaces (glycoproteins and glycolipids, including the ABO
blood-group antigens). Agar from red algae solidifies microbiological culture
media.

### Lipids

**Lipids** are esters of fatty acids with alcohols (or substances related to
them) that are **insoluble in water and soluble in organic solvents** such as
ether, chloroform and benzene. Unlike carbohydrates and proteins, they are not
polymers — a triglyceride is a small molecule (relative molecular mass usually
below 1000) built from four parts, not a chain of repeating units.

| Type | What it is | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple lipids** — fats and oils | ester of **glycerol** + 3 fatty acids (triglyceride) | ghee, butter, mustard oil |
| Simple lipids — waxes | ester of a long-chain fatty acid + a long-chain **monohydric** alcohol | cuticle wax, beeswax, lanolin |
| **Compound lipids** — phospholipids | glycerol + 2 fatty acids + phosphate + a base | lecithin (choline), cephalin (ethanolamine) |
| Compound lipids — others | lipid + sugar or protein | glycolipids (cerebrosides), lipoproteins |
| **Derived lipids** — steroids | four fused rings (three 6-carbon + one 5-carbon) | cholesterol (animals), ergosterol (fungi), sitosterol (plants), sex hormones, brassinosteroids |
| Derived lipids — terpenes | built from isoprene (C₅H₈) units | carotenoids, rubber, essential oils (menthol), phytol tail of chlorophyll, gibberellins |

A **triglyceride** forms when each of the three –OH groups of glycerol makes an
**ester bond** with the –COOH of a fatty acid; **three molecules of water** are
released. A **fatty acid** is a hydrocarbon chain (usually 14–20 carbons, almost
always an even number) ending in –COOH.

| Feature | Saturated fatty acid | Unsaturated fatty acid |
|---|---|---|
| C=C double bonds | none | one (mono-) or more (poly-unsaturated) |
| Chain shape | straight, packs tightly | kinked at each cis double bond |
| State of its fat at room temperature | solid (fat) | liquid (oil) |
| Examples | palmitic (C16:0), stearic (C18:0) | oleic (C18:1), linoleic (C18:2), linolenic (C18:3), arachidonic (C20:4) |
| Typical source | ghee, butter, coconut and palm oil | mustard, sunflower, soybean, fish oil |

**Essential fatty acids** — linoleic and linolenic acid (arachidonic acid is made
from linoleic) — cannot be made by animals and must come from plant food.
Hydrogenating an oil with a nickel catalyst saturates its double bonds and turns
it into a solid cooking fat (vanaspati ghee).

A **phospholipid** is **amphipathic**: its phosphate head is hydrophilic and its
two fatty-acid tails are hydrophobic, so in water phospholipids line up
tail-to-tail into the **bilayer** of every cell membrane. **Cutin** (in the
cuticle) and **suberin** (in cork and the Casparian strip) are lipid polymers that
waterproof plant surfaces.

::: key Why seeds store oil
Lipids give about **39 kJ g⁻¹** (9.3 kcal g⁻¹) on oxidation against about
**17 kJ g⁻¹** (4.1 kcal g⁻¹) for carbohydrate and protein — more than twice as
much — because their carbon is far less oxidised, and they are stored without
water. Mustard, groundnut, sesame and sunflower seeds therefore store oil.
Lipids stain red with **Sudan III**, black with osmic acid, and leave a
translucent grease spot on paper.
:::

**Biological roles of lipids:** long-term energy store; structural basis of
membranes (phospholipids; sterols such as cholesterol in animals and
phytosterols in plants — bacteria have no sterols, *Mycoplasma* being the
exception); waterproofing (wax, cutin, suberin); insulation and cushioning in
animals; hormones (steroids, and in plants gibberellins, abscisic acid and
brassinosteroids); pigments (carotenoids) and vitamins A, D, E and K, which are
fat-soluble.

## 1.2 Minerals and their biological roles

Burn a plant and the ash holds about 60 elements, but only some are
**essential**. Arnon and Stout (1939) gave the criteria: without the element
the plant cannot complete its life cycle; no other element can replace it; and
it is directly involved in the plant's metabolism. **Seventeen** elements meet
them — C, H and O from air and water, and **14 mineral elements** from the soil.
They are grouped by the **amount** needed (macronutrients occur in excess of
10 mmol per kg of dry matter, micronutrients below it), not by importance.

| Element (absorbed as) | Main roles | Deficiency sign or disease |
|---|---|---|
| **N** (NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺) | amino acids, proteins, nucleic acids, chlorophyll, ATP, hormones | yellowing of **old** leaves first; stunting |
| **P** (H₂PO₄⁻, HPO₄²⁻) | ATP, nucleic acids, phospholipids, NADP | dark green or **purple** leaves (anthocyanin); poor roots; late maturity |
| **K** (K⁺) | opening and closing of stomata (guard-cell turgor); activator of over 40 enzymes; osmotic and ionic balance | scorched leaf **margins** and tips of old leaves; weak stems (lodging) |
| **Ca** (Ca²⁺) | **calcium pectate of the middle lamella**; spindle formation; membrane function; second messenger | death of **meristems** and young leaf tips; blossom-end rot of tomato |
| **Mg** (Mg²⁺) | **centre of the chlorophyll molecule**; activates RuBisCO, PEP carboxylase and the kinases; holds ribosome subunits together | interveinal chlorosis of **old** leaves |
| **S** (SO₄²⁻) | cysteine and methionine; coenzyme A, thiamine, biotin, ferredoxin | chlorosis of **young** leaves |
| **Fe** (Fe³⁺, Fe²⁺) | cytochromes, ferredoxin, catalase, peroxidase; needed to **make** chlorophyll though not part of it | interveinal chlorosis of **young** leaves |
| **Mn** (Mn²⁺) | **splitting of water** in photosystem II; activates Krebs-cycle enzymes | grey speck of oats, marsh spot of pea |
| **Zn** (Zn²⁺) | **auxin** (IAA) synthesis via tryptophan; carbonic anhydrase, alcohol dehydrogenase | little leaf, rosetting; **khaira disease of rice** |
| **Cu** (Cu²⁺) | **plastocyanin**; cytochrome oxidase | die-back (exanthema) of citrus; reclamation disease |
| **B** (BO₃³⁻, B₄O₇²⁻) | pollen germination and pollen-tube growth; Ca uptake; sugar translocation | heart rot of beet, browning of cauliflower, internal cork of apple |
| **Mo** (MoO₄²⁻) | **nitrogenase** and **nitrate reductase**; needed in the smallest amount of all | whiptail of cauliflower |
| **Cl** (Cl⁻) | with Mn, **water splitting** in PS II; anion balance; stomatal movement | wilting, bronzing of leaves |
| **Ni** (Ni²⁺) | **urease**; hydrogenase | necrotic leaf tips (urea builds up) |

::: memory Which leaves show the deficiency first?
**Mobile** elements (N, P, K, Mg) are moved out of old leaves to the growing
parts, so the **old leaves** show the symptom first. **Immobile** elements (Ca,
S, Fe, B) stay where they were deposited, so the **young leaves and buds** show it
first. For the macronutrients say "**C. HOPKiNS CaFe, Mighty good**" — C, H, O, P, K, N, S, Ca, (Fe), Mg — and remember that Fe, though it sits in the mnemonic, is a micronutrient.
:::

::: caution Mg is in chlorophyll; Fe only helps to make it
Chlorophyll is a porphyrin ring with **magnesium** at its centre and four
nitrogen atoms holding it; **iron** sits at the centre of haem (cytochromes,
haemoglobin, catalase). Iron deficiency causes chlorosis because iron is needed
for chlorophyll **synthesis**, not because it is part of the molecule.
:::

**Mineral roles in animals and humans** are asked occasionally: Fe in haemoglobin
and myoglobin (lack causes anaemia); Ca and P in bones and teeth, Ca²⁺ in blood
clotting and muscle contraction; Na⁺ and K⁺ in nerve impulses and osmotic
balance; I in thyroxine (lack causes goitre); Co in vitamin B₁₂; Zn in carbonic
anhydrase and insulin storage; F in tooth enamel.

## 1.3 Proteins and enzymes: structure, types and biological role

### Amino acids and the peptide bond

A protein is a polymer of **amino acids**. Each amino acid has a central
α-carbon carrying an **amino group** (–NH₂), a **carboxyl group** (–COOH), a
hydrogen atom and a variable **side chain (R)**. Twenty kinds are coded by the
genetic code. **Glycine** (R = H) is the simplest and the only one that is not
optically active; **proline** is strictly an imino acid; **cysteine** and
**methionine** contain sulphur; phenylalanine, tyrosine and tryptophan are
aromatic. Plants make all twenty; humans cannot make nine **essential amino
acids** and must eat them.

Amino acids are **amphoteric**. At a particular pH, the **isoelectric point**,
the molecule carries both charges at once (–NH₃⁺ and –COO⁻) and is called a
**zwitterion**.

The **peptide bond** (–CO–NH–) forms by condensation between the –COOH of one
amino acid and the –NH₂ of the next. A chain of *n* amino acids has **n − 1**
peptide bonds and releases **n − 1** water molecules when it forms. The chain has
a free amino end (**N-terminus**) and a free carboxyl end (**C-terminus**); the
sequence is always written from N to C.

::: memory The essential amino acids
**PVT TIM HaLL** — Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine,
Methionine, Histidine, Arginine, Leucine, Lysine. Arginine is only
semi-essential (needed from food in childhood), which leaves nine for adults.
:::

### The four levels of protein structure

| Level | What it is | Bonds that hold it | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary** | the sequence of amino acids, fixed by the gene | peptide (covalent) | insulin: 51 amino acids in chains A (21) and B (30), sequenced by Sanger |
| **Secondary** | regular local folding: **α-helix** (right-handed, 3.6 residues per turn) or **β-pleated sheet** | **hydrogen bonds** between C=O and N–H of the backbone | α-keratin of hair and wool (helix); silk fibroin (sheet) |
| **Tertiary** | the whole chain folded into a compact 3-D shape | H-bonds, ionic bonds, **disulphide bridges** (–S–S–), hydrophobic interactions | myoglobin; every enzyme |
| **Quaternary** | two or more folded polypeptides together | the same weak bonds as tertiary | **haemoglobin** — 2α + 2β chains, four haem groups |

Pauling and Corey described the α-helix and β-sheet (1951). Only proteins with
more than one polypeptide chain have a quaternary level. Heat, extreme pH,
heavy-metal ions (Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺, Ag⁺), urea and strong alcohol break the weak
bonds of the secondary, tertiary and quaternary levels — the protein
**denatures** and loses its function, but its peptide bonds (primary structure)
survive. Boiling an egg and curdling milk with lemon juice are denaturation.

### Types of proteins

| Basis | Type | Features | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shape | **Fibrous** | long parallel chains; insoluble; structural | keratin, collagen, fibroin, myosin, elastin |
| Shape | **Globular** | chains folded into a ball; soluble; functional | enzymes, antibodies, haemoglobin, insulin, albumin |
| Composition | **Simple** | amino acids only | albumins, globulins, histones, glutelins (glutenin of wheat), prolamines (zein of maize) |
| Composition | **Conjugated** | protein + a non-protein **prosthetic group** | nucleoproteins, glycoproteins, lipoproteins, **chromoproteins** (haemoglobin, cytochromes, phytochrome), **phosphoproteins** (casein of milk), metalloproteins |
| Composition | **Derived** | made by partial hydrolysis or denaturation | proteoses, peptones, peptides |

| Role | Examples |
|---|---|
| Catalysis | all enzymes — amylase, RuBisCO (the most abundant protein on Earth) |
| Structure | collagen (the most abundant protein in mammals), keratin, extensin of plant cell walls |
| Transport | haemoglobin, carrier proteins and channels in membranes |
| Defence | antibodies (immunoglobulins), fibrinogen in clotting |
| Hormones and signalling | insulin, glucagon; receptor proteins |
| Movement | actin and myosin, tubulin, flagellin |
| Storage | casein, ovalbumin, seed proteins (glutenin, zein) |

**Tests:** biuret (CuSO₄ + NaOH) gives **violet** with two or more peptide bonds;
xanthoproteic (conc. HNO₃) gives **yellow** with aromatic amino acids; Millon's
gives **red** with tyrosine; ninhydrin gives **purple** with free amino acids.

### Enzymes: nature and history

An **enzyme** is a biological catalyst — usually a globular protein — that speeds
up a specific reaction by **lowering its activation energy**, without being used
up and without changing the equilibrium or the energy released ($\Delta G$).

| Year | Person | Contribution |
|---|---|---|
| 1833 | Payen and Persoz | diastase (amylase) from malt — the first enzyme discovered |
| 1877 | Wilhelm Kühne | coined the word "enzyme" ("in yeast"); some books give 1878 |
| 1894 | Emil Fischer | **lock-and-key** model |
| 1897 | Eduard Buchner | a cell-free yeast extract (**zymase**) ferments sugar |
| 1913 | Michaelis and Menten | the kinetic equation and $K_m$ |
| 1926 | James Sumner | crystallised **urease** from jack bean — enzymes are proteins |
| 1958 | Daniel Koshland | **induced-fit** model |
| 1982–83 | Cech and Altman | **ribozymes** — catalytic RNA (Nobel 1989) |

**Ribozymes** break the rule that all enzymes are proteins: RNase P and the
peptidyl transferase of the ribosome (23S rRNA in bacteria) are RNA.

**Cofactors.** Many enzymes need a non-protein part. The protein part alone is
the **apoenzyme** (inactive); apoenzyme + cofactor = **holoenzyme** (active).

| Cofactor | Nature | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Prosthetic group** | organic, **tightly** (often covalently) bound | haem in catalase and peroxidase; FAD in succinate dehydrogenase; biotin in carboxylases |
| **Coenzyme** | organic, **loosely** bound, leaves after the reaction; most are made from B vitamins | NAD⁺/NADP⁺ (niacin, B₃), FAD/FMN (riboflavin, B₂), coenzyme A (pantothenic acid, B₅), TPP (thiamine, B₁) |
| **Metal-ion activator** | inorganic ion | Zn²⁺ in carbonic anhydrase and carboxypeptidase; Mg²⁺ in kinases; Mo in nitrogenase; Fe and Cu in cytochrome oxidase |

### How enzymes work

The substrate binds to a small pocket, the **active site**, forming an
**enzyme–substrate (ES) complex**; the reaction occurs and the products leave:
E + S ⇌ ES → E + P. In Fischer's **lock-and-key** model the active site is rigid
and fits the substrate exactly. In Koshland's **induced-fit** model — the accepted
one — the active site changes shape as the substrate binds, closing round it.

The rate rises with substrate concentration [S] and levels off when every active
site is busy, at the maximum rate $V_{max}$. Michaelis and Menten wrote

$$ v = \frac{V_{max}\,[S]}{K_m + [S]} $$

The **Michaelis constant** $K_m$ is the substrate concentration at which the rate
is **half** of $V_{max}$. A **low $K_m$ means high affinity** — the enzyme reaches
half speed at a low substrate concentration. Useful values: at $[S] = K_m$,
$v = V_{max}/2$; at $[S] = 4K_m$, $v = 0.8\,V_{max}$; at $[S] = 9K_m$,
$v = 0.9\,V_{max}$.

### Factors affecting enzyme activity

- **Temperature.** The rate roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise
  (temperature coefficient $Q_{10} \approx 2$) up to the **optimum** — about 37 °C
  for human enzymes and 25–40 °C for most plant enzymes. Above it the enzyme
  **denatures** and the rate falls steeply, usually irreversibly. Low temperature
  only **inactivates** an enzyme, reversibly — which is why seeds and food keep
  in the cold. Enzymes of hot-spring bacteria survive far higher temperatures
  (*Taq* polymerase of *Thermus aquaticus* works at about 72 °C).
- **pH.** Each enzyme has an **optimum pH**: pepsin about 2, salivary amylase 6.8,

- **Substrate concentration** — a rising curve that levels off at $V_{max}$

- **Enzyme concentration** — rate is directly proportional to [E] while

### Enzyme inhibition

| Type | Where the inhibitor binds | Effect on $K_m$ and $V_{max}$ | Reversed by more substrate? | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Competitive** | active site — it resembles the substrate | $K_m$ **rises**, $V_{max}$ unchanged | **Yes** | **malonate** blocks succinate dehydrogenase; sulpha drugs mimic PABA in bacteria; ethanol competes with methanol for alcohol dehydrogenase |
| **Non-competitive** | another site on the enzyme | $V_{max}$ **falls**, $K_m$ unchanged | No | cyanide on cytochrome oxidase; Hg²⁺, Ag⁺, Pb²⁺ on –SH groups |
| **Irreversible** | binds the active site covalently | enzyme destroyed for good | No | nerve gas (DFP) on acetylcholinesterase; penicillin on the transpeptidase of bacterial walls |
| **Feedback (allosteric)** | a regulatory (allosteric) site; the inhibitor is the **end product** of the pathway | slows the first enzyme of the pathway | — | isoleucine inhibits threonine deaminase; ATP inhibits phosphofructokinase |

**Isoenzymes (isozymes)** are different molecular forms of one enzyme that
catalyse the same reaction — lactate dehydrogenase has five.

### Classification of enzymes

The International Union of Biochemistry (1961) sorts enzymes into **six classes**
by the reaction they catalyse; each enzyme has an EC number whose first digit
is its class. (A seventh class, translocases, was added in 2018.)

| EC | Class | Reaction | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Oxidoreductases** | transfer of H or electrons (oxidation–reduction) | dehydrogenases, oxidases, catalase, peroxidase |
| 2 | **Transferases** | transfer of a group (not H) from one molecule to another | transaminases (–NH₂), kinases (phosphate), e.g. hexokinase |
| 3 | **Hydrolases** | breaking a bond **with water** | amylase, lipase, pepsin, trypsin, sucrase, urease |
| 4 | **Lyases** | removing a group **without water**, leaving a double bond (or adding to one) | aldolase, fumarase, carbonic anhydrase, decarboxylases |
| 5 | **Isomerases** | rearranging atoms within one molecule | phosphohexose isomerase, triose phosphate isomerase, mutases |
| 6 | **Ligases** (synthetases) | **joining** two molecules using energy from ATP | DNA ligase, aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, pyruvate carboxylase |

::: memory The six classes in order
"**O**h **T**he **H**ungry **L**ion **I**s **L**azy" — Oxidoreductase,
Transferase, Hydrolase, Lyase, Isomerase, Ligase (EC 1 to 6).
:::

::: tip Classifying an enzyme you have never seen
Read the reaction, not the name. Water added across a bond that breaks →
hydrolase. A group moved between two substrates → transferase. ATP spent to join
two molecules → ligase. One molecule in, one rearranged molecule out →
isomerase. A molecule split without water, or a C–C bond broken to release CO₂ →
lyase. H atoms or electrons moved → oxidoreductase.
:::

**Uses of enzymes:** amylase in brewing and baking; pectinase to clear fruit
juice; proteases and lipases in detergents; rennet (chymosin) to curdle milk for
cheese; **papain** from papaya and bromelain from pineapple to tenderise meat;
invertase for confectionery syrup; **streptokinase** from *Streptococcus* to
dissolve blood clots.

::: example Counting peptide bonds and water loss in a polypeptide
**Problem.** A functional plant enzyme consists of two polypeptide chains containing 120 and 80 amino acid residues respectively. What is the total number of peptide bonds in the enzyme, and what is the molecular mass of a linear malto-oligosaccharide made of 10 glucose units ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 = 180$)?

**Solution.** In a linear protein of $n$ total residues across $c$ polypeptide chains, the number of peptide bonds is $n - c$: here $(120 + 80) - 2 = \mathbf{198}$ peptide bonds. For 10 glucose units linked by $\alpha(1\rightarrow4)$ glycosidic bonds, $10 - 1 = 9$ water molecules ($9 \times 18 = 162$) are lost, so $M = 10 \times 180 - 162 = \mathbf{1638\text{ Da}}$.
:::

::: example Reading a Michaelis–Menten curve at half-maximal velocity
**Problem.** An enzyme has $V_{\max} = 60\text{ }\mu\text{mol min}^{-1}$ and $K_m = 2.0\text{ mM}$. What initial reaction velocity $v_0$ is obtained at $[S] = 6.0\text{ mM}$, and how does $K_m$ change if a competitive inhibitor is added?

**Solution.** By $v_0 = \frac{V_{\max}[S]}{K_m + [S]}$, at $[S] = 6.0\text{ mM}$ we have $[S] = 3K_m$, so $v_0 = \frac{3}{4}V_{\max} = \mathbf{45\text{ }\mu\text{mol min}^{-1}}$. A competitive inhibitor raises the apparent $K_m$ while leaving $V_{\max}$ unchanged.
:::

::: example Identifying mineral-deficiency mobility on the shoot
**Problem.** A tomato seedling grown in nutrient-deficient sand shows chlorosis first on its oldest lower leaves, whereas a second seedling shows death of the terminal bud and hooked young leaves while old leaves stay green. Which two elements are deficient?

**Solution.** Old-leaf chlorosis indicates a **mobile** element (N, P, K, or Mg) translocated to young sinks. Terminal-bud death with hooked young leaves is the signature of an **immobile** structural element, specifically **calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$)** (or boron).
:::

## Chapter summary

- Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones; glucose is an aldohexose, fructose a ketohexose, ribulose a ketopentose.
- Sucrose (glucose + fructose, α-1 → β-2) is non-reducing; maltose, lactose and cellobiose are reducing.
- Starch (amylose + amylopectin) and glycogen are α-glucose stores with α-1,4 chains and α-1,6 branches; cellulose is β-1,4 glucose; chitin is β-1,4 N-acetylglucosamine; inulin is a fructose polymer.
- A triglyceride is glycerol esterified with three fatty acids (three water molecules lost); lipids give about 39 kJ g⁻¹, more than twice carbohydrate.
- Phospholipids are amphipathic and form membrane bilayers; steroids have four fused rings; waxes are esters of long-chain alcohols.
- Seventeen elements are essential; Mg is in chlorophyll, Mo in nitrogenase, Mn and Cl split water, Ca forms calcium pectate, Zn is needed for auxin; mobile N, P, K, Mg show deficiency in old leaves first.
- Proteins are chains of amino acids joined by peptide bonds (n − 1 for n residues); secondary structure is held by H-bonds, tertiary adds ionic, disulphide and hydrophobic bonds, quaternary needs two or more chains.
- Enzymes lower activation energy; holoenzyme = apoenzyme + cofactor; the induced-fit model (Koshland) replaced lock-and-key (Fischer).
- $K_m$ is the substrate concentration at half $V_{max}$; competitive inhibitors raise $K_m$, non-competitive inhibitors lower $V_{max}$; feedback inhibition acts on the first enzyme of a pathway.
- The six enzyme classes: oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases.
