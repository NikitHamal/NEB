The smallest chapter in the Botany paper — **1 of the 40 Botany
questions** — and a gift for the prepared student, because its territory
is tightly fenced: the three growth promoters (**auxins, gibberellins,
cytokinins**) with their discovery stories and farm applications, the
types of **seed germination** (epigeal, hypogeal, vivipary), and the
causes and cures of **seed dormancy**. One question, drawn from a short
list of named people, named chemicals and named examples — learn the
list below and the mark is banked.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- **Auxin** — IAA (from tryptophan; shoot apex, young leaves); Darwin → Boysen-Jensen → Paál → Went experiments; *Avena* curvature bioassay; polar basipetal transport; elongation, apical dominance, rooting, parthenocarpy; IBA/NAA rooting, 2,4-D dicot-selective weedicide; bell-shaped dose–response (shoot optimum inhibits roots).
- **Gibberellin** — bakanae (foolish seedling) disease, *Gibberella fujikuroi*, Kurosawa; internode elongation (dwarf rescue), bolting, dormancy breaking, aleurone α-amylase in malting, grape enlargement.
- **Cytokinin** — kinetin (Miller and Skoog, 1955, autoclaved herring sperm DNA); zeatin (Letham, 1963, maize); root apex source, xylem transport; cell division (with auxin), lateral buds, Richmond–Lang delay of senescence; auxin : cytokinin ratio decides roots vs shoots in culture.
- **Germination** — imbibition first, then enzyme activation, radicle out before plumule; needs water, oxygen, suitable temperature (light for photoblastic seeds); GA → aleurone → α-amylase mobilises endosperm.
- **Types** — epigeal (hypocotyl lifts cotyledons: bean, castor, sunflower); hypogeal (cotyledons stay below: pea, maize, mango); vivipary (mangroves: *Rhizophora*).
- **Dormancy vs quiescence** — dormant seeds fail *even under favourable conditions*; causes: hard impermeable coat, immature embryo, ABA/inhibitors, light requirement; cures: scarification, **stratification** (moist chilling), light, gibberellin, leaching; controlled by the ABA : GA balance.
:::

## 13.1 Auxins, gibberellins and cytokinins: roles and applications

A **plant hormone** (phytohormone, growth regulator) is an organic
substance made in tiny amounts in one part of the plant and transported
to act elsewhere. The syllabus names the three great promoters; the
inhibitor **abscisic acid (ABA)** enters through dormancy, where it
belongs.

### Auxins

The principal natural auxin is **indole-3-acetic acid (IAA)**, made from
the amino acid **tryptophan** mainly in the **shoot apical meristem,
young leaves and developing seeds**. Its discovery is a chain of four
classic experiments the paper loves:

1. **Charles and Francis Darwin (1880)** — a covered coleoptile tip no
   longer bends towards light: the tip **perceives** light, bending
   happens below, so an "influence" travels down.
2. **Boysen-Jensen (1913)** — the tip replaced on gelatin still works:
   the influence is a diffusible **chemical**.
3. **Paál (1918)** — an off-centre tip in darkness still bends the stump:
   the chemical itself causes curvature.
4. **Went (1926–28)** — the chemical collected into agar blocks bends
   decapitated *Avena* coleoptiles in proportion to concentration: he
   named it **auxin**, and the ***Avena* curvature test** became its
   bioassay.

Auxin travels by **polar (basipetal) transport**, cell to cell from tip
to base. **Roles:** cell elongation (acid growth in coleoptiles and young
stems); **apical dominance** — auxin from the apex suppresses axillary
buds, which is why pinching out a tea bush's tip makes it branch; root
initiation on cuttings; preventing premature leaf and fruit fall; and, at
fruit set, **parthenocarpy** (seedless fruit without fertilisation).
**Applications:** the synthetics **IBA and NAA** as rooting powders,
**NAA sprays** to stop pre-harvest fruit drop, auxin-induced seedless
cucumbers and tomatoes, and **2,4-D** as a **selective weedicide that
kills broad-leaved dicots while sparing cereals**.

::: caution Dose makes the poison
Auxin's dose–response is **bell-shaped** and roots are about a thousand
times more sensitive than shoots: the concentration that best elongates a
stem (about 10⁻⁵ M) **inhibits** roots, which are promoted only near
10⁻¹⁰–10⁻⁸ M. "Auxin promotes growth" is only half a sentence — and it
is why over-applied 2,4-D twists and kills.
:::

### Gibberellins

Over 130 are known; **gibberellic acid (GA₃)** is the famous one. The
story starts with **bakanae, the "foolish seedling" disease of rice** —
infected plants shoot up tall, pale and spindly, then collapse.
**Kurosawa (1926)** showed a cell-free filtrate of the fungus
***Gibberella fujikuroi*** reproduced the symptoms; **Yabuta and Sumiki
(1938)** crystallised the active substance.

**Roles:** **internode elongation** — a GA-sprayed dwarf pea reaches
normal height, the classic proof it replaces a missing hormone;
**bolting**, the sudden stem elongation of rosette plants (cabbage,
beet) before flowering; **breaking seed and bud dormancy**; switching on
the aleurone in germinating barley (below); delaying senescence in
citrus. **Applications:** spraying **Thompson Seedless grapes** to
enlarge berries and loosen bunches; boosting α-amylase in the **malting**
of barley for beer and jaand chhyang-type fermentations; uniform
germination of dormant seed lots.

### Cytokinins

**Miller and Skoog (1955)** isolated **kinetin** from **autoclaved
herring sperm DNA** — an artefact of DNA breakdown, not a natural plant
product. The first true natural cytokinin was **zeatin**, from the milky
endosperm of **maize** (**Letham, 1963**). Cytokinins are adenine
derivatives made chiefly in the **root apex** and carried up in the
**xylem**.

**Roles:** promotion of **cell division (cytokinesis)** — but only
**together with auxin**; breaking bud dormancy and **releasing lateral
buds from apical dominance** (the counterweight to auxin); and delaying
leaf senescence, the **Richmond–Lang effect** (a detached leaf with a
cytokinin-treated patch stays green there while the rest yellows).
**Applications:** tissue culture media, keeping cut greens and flowers
fresh, and promoting branching.

::: memory The ratio that builds a plant
In culture, the **auxin : cytokinin ratio** (Skoog and Miller, 1957)
decides the callus's fate — **high auxin → roots; high cytokinin →
shoots; balanced → callus**. It is the most commonly inverted fact in
the chapter; do not be the student who inverts it.
:::

| Hormone | Chemical nature | Main source | Signature functions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Auxin (IAA) | indole (from tryptophan) | shoot apex, young leaves | elongation, apical dominance, rooting |
| Gibberellin (GA₃) | diterpenoid acid | embryo, young leaves, roots | internode elongation, bolting, germination |
| Cytokinin (zeatin) | adenine derivative | root apex | cell division, bud release, anti-senescence |
| Abscisic acid | apocarotenoid | leaves, roots, seeds | dormancy, stomatal closure (Unit 11) |


::: example Worked example — choosing the hormone for the job
Match the horticultural task to the treatment, asking what each hormone *does*:

- **Root this rose cutting quickly** — an **auxin** (IBA/NAA) dip: auxins initiate adventitious roots.
- **Get seedless (parthenocarpic) grapes with larger berries** — **gibberellin** spray at flowering (auxins also help set parthenocarpic fruit, e.g. tomato).
- **Keep harvested leafy vegetables green longer** — **cytokinin**: it delays senescence by holding back protein/chlorophyll breakdown.
- **Make a "dwarf" rosette cabbage bolt and flower** — **gibberellin**, which overcomes genetic dwarfism by elongating internodes.
- **Kill broad-leaved weeds in a wheat field without harming the wheat** — a high dose of the synthetic auxin **2,4-D**: dicot weeds over-grow and die; the monocot crop is little affected.

Each answer falls out of the hormone's basic action — never memorise the pairings blindly.
:::

::: caution Trap — auxin as friend *and* poison
Auxin is a growth **promoter** at low concentration and a **herbicide** (2,4-D) at high concentration — the dose decides. Two more swaps to guard: **ethylene** ripens fruit and promotes abscission, while **ABA** is the stress/dormancy hormone that *closes* stomata and *antagonises* gibberellin in seeds.
:::

::: example Growth that doubles: reading a germination curve
**Problem.** In a germination trial, 80 of 100 sown seeds produce seedlings.
A seedling's dry mass rises from 0.5 g to 4.0 g over six days of feeding
itself on reserves. What is the germination percentage, and how many
doublings does the mass curve represent?
**Solution.** Germination = 80 ÷ 100 = **80 %** — the trial's headline
number. Mass ratio = 4.0 ÷ 0.5 = 8 = 2³, so **three doublings** in six days:
a doubling time of two days, the geometric signature of growth by cell
division — arithmetic growth (a constant addition per day) would look linear
instead of explosive, and seedlings are exponential until reserves or space
say otherwise.
:::
## 13.2 Seed germination and its types; seed dormancy

**Germination** is the resumption of active growth of the embryo,
ending when the seed coat ruptures and a root and shoot emerge. It runs
in three steps. First, **imbibition** — the dry seed drinks by matric
force (Unit 11) and swells. Then **activation**: respiration climbs,
enzymes are switched on and stored food is mobilised. Finally the
**radicle emerges first** (anchoring and drinking), protected by the
coleorhiza in grasses, and the plumule follows, hooded by the coleoptile
in monocots. Given viability, germination needs **water, oxygen** (for
the aerobic respiration that powers growth — rice is the famous partial
exception, its coleoptile elongating under floodwater) and a **suitable
temperature**. Some seeds additionally need **light** — positive
photoblastic seeds such as **lettuce** (red light via phytochrome
promotes, far-red inhibits) — and a few, such as *Nigella*, need
darkness.

The star mechanism is hormonal. The hydrated cereal embryo releases
**gibberellin**, which diffuses to the living **aleurone layer** around
the starchy endosperm; there it switches on synthesis of **α-amylase**
and other hydrolases, which digest the endosperm's starch to sugars that
feed the growing embryo. Gibberellin here is the **embryo's messenger to
its pantry** — and ABA blocks the message, which is the chemistry of
dormancy.

### Types of germination

- **Epigeal germination** — the **hypocotyl elongates** and hoists the
  **cotyledons above the soil**, where they may green and photosynthesise
  briefly: **bean (*Phaseolus*), castor, sunflower, mustard**.
- **Hypogeal germination** — the **epicotyl elongates** and the
  **cotyledons stay below ground**, still inside the seed: **pea, maize,
  gram, mango**.
- **Vivipary (viviparous germination)** — the seed germinates **while
  the fruit is still on the parent**, the radicle growing out before the
  propagule drops: the mangroves ***Rhizophora* and *Ceriops***, so the
  seedling spears into the tidal mud already rooted.

### Seed dormancy

A **quiescent** seed is merely waiting for water and warmth. A
**dormant** seed **fails to germinate even under favourable conditions**
because of a block inside itself. The causes:

- **Hard, impermeable seed coat** barring water or oxygen (many legumes,
  lotus).
- **Immature embryo** that must finish developing after shedding
  (morphological dormancy; many orchids, *Ginkgo*).
- **Physiological inhibition** — **ABA** and other inhibitors (coumarin,
  ferulic acid) hold the embryo back; the seed needs **after-ripening** —
  time, often moist cold, during which the **ABA : GA balance** tips
  towards GA.
- **Light or temperature requirements** (photoblastic seeds; species
  needing winter chill).

The corresponding cures, all used in nurseries and seed testing:

- **Scarification** — nicking, abrading or brief acid treatment of hard
  coats.
- **Stratification** — weeks of **moist chilling** (apple, peach and many
  temperate seeds).
- **Light exposure** (or darkness) for photoblastic seeds; alternating
  temperatures for others.
- **Gibberellin application**, which can substitute for the cold or
  light requirement; **leaching** inhibitors out with running water.

Dormancy is not failure — it is timing. It stops seeds germinating on
the parent or in a brief warm spell before winter, spreads germination
across years (the soil seed bank), and lets seeds survive dispersal and
drought; sacred lotus seeds have germinated after an estimated thousand
years. In agriculture the balance matters both ways: too little dormancy
gives wheat sprouting in the ear during a wet monsoon harvest; too much
gives uneven nursery stands.


::: caution Trap — epigeal or hypogeal? Ask where the cotyledons end up
If the **cotyledons rise above the soil** (hypocotyl elongates), germination is **epigeal**: bean, castor, sunflower, mustard. If they **stay below ground** (epicotyl elongates), it is **hypogeal**: pea, gram, maize, mango. The classic wrong pairing is bean — students invert it. Two neighbours: **vivipary** is germination while still on the mother plant (mangroves, e.g. *Rhizophora*), and dormancy is broken by scarification (hard coat), stratification (cold requirement) or gibberellin treatment.
:::

::: example Phytochrome switch and hormone ratio in organogenesis
**Problem.** Lettuce seeds receive a sequence of flashes: R – FR – R – FR – R, then are kept in the dark. Do they germinate? And in a callus medium, what auxin-to-cytokinin ratio induces shoot buds?

**Solution.** Germination depends only on the **last** flash: because the final flash is **red (R, 660 nm)**, $P_r$ is converted to active $P_{fr}$ and the seeds **germinate**. In Skoog–Miller callus culture, a **low auxin : high cytokinin** ratio induces **shoot morphogenesis** (caulogenesis).
:::

## Formula and fact sheet

- **Auxin chain**: Darwin (tip senses light) → Boysen-Jensen (diffusible) → Paál (chemical bends in dark) → Went (auxin, *Avena* curvature bioassay). IAA from tryptophan; polar basipetal transport.
- **Auxin jobs**: elongation, apical dominance, rooting (IBA, NAA), parthenocarpy, prevent fruit drop; 2,4-D kills dicot weeds in cereals; shoot optimum ≈10⁻⁵ M inhibits roots.
- **GA**: bakanae disease, *Gibberella fujikuroi*, Kurosawa 1926, Yabuta and Sumiki 1938; bolting, dwarf rescue, dormancy breaking, aleurone α-amylase, grapes/malting.
- **Cytokinin**: kinetin (Miller and Skoog 1955, herring sperm DNA), zeatin (Letham 1963, maize); root apex → xylem; division with auxin; Richmond–Lang anti-senescence.
- **Culture ratio**: high auxin = roots, high cytokinin = shoots, balanced = callus (Skoog and Miller 1957).
- **Germination**: imbibition → activation → radicle out first; needs water, O₂, suitable temperature; lettuce needs red light; embryo GA → aleurone → α-amylase → sugars.
- **Types**: epigeal = hypocotyl lifts cotyledons (bean, castor, sunflower); hypogeal = epicotyl rises, cotyledons buried (pea, maize, mango); vivipary = *Rhizophora*.
- **Dormancy**: dormant ≠ quiescent; causes hard coat, immature embryo, ABA/inhibitors, light/chill demands; cures scarification, stratification, light, GA, leaching; ABA : GA balance decides.

## Chapter summary

- Auxin (IAA) was hunted down through the Darwin–Boysen-Jensen–Paál–Went experiments; it elongates cells, enforces apical dominance, roots cuttings and, as 2,4-D, selectively kills dicot weeds — always with a bell-shaped, tissue-specific dose response.
- Gibberellins, discovered through the bakanae fungus, elongate internodes (curing genetic dwarfs), bolt rosettes, break dormancy and order the aleurone to make α-amylase.
- Cytokinins (kinetin, then natural zeatin from maize) drive cell division with auxin, free lateral buds, and delay senescence; the auxin : cytokinin ratio steers tissue culture between roots, shoots and callus.
- Germination begins with imbibition, is powered by aerobic respiration, and is fed when embryo gibberellin switches on the aleurone; the radicle leads the way out.
- Hypocotyl growth lifts the cotyledons in epigeal species (bean, castor), epicotyl growth leaves them buried in hypogeal ones (pea, maize), and mangroves skip the wait by vivipary.
- Dormant seeds refuse even perfect conditions because of impermeable coats, unfinished embryos or ABA-based inhibition; scarification, stratification, light, gibberellin and leaching remove the blocks nature uses to time germination.

   (a) seeds need no water (b) embryo needs no food (c) coleoptile can elongate under anaerobic conditions (d) radicle performs photosynthesis

   (a) ripens them faster (b) delays senescence and yellowing (c) closes their stomata permanently (d) kills surface fungi

   (a) gets too much light (b) is crushed by soil weight alone (c) lacks oxygen in all cases (d) exhausts its stored food before reaching the light

::: note Answers and explanations — Set B
**1.** (c) — Optima differ by orders of magnitude (shoot about 10⁻⁵ M, root about 10⁻¹⁰ M): one dose, opposite effects.
**2.** (a) — With the auxin source gone, root-born cytokinin frees the axillary buds — the pruner's whole trick.
**3.** (b) — Skoog and Miller: cytokinin dominance → shoots, auxin dominance → roots.
**4.** (a) — Exogenous GA substitutes for the defective step — a hormone-replacement proof.
**5.** (c) — Quiescence is waiting for conditions; dormancy is an internal block despite them.

**7.** (d) — GA induces de novo enzyme synthesis; the aleurone then exports amylase into the dead starchy endosperm.

**10.** (c) — Rice coleoptiles extend by fermentation-supported growth to reach air — an adaptation to paddies.
**11.** (b) — The Richmond–Lang effect keeps chlorophyll and protein from being dismantled.
**12.** (d) — Emergence runs on reserves; a long dark climb bankrupts a small seed.
