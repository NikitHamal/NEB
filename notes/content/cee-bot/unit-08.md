Molecular Genetics carries **2 of the 40 Botany questions**, and they are the most
number-dense two marks in the whole paper. One question is almost always a structural
fact — the width of the helix, the base pairs per turn, which bases are purines, how
many hydrogen bonds hold A to T — and the other is a piece of code arithmetic or a
one-line statement about replication, the enzymes or the properties of the genetic code.
No calculation here is longer than a division. Learn the numbers in the tables and the
three classic experiments, and this unit pays two marks for very little work.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- **The three experiments** — Griffith (transformation, 1928), Avery–MacLeod–McCarty (DNase destroys the principle, 1944), Hershey–Chase (³²P enters the cell, ³⁵S does not, 1952).
- **Nucleotide anatomy** — phosphate + pentose + base; purines A and G (two rings), pyrimidines C, T, U (one ring); phosphodiester bond; 5′→3′ direction.
- **The helix numbers** — 2 nm wide, 3.4 nm per turn, 10 bp per turn, 0.34 nm rise, antiparallel, A=T with **2** hydrogen bonds, G≡C with **3**.
- **Chargaff's rule** — A = T, G = C, purines = pyrimidines; arithmetic in one line.
- **The three RNAs** — mRNA (codons), tRNA (cloverleaf, anticodon, CCA end), rRNA (most abundant, catalytic).
- **Semi-conservative replication** — Meselson–Stahl and the ¹⁵N bands; helicase, primase, DNA polymerase III and I, ligase; leading versus lagging and Okazaki fragments.
- **The code** — triplet, 64 codons, 61 sense, AUG start, UAA/UAG/UGA stop, degenerate but not ambiguous, non-overlapping, comma-less, universal.
- **Translation** — ribosome A, P and E sites; mRNA read 5′→3′; peptide bond made by rRNA.
:::

## 8.1 Composition, structure and functions of DNA and RNA

### The evidence that DNA is the genetic material

By 1940 most biologists assumed the genetic material was protein, because proteins are
made of twenty different building blocks and DNA of only four. Three experiments
destroyed that assumption. Each is a favourite of the paper, and each is worth one
sentence in your memory.

**Griffith, 1928 — transformation.** Frederick Griffith worked with
*Streptococcus pneumoniae*, the pneumococcus. The **S strain** has a polysaccharide
capsule, forms smooth colonies and kills a mouse; the **R strain** has no capsule,
forms rough colonies and is harmless. Griffith found that mice injected with
**heat-killed S bacteria plus live R bacteria** died, and live S bacteria could be
recovered from their blood. Something released by the dead S cells had permanently
changed the harmless R cells into virulent S cells. He called it the **transforming
principle** — but he did not know what it was. Note the logic carefully: neither
component alone kills; the mixture does. That is the evidence.

**Avery, MacLeod and McCarty, 1944 — the principle is DNA.** Oswald Avery's group
purified the transforming principle from heat-killed S cells and then destroyed each
class of molecule in turn. Digested with **protease** (protein destroyed) the extract
still transformed. Digested with **ribonuclease** (RNA destroyed) it still transformed.
Digested with **deoxyribonuclease** (DNA destroyed) transformation stopped completely.
The conclusion was that the transforming principle is DNA.

**Hershey and Chase, 1952 — the label goes with the DNA.** Alfred Hershey and Martha
Chase used the **T2 bacteriophage**, which is nothing but a DNA molecule inside a
protein coat. They grew one batch of phage in medium containing **³⁵S**, which is
built into the sulphur-containing amino acids of protein but not into DNA, and another
in **³²P**, which is built into the phosphate backbone of DNA but not into protein.
Each batch was allowed to infect *E. coli*. A kitchen **blender** then sheared the
empty coats off the bacterial surface, and centrifugation packed the bacteria into a
pellet. Almost all the **³²P** was inside the pellet with the bacteria; almost all the
**³⁵S** stayed in the supernatant with the coats. Since the injected material directs
the making of new phage, the genetic material is **DNA**.

| Experiment | Organism | The decisive observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Griffith (1928) | *Streptococcus pneumoniae* | heat-killed S + live R kills the mouse; live S recovered | a "transforming principle" exists |
| Avery, MacLeod, McCarty (1944) | *S. pneumoniae* extract | DNase stops transformation; protease and RNase do not | the principle is **DNA** |
| Hershey–Chase (1952) | T2 phage and *E. coli* | ³²P enters the bacterium, ³⁵S stays outside | **DNA** carries the instructions |
| Indirect evidence | all cells | DNA amount is constant for a species, halves in gametes, doubles in S phase; DNA absorbs ultraviolet at 260 nm, the most mutagenic wavelength | DNA is the hereditary material |

::: caution Three statements the paper likes to distort
1. Griffith did **not** identify DNA. He showed only that a principle existed; Avery's
   group named it.
2. Hershey and Chase labelled **protein with ³⁵S** and **DNA with ³²P** — not the other
   way round. Sulphur is in cysteine and methionine; phosphorus is in the backbone.
3. The blender does not break the bacteria open. It only removes the empty coats from
   the outside of intact cells; if the cells were broken, the experiment would prove
   nothing.
:::

### The nucleotide: sugar, phosphate and base

DNA and RNA are **polynucleotides** — chains of nucleotides. One **nucleotide** has
three parts joined by two bonds:

- a **pentose sugar** — 2-deoxy-D-ribose in DNA (C₅H₁₀O₄), D-ribose in RNA (C₅H₁₀O₅);
- a **phosphate group** attached to carbon 5′ of the sugar;
- a **nitrogenous base** attached to carbon 1′ of the sugar.

A **nucleoside** is the sugar plus the base, without the phosphate. Adding the
phosphate to a nucleoside gives the nucleotide: adenosine → adenosine monophosphate
(AMP). Successive nucleotides are joined when the **3′-OH** of one sugar makes a
**phosphodiester bond** with the **5′-phosphate** of the next. The result is a
sugar–phosphate backbone with the bases projecting from it, and the chain has a
chemical direction: a free phosphate at the **5′ end** and a free hydroxyl at the
**3′ end**. Sequences are always written 5′ → 3′.

The two families of bases are told apart by their rings:

| Family | Ring structure | Members | Found in |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Purines** | **two** fused rings (a six-membered pyrimidine ring fused to a five-membered imidazole ring); 9 atoms | **adenine (A), guanine (G)** | DNA and RNA |
| **Pyrimidines** | **one** six-membered ring; 6 atoms | **cytosine (C)** | DNA and RNA |
| | | **thymine (T)** | DNA only |
| | | **uracil (U)** | RNA only |

Thymine is 5-methyluracil; RNA uses uracil in its place, and uracil lacks the methyl
group. In double-stranded DNA the number of purines always equals the number of
pyrimidines, because every purine is paired with a pyrimidine.

::: memory Purines and pyrimidines
**PURe As Gold** — **A**denine and **G**uanine are the **purines** (two rings, like a
big word). **CUT the PY** — **C**ytosine, **U**racil and **T**hymine are the
**pyrimidines** (one ring). Purine pairs with pyrimidine so that every rung of the
ladder is the same width.
:::

### The Watson–Crick double helix

James Watson and Francis Crick published the model in *Nature* in **1953**. They used
three things: the X-ray diffraction photographs of **Rosalind Franklin** and Maurice
Wilkins (Franklin's "Photograph 51" showed a helix), the **Chargaff ratios**, and the
chemistry of the bases. The model has these features, and the paper asks for them one
by one:

1. Two polynucleotide chains wind round a common axis in a **right-handed** helix.
2. The chains are **antiparallel** — one runs 5′ → 3′ upwards, the other 5′ → 3′ downwards.
3. The **sugar–phosphate backbones lie outside**, and the **bases point inwards**, paired in the middle.
4. Each base pair is one purine with one pyrimidine, held by hydrogen bonds:
   **A pairs with T by two** hydrogen bonds, **G pairs with C by three**.
5. The two strands are **complementary**: the sequence of one fixes the sequence of the other. This is what makes faithful copying possible.
6. The helix has a **major groove** and a **minor groove**, where proteins bind.

| Quantity | Value | Useful arithmetic |
|---|---|---|
| Diameter of the helix | **2 nm** | — |
| Pitch (one complete turn) | **3.4 nm** | turns = length ÷ 3.4 nm |
| Base pairs per turn | **10** | base pairs = turns × 10 |
| Rise per base pair | **0.34 nm** | length = base pairs × 0.34 nm |
| Handedness | right-handed (B-DNA) | — |
| A=T hydrogen bonds | **2** | AT pairs × 2 |
| G≡C hydrogen bonds | **3** | GC pairs × 3 |

::: caution The two traps in the helix numbers
1. **A=T is two hydrogen bonds; G≡C is three.** Students reverse them constantly. The
   consequence is the exam's favourite follow-up: a **GC-rich DNA is more stable and
   melts at a higher temperature**, because each GC pair has one more hydrogen bond
   than an AT pair. Heating DNA separates the strands — *denaturation* or melting —
   and the absorbance at 260 nm rises (the hyperchromic effect). Cooling lets the
   strands re-pair (annealing).
2. **0.34 nm is the rise per base pair; 3.4 nm is the whole turn.** A question that
   gives a length in millimetres and asks for the number of turns wants division by
   3.4 nm, not by 0.34 nm.
:::

**Other forms of DNA.** B-DNA is the Watson–Crick form and the one in living cells.
A-DNA is also right-handed but shorter and fatter (11 bp per turn) and forms in
dehydrated samples. Z-DNA is **left-handed**, with a zigzag backbone (12 bp per turn).
Prokaryotes usually carry one circular DNA molecule; eukaryotes carry linear
chromosomes, in which the DNA is wound round histone octamers into nucleosomes and
then coiled into chromatin (Chapter 7).

### Chargaff's rule and its arithmetic

Erwin Chargaff analysed DNA from many species in 1950 and found, without knowing the
structure, that the bases are not present in equal amounts but are present in fixed
**pairs**:

- **%A = %T** and **%G = %C**, so **A + G = T + C** — purines equal pyrimidines.
- The **A + T to G + C ratio** differs from species to species, but is constant within a species.

Every Chargaff question is solved in two lines. If one base is given, subtract its
partner, halve the remainder, and remember that the four percentages add to 100.

::: example Problem. A double-stranded DNA sample contains 28 % adenine. Find the percentage of each base, and the number of hydrogen bonds in a 200 base-pair fragment of it.
**Solution.** A pairs with T, so T = 28 %. Then A + T = 56 %, leaving 44 % for G + C.
Since G = C, each is 44 ÷ 2 = **22 %**.
In 200 bp there are 400 bases: A = T = 0.28 × 400 = **112**, G = C = **88**.
Each AT pair has 2 hydrogen bonds and each GC pair has 3, and the pairs equal the
counts of A and G:
H = 112 × 2 + 88 × 3 = 224 + 264 = **488 hydrogen bonds.**
:::

::: example Problem. The haploid human genome contains 3.2 × 10⁹ base pairs. What is the total length of DNA in one diploid body cell, and how many turns of the helix does it contain?
**Solution.** A diploid cell has 2 × 3.2 × 10⁹ = 6.4 × 10⁹ bp.
Length = 6.4 × 10⁹ × 0.34 nm = 2.18 × 10⁹ nm = **2.18 m** (about 2 m — the figure to quote).
Turns = base pairs ÷ 10 = 6.4 × 10⁹ ÷ 10 = **6.4 × 10⁸ turns.**
(Equivalently 2.18 m ÷ 3.4 nm gives the same number.)
:::

### Functions of DNA

- **Storage of information.** The base sequence is the information; a gene is a length of DNA that specifies one RNA or protein.
- **Replication.** Before a cell divides the DNA copies itself semi-conservatively, so both daughters receive a complete set.
- **Control of the cell.** Through transcription into RNA and then translation into protein, DNA decides the enzymes a cell makes and therefore its structure and behaviour.
- **Mutation and variation.** Rare copying errors change the sequence and provide the raw material of evolution.
- **Inheritance.** The gametes carry half the DNA of a body cell, so the information passes from parent to offspring.

### RNA: the three types and their roles

RNA differs from DNA in four ways that the paper tests directly: it has **ribose** in
place of deoxyribose, **uracil** in place of thymine, it is usually **single-stranded**
(though it folds back on itself), and it is **shorter and far less stable** — the
2′-OH group of ribose makes RNA easy to hydrolyse, especially in alkali. Its single
strandedness and its exposed bases let it fold into shapes and act as a catalyst.

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | 2-deoxy-D-ribose | D-ribose (2′-OH present) |
| Bases | A, G, C, **T** | A, G, C, **U** |
| Strands | two, antiparallel | usually one, folded |
| Size | very long (millions of bp) | short (70 – 10 000 nucleotides) |
| Location | mainly nucleus/chromosomes; also mitochondria and chloroplasts | nucleus and cytoplasm |
| Stability | stable | unstable, short-lived |
| Functions | store and transmit information | read the information and build protein; genetic material in some viruses |

| RNA | Share of cell RNA | Structure | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **mRNA** (messenger) | about 5 % | single strand, carries **codons**; in eukaryotes capped and poly-A tailed | carries the message from DNA to the ribosome — the only RNA that is translated |
| **tRNA** (transfer) | about 15 % | **75–90 nucleotides**, folded into a **cloverleaf**; four arms — acceptor (with the **CCA-3′** end that carries the amino acid), D arm, **anticodon** arm, T arm; L-shaped in three dimensions | the **adaptor**: reads the codon by its anticodon and delivers the matching amino acid |
| **rRNA** (ribosomal) | about **80 %** — the most abundant | part of the ribosome; eukaryote 28S, 18S, 5.8S and 5S; prokaryote 23S, 16S and 5S | structural framework of the ribosome, and the **peptidyl transferase** that makes the peptide bond — a ribozyme |

Small nuclear RNA (snRNA) helps splice introns out of mRNA; micro-RNA regulates
translation. In some viruses — tobacco mosaic virus, poliovirus, influenza, HIV — **RNA
is itself the genetic material**, which is the exception to "DNA is the genetic
material".

::: key tRNA in one line
The **anticodon** of tRNA is complementary and antiparallel to the **codon** of mRNA,
and the amino acid sits at the **3′-CCA end**. One end reads the message, the other
end carries the cargo. That is why tRNA is called an adaptor: it converts a
three-letter nucleic-acid word into one amino acid.
:::

## 8.2 DNA replication; the central dogma; the genetic code

### Semi-conservative replication

Three models were possible. In **conservative** replication the parent double helix
stays intact and an entirely new one is made. In **semi-conservative** replication the
two strands separate and each acts as a template, so every daughter molecule has one
old strand and one new one. In **dispersive** replication the old and new material is
broken into pieces and mixed.

**Meselson and Stahl, 1958** settled it with *E. coli* and a heavy isotope of
nitrogen. Bacteria were grown for many generations in **¹⁵N** (as ¹⁵NH₄Cl), so all
their DNA was heavy. They were then transferred to ordinary **¹⁴N** medium, and samples
were taken after each division. DNA was extracted and spun in a **caesium chloride
density gradient**, where DNA bands at the position matching its density.

- **Before the transfer:** one heavy band at the bottom.
- **After one division:** **one band of intermediate (hybrid) density**, in the middle.
  This rules out conservative replication, which would have given two bands, one heavy
  and one light.
- **After two divisions:** **two bands in equal amounts**, one hybrid and one light.
  This rules out dispersive replication, which would have given a single band drifting
  upwards.

Only semi-conservative replication predicts exactly this. The same result was shown in
plant roots by Taylor, Woods and Hughes (1957) using tritiated thymidine in *Vicia faba*.

### The machinery of replication

Replication starts at an **origin** (one in a bacterial chromosome, many in a
eukaryotic chromosome), and proceeds **bidirectionally**, so a replication bubble
opens with a **fork** at each end. In eukaryotes it happens in the **S phase** of the
cell cycle. Every enzyme has one job, and the paper asks "which enzyme does this?":

| Protein | Job |
|---|---|
| **DNA helicase** | unwinds the double helix at the fork, breaking the hydrogen bonds, using ATP |
| Single-strand binding proteins | hold the separated strands apart so they cannot re-pair |
| **Topoisomerase** (DNA gyrase in bacteria) | relieves the supercoiling ahead of the fork |
| **Primase** | an RNA polymerase that lays down a short **RNA primer** |
| **DNA polymerase III** | the main enzyme: adds deoxyribonucleotides to the free 3′-OH, synthesising **5′ → 3′**; proofreads with its 3′ → 5′ exonuclease activity |
| **DNA polymerase I** | removes the RNA primer (5′ → 3′ exonuclease) and fills the gap with DNA |
| **DNA ligase** | seals the nick between fragments, making the last phosphodiester bond; uses ATP |

::: caution Why a primer is needed
DNA polymerase can only **add** a nucleotide to an existing free **3′-OH** group; it
cannot start a chain on a bare template. Primase therefore makes a short RNA primer
first, which DNA polymerase I later removes and replaces with DNA. A question that says
"DNA polymerase can synthesise DNA in the absence of a primer" is false.
:::

### Leading and lagging strands

Because the two parental strands are **antiparallel**, and because DNA polymerase can
only work 5′ → 3′, the two new strands cannot be made the same way.

- The **leading strand** is made **continuously**, in the same direction as the fork is
  moving, from a single primer.
- The **lagging strand** is made **discontinuously**, in short pieces that grow *away*
  from the fork, each started by its own primer. These pieces are the **Okazaki
  fragments** — about 100–200 nucleotides long in eukaryotes and 1000–2000 in bacteria.
  DNA polymerase I replaces their RNA primers and **DNA ligase** joins them into one strand.

So each fork produces one continuous and one fragmented strand. The lagging strand is
the slower and more complicated of the two, and it is where the primers and the ligase
are needed.

### Transcription in outline

**Transcription** is the making of an RNA copy of one gene. **RNA polymerase** binds a
**promoter** just upstream of the gene, unwinds a short stretch, and builds RNA
**5′ → 3′** by reading the **template strand** in the 3′ → 5′ direction. The other
strand, the **coding (sense) strand**, has the same sequence as the RNA except that T
replaces U. No primer is needed, and only one strand of the gene is copied. The enzyme
stops at a **terminator**.

In eukaryotes there are three polymerases: **RNA polymerase I** makes the large rRNA,
**RNA polymerase II** makes mRNA, and **RNA polymerase III** makes tRNA and 5S rRNA.
The mRNA is then processed: a **5′ cap** of 7-methylguanosine, a **3′ poly-A tail**, and
**splicing** out of introns by the spliceosome.

| Point | Replication | Transcription |
|---|---|---|
| What is copied | the whole chromosome | one gene |
| Strands used as template | both | one |
| Product | DNA | RNA |
| Enzyme | DNA polymerase | RNA polymerase |
| Primer | needed | not needed |
| Base pairing with A | T | U |
| When | S phase | throughout the cell cycle, as the gene is needed |

### The central dogma

Francis Crick stated the **central dogma** in 1958: information flows
**DNA → RNA → protein**. Replication is DNA → DNA; transcription is DNA → RNA;
translation is RNA → protein. The transfer is one-way in cells — protein sequence does
not flow back into nucleic acid.

There are two real exceptions, and the paper likes them:

- **Reverse transcription**, RNA → DNA, catalysed by **reverse transcriptase** in
  retroviruses such as HIV. It is the basis of the enzyme used in the laboratory to make
  cDNA from mRNA.
- **RNA replication**, RNA → RNA, in some RNA viruses.

### The genetic code

The code was broken between 1961 and 1966. **Nirenberg and Matthaei** made a synthetic
mRNA of only uracil (poly-U) and got a polypeptide of only phenylalanine, so UUU codes
for Phe. Khorana then made RNAs of known repeating sequence, and within five years all
64 codons had been assigned.

| Property | What it means | Consequence the paper tests |
|---|---|---|
| **Triplet** | three bases code for one amino acid | 4³ = **64** codons for 20 amino acids |
| **61 sense + 3 stop** | **UAA, UAG, UGA** do not code for an amino acid | they end translation |
| **Start codon AUG** | also codes methionine | the first amino acid of nearly every protein |
| **Degenerate (redundant)** | most amino acids have more than one codon; Leu, Ser and Arg have six, Met and Trp have one | a base change may not change the protein |
| **Unambiguous** | one codon never specifies two amino acids | the message has only one reading |
| **Non-overlapping** | each base belongs to one codon only | a change affects one amino acid at most |
| **Comma-less** | no bases are wasted between codons | the reading frame runs continuously |
| **Universal** | the same in almost all organisms, from bacteria to humans | a human gene can be expressed in a bacterium |

::: caution Degenerate is not ambiguous
These two words are the commonest confusion in the unit. **Degenerate** means *many
codons → one amino acid* (Leu has six codons). **Ambiguous** would mean *one codon →
many amino acids*, and that never happens. A question that says "the code is degenerate,
so one codon can code for more than one amino acid" is false on its second half.
:::

The **wobble hypothesis** (Crick, 1966) explains why a cell needs fewer than 61 tRNAs:
the pairing between the **third base of the codon** and the **first base of the
anticodon** is loose, so one tRNA can read several codons that differ only in that
position. The first two bases pair strictly.

### Translation

**Translation** turns the base sequence of mRNA into the amino acid sequence of a
polypeptide. It happens on the **ribosome** — 70S (50S + 30S) in prokaryotes, 80S
(60S + 40S) in eukaryotes — and it reads the mRNA **5′ → 3′**.

1. **Activation.** Each amino acid is joined to its tRNA by an aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase, using ATP. The amino acid is attached to the **CCA-3′ end**.
2. **Initiation.** The small subunit binds the mRNA, the initiator tRNA carrying methionine pairs with the **AUG** start codon, and the large subunit joins. This tRNA sits in the **P site**.
3. **Elongation.** The next codon lies in the **A site**; the matching aminoacyl-tRNA binds; **peptidyl transferase** — the rRNA of the large subunit — forms the peptide bond; then the ribosome **translocates** one codon along, so the tRNA that has given up its amino acid moves to the **E (exit) site** and leaves.
4. **Termination.** A stop codon enters the A site, a release factor binds, and the polypeptide, mRNA and ribosomal subunits separate.

| Ribosomal site | What sits there |
|---|---|
| **A** (aminoacyl) | the incoming aminoacyl-tRNA, reading the new codon |
| **P** (peptidyl) | the tRNA holding the growing polypeptide chain |
| **E** (exit) | the tRNA that has lost its amino acid, about to leave |

Several ribosomes may travel along one mRNA at once, forming a **polysome**, so many
copies of the protein are made from one message.

::: example Problem. A gene has the template strand 3′-TAC GGA TTC ACT-5′. (i) Write the mRNA and the polypeptide. (ii) What happens if the fifth base from the left is deleted?
**Solution.**
(i) The mRNA is complementary and antiparallel to the template, with U in place of T:
5′-AUG CCU AAG UGA-3′. Reading in triplets: **AUG** = Met, **CCU** = Pro, **AAG** = Lys,
**UGA** = stop. The polypeptide is **Met–Pro–Lys**, three amino acids.
(ii) Deleting the fifth base (the second G) gives the template 3′-TAC GAT TCA CT-5′ and
the mRNA 5′-AUG CUA AGU GA…-3′. The codons are now **AUG CUA AGU**, that is
**Met–Leu–Ser**, and there is no stop codon in the same place. This is a **frameshift**:
one base lost shifts every later codon by one, so almost the whole protein changes.
A substitution, by contrast, usually changes one codon only, and often not even that,
because the code is degenerate.
:::

::: example Problem. A polypeptide contains 90 amino acids. What is the minimum number of nucleotides in the coding region of its mRNA, including the stop codon?
**Solution.** Each amino acid needs one codon of three bases: 90 × 3 = 270. The stop
codon adds three more: 270 + 3 = **273 nucleotides.** (The start codon is already
counted, because the first methionine is one of the 90.)
:::

::: example Problem. An *E. coli* chromosome has 4.6 × 10⁶ base pairs. Replication starts at one origin and each of the two forks moves at 1000 nucleotides per second. How long does replication take?
**Solution.** Two forks copy the chromosome at the same time, so the rate is
2 × 1000 = 2000 nucleotides per second.
Time = 4.6 × 10⁶ ÷ 2000 = 2300 s = **about 38 minutes.**
:::

## Chapter summary

- Griffith showed that a heat-killed virulent pneumococcus could transform a live avirulent one; Avery's group proved with DNase that the principle is DNA; Hershey and Chase proved with ³²P and ³⁵S that DNA, not protein, enters the bacterium.
- A nucleotide is phosphate + pentose + base; purines (A, G) have two rings and pyrimidines (C, T, U) one; nucleotides are joined by phosphodiester bonds into a 5′ → 3′ chain.
- The Watson–Crick helix is right-handed and antiparallel, 2 nm wide, 3.4 nm per turn, 10 bp per turn and 0.34 nm per base pair, with A=T (2 hydrogen bonds) and G≡C (3 hydrogen bonds) — so GC-rich DNA is more stable.
- Chargaff: A = T, G = C, so purines = pyrimidines; one given percentage fixes the other three.
- mRNA carries codons, tRNA is the cloverleaf adaptor with an anticodon and a CCA-3′ amino-acid end, and rRNA is the most abundant RNA and the catalyst that forms peptide bonds.
- Replication is semi-conservative: Meselson and Stahl's ¹⁵N experiment gave one hybrid band after one division and equal hybrid and light bands after two.
- Helicase unwinds, primase primes, DNA polymerase III extends 5′ → 3′, DNA polymerase I replaces the primer and ligase seals the nicks; the leading strand is continuous and the lagging strand is a series of Okazaki fragments.
- Transcription copies one gene with RNA polymerase and needs no primer; the central dogma is DNA → RNA → protein, with reverse transcription as the exception.
- The code is a triplet code of 64 codons — 61 sense, AUG start, UAA/UAG/UGA stop — degenerate, unambiguous, non-overlapping, comma-less and universal.
- Translation reads mRNA 5′ → 3′ on the ribosome through the A, P and E sites; a frameshift changes every codon after the mutation, a substitution usually changes one.

::: note Answers and explanations — Set A
**1.** (b) — Neither heat-killed S nor live R alone killed the mouse; the mixture did, and live S was recovered. Griffith did not identify the substance — that was Avery's group.
**2.** (a) — DNase destroys DNA, and transformation stopped; protease and RNase had no effect, so protein and RNA were excluded.
**3.** (c) — Phosphorus is in the DNA backbone, so ³²P labels DNA; ³⁵S labels the sulphur amino acids of the protein coat.
**4.** (d) — Adenine and guanine have the two-ring purine structure. Thymine, cytosine and uracil are single-ring pyrimidines.
**5.** (b) — A nucleoside is sugar + base. Adding a phosphate makes it a nucleotide.
**6.** (c) — The 3′-OH of one sugar bonds to the 5′-phosphate of the next through a phosphodiester bond; hydrogen bonds hold the two strands together, not the backbone.
**7.** (a) — B-DNA is 2 nm across. 0.34 nm is the rise per base pair and 3.4 nm the pitch of one turn.
**8.** (d) — Ten base pairs per turn, each rising 0.34 nm, gives 10 × 0.34 = 3.4 nm.
**9.** (c) — A=T has two hydrogen bonds; G≡C has three, which is why GC-rich DNA is harder to melt.
**10.** (b) — Every A is paired with a T and every G with a C, so the two are equal and purines equal pyrimidines.
**11.** (a) — Rosalind Franklin's photograph 51, with Wilkins's data, showed the helical pattern. Chargaff supplied the base ratios, not the diffraction data.
**12.** (c) — rRNA is about 80 % of the RNA of a cell because every ribosome is mostly rRNA. mRNA is only about 5 %.
**13.** (d) — The anticodon of tRNA is complementary and antiparallel to the mRNA codon; the codon itself is on mRNA.
**14.** (b) — UAA, UAG and UGA are the three stop codons. AUG is the start; UGG is tryptophan; GUG is valine.
**15.** (c) — AUG is both the start signal and the codon for methionine, so nearly every polypeptide begins with methionine.
**16.** (a) — Four bases in triplets give 4³ = 64 codons: 61 sense and 3 stop.
**17.** (b) — ¹⁵N makes DNA heavy, and the bands in the caesium chloride gradient show how the old and new strands are distributed.
**18.** (d) — DNA ligase makes the final phosphodiester bond between adjacent fragments. Primase only makes the RNA primer.
**19.** (c) — Helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds and separates the strands; topoisomerase only relieves the supercoiling ahead of it.
**20.** (a) — Information flows from DNA to RNA to protein: replication, transcription, translation.
**21.** (d) — Reverse transcriptase copies an RNA template into DNA; retroviruses such as HIV use it to make DNA from their RNA genome.
**22.** (b) — RNA polymerase II transcribes mRNA; I makes the large rRNA and III makes tRNA and 5S rRNA.
**23.** (c) — The 5′ end carries the phosphate and the 3′ end the free hydroxyl, which is why new nucleotides are always added at the 3′ end.

   (a) break open the bacteria (b) release the DNA from the phage (c) mix the two isotopes evenly (d) shear the empty phage coats off the bacterial surface

   (a) they contain different genes (b) different genes are expressed in them (c) their DNA contains different bases (d) their ribosomes are different
14. The genetic code is described as non-overlapping because
   (a) no base is used twice anywhere in the mRNA (b) the codons are separated by punctuation (c) each base is read in one codon only, and the next codon follows at once (d) no two codons share an amino acid

::: note Answers and explanations — Set B
**1.** (c) — The 2′-OH of ribose makes RNA attack its own backbone, so RNA is short-lived. DNA has deoxyribose, no 2′-OH, and a second strand to check the sequence against.
**2.** (a) — Three hydrogen bonds per GC pair against two per AT pair means more energy is needed to separate the strands. Base size and position play no part.
**3.** (d) — Conservative replication would have kept the heavy parent intact and produced a separate light molecule, giving two bands. One hybrid band means each daughter molecule has one old and one new strand.
**4.** (b) — Polymerase works only 5′ → 3′, and on the lagging template that direction points away from the fork, so the new strand has to be restarted repeatedly.
**5.** (c) — It extends a chain; it cannot begin one. Primase therefore lays an RNA primer that supplies the first 3′-OH.
**6.** (a) — Degeneracy is several codons for one amino acid, as when four codons specify proline. One codon specifying several amino acids would be ambiguity, and the code is not ambiguous.
**7.** (b) — The third base is the wobble position and is often interchangeable, so UUU and UUC both give phenylalanine.
**8.** (d) — Codons are read continuously from the start, so losing one base moves every later triplet by one place and changes all the amino acids downstream.
**9.** (c) — Its anticodon reads a nucleic-acid codon and its CCA end carries the corresponding amino acid; it is the physical link between the two languages.
**10.** (b) — The wobble hypothesis: the third codon base pairs loosely with the first anticodon base, so one tRNA serves several codons.
**11.** (a) — The 2′-OH can attack the phosphodiester bond, so alkali breaks RNA quickly; DNA lacks this group and survives.
**12.** (d) — The bacteria were left intact; the blender only detached the empty coats so that they could be separated from the cells by centrifugation.
**13.** (b) — Every body cell has the same genes; what differs is which genes are switched on, so a leaf cell and a root cell make different proteins.
**14.** (c) — Each base is read once, in one triplet, and the next triplet follows immediately with no bases skipped — that is also why the code is comma-less.
:::

::: note Answers and explanations — Set C
**1.** (d) — C = G = 20 %, so G + C = 40 % and A + T = 60 %; since A = T, A = 30 %.
**2.** (b) — 60 GC pairs × 3 = 180 and 40 AT pairs × 2 = 80; total 180 + 80 = 260 hydrogen bonds.
**3.** (a) — A diploid cell has 6.4 × 10⁹ bp, and 6.4 × 10⁹ × 0.34 nm = 2.18 × 10⁹ nm = 2.18 m. Option (b) is the haploid figure.

**5.** (d) — AUG = Met, CCU = Pro, AAG = Lys, and UGA stops. Three amino acids are made; the stop codon adds none.
**6.** (b) — The mRNA is complementary and antiparallel to the template: T→A, A→U, C→G, G→C, giving AUG CCU AAG. Option (a) is a DNA copy, not RNA.
**7.** (c) — 1.36 mm = 1.36 × 10⁶ nm; dividing by the 3.4 nm pitch gives 4 × 10⁵ turns. Dividing by 0.34 nm would wrongly count base pairs.

**9.** (b) — G = C = 30 %, so G + C = 60 % and A + T = 40 %; A = T = 20 %.
