Comprehension carries about **4 of the 16 English questions** (our estimate) and mixes General English and Technical English passages. In the official model paper one passage with a Section B question (2 marks) is printed: a passage on how English words label animals, with the question "In the English language the animals are used for unpleasant comparisons". Passage questions are the most *learnable* English marks: there is no unseen rule, only a method.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- The main idea / purpose of the passage and the best title.
- A fact stated directly (scan the text) versus a conclusion that has to be inferred.
- The meaning of a word or phrase *in context*.
- The writer's tone and attitude; what is *not* mentioned (NOT / EXCEPT).
- Technical passages: define, compare, cause and effect, sequence of a process.
:::

## 4.1 Comprehension of reading passages: General and Technical English

### Method for a two-minute passage

1. **Read the questions first** (not the options) so you know what to look for.
2. **Read the passage once** for the main idea; underline the first and last sentences of paragraphs, which usually state the argument and its conclusion.
3. **For each question**, find the sentence in the passage that answers it; do not answer from memory or from outside knowledge. Eliminate options that are *true but not stated*, *too extreme* (always, never, only, all), or *opposite*.
4. **Vocabulary-in-context:** replace the word with each option and see which keeps the sentence meaning.
5. **Inference:** the answer must follow *necessarily* from the text; the passage does not need to say it in the same words.
6. **Tone words:** *critical, objective, optimistic, ironic, sympathetic, cautionary*.

Time: at most 2 minutes per Section B question; a long passage at 2 marks is a *quick* mark once you have the method.

### Question types, in order of difficulty

| Type | How to answer |
|---|---|
| detail ("According to the passage…") | locate and match the text |
| main idea / title | cover every paragraph, not one detail |
| vocabulary in context | substitute and test |
| inference ("It can be inferred…") | the one option that must be true |
| purpose / tone | what the writer is *doing*: warning, explaining, persuading |
| NOT / EXCEPT | find the three true statements; the fourth is the answer |

### Technical English passages

They explain a device, a process, a material, a safety rule or a measurement. Expect: definitions (*is defined as*), classification (*two types*), process sequence (*first, then, finally*), cause and effect (*as a result, because*), comparison (*whereas, unlike*), numerical data. Read the numbers carefully; one option often reverses a comparison (*higher* versus *lower*).

## Practice passages

### Passage 1 — General English

> Cities have always been shaped by the way people move. The earliest towns were small enough to cross on foot, so shops, homes and workplaces stood close together. When the railway arrived, towns stretched along the tracks, and when the car became common, they spread out into wide suburbs that most people could only reach by driving. Planners now see the cost: long commutes, polluted air and roads so crowded that new lanes fill up within a few years of opening. Several cities have therefore begun to reverse the trend. They are building cycle lanes, protecting pavements and encouraging homes, offices and markets to share the same neighbourhoods. Early results suggest that such changes do not slow business; shops on pedestrian streets often report more customers, not fewer. The lesson is not that cars should disappear, but that a city designed around the car alone leaves many of its residents worse off.

**Questions**

1. The passage is mainly about
   (a) how transport has shaped cities and why some now change course (b) the history of the railway (c) the dangers of cycling (d) the economics of car manufacturing
2. According to the passage, early towns had homes and workplaces close together because
   (a) land was cheap (b) there were no cars (c) people travelled on foot (d) planners ordered it
3. The word "reverse" in the passage most nearly means
   (a) copy (b) turn back (c) accelerate (d) ignore
4. Which of the following is NOT mentioned as a cost of car-based planning?
   (a) long commutes (b) polluted air (c) crowded roads (d) higher rents
5. The writer would most likely agree that
   (a) cars should be banned from cities (b) pedestrian streets harm local trade (c) a city built only for cars disadvantages many residents (d) railways caused the suburbs

::: note Answers and explanations — Passage 1
**1.** (a) — the passage traces transport and its effect on city shape, then describes the change of direction.
**2.** (c) — "small enough to cross on foot".
**3.** (b) — "reverse the trend" means turn it back.
**4.** (d) — long commutes, polluted air and crowded roads are named; rents are not.
**5.** (c) — the last sentence says exactly this; (a) is explicitly rejected ("not that cars should disappear"), (b) is contradicted.
:::

### Passage 2 — Technical English

> A transformer changes the voltage of an alternating current without changing its frequency. It consists of two coils of insulated wire, the primary and the secondary, wound on a common core made of thin laminated sheets of soft iron. When an alternating voltage is applied to the primary, it produces a changing magnetic flux in the core, and this flux induces a voltage in the secondary. The ratio of the voltages is equal to the ratio of the number of turns in the two coils. A step-up transformer has more turns in the secondary and raises the voltage, whereas a step-down transformer does the opposite. Because the power cannot increase, a rise in voltage is always accompanied by a fall in current. This is why electricity is sent over long distances at very high voltage: the smaller current wastes less energy as heat in the transmission lines. The core is laminated, not solid, to reduce the eddy currents that would otherwise heat it.

**Questions**

1. A transformer does NOT change
   (a) the voltage (b) the current (c) the magnetic flux in the core (d) the frequency
2. In a step-up transformer
   (a) the secondary has more turns than the primary (b) the primary has more turns than the secondary (c) both coils have equal turns (d) the current rises
3. Power is transmitted at high voltage because
   (a) the current is smaller, so less heat is lost in the wires (b) the frequency becomes lower (c) the eddy currents are reduced (d) the wires become thinner
4. The core is laminated in order to
   (a) increase the voltage (b) reduce eddy currents (c) raise the frequency (d) insulate the coils
5. The word "accompanied" in the passage is closest in meaning to
   (a) preceded (b) replaced (c) occurring together with (d) prevented by

::: note Answers and explanations — Passage 2
**1.** (d) — the first sentence: "without changing its frequency".
**2.** (a) — "A step-up transformer has more turns in the secondary".
**3.** (a) — "the smaller current wastes less energy as heat".
**4.** (b) — stated in the last sentence.
**5.** (c) — "a rise in voltage is always accompanied by a fall in current" means they occur together.
:::

### Passage 3 — General English (inference and tone)

> Many students believe that the best way to learn is to read the same chapter several times. The comfortable feeling of familiarity is, however, easily mistaken for understanding. Researchers who compare study habits repeatedly find that students who close the book and try to recall what they have read remember far more a week later than those who simply reread. Testing yourself is harder and feels less productive in the moment, which is precisely why many learners avoid it. The wise student treats difficulty not as a sign of failure but as evidence that the memory is being strengthened.

**Questions**

1. The writer's attitude toward rereading is best described as
   (a) enthusiastic (b) mildly critical (c) hostile (d) indifferent
2. "precisely why" in the passage suggests that the difficulty of self-testing
   (a) is a reason students skip it (b) proves it is useless (c) makes it fun (d) is exaggerated
3. Which can be inferred from the passage?
   (a) Familiarity can give a false sense of knowing (b) Rereading never helps (c) Self-testing is easy (d) Researchers prefer rereading
4. The best title for the passage is
   (a) The Joy of Reading (b) A History of Study Methods (c) Why Recall Beats Rereading (d) How to Write an Exam

::: note Answers and explanations — Passage 3
**1.** (b) — it questions rereading without attacking it ("easily mistaken for understanding").
**2.** (a) — learners avoid self-testing *because* it is hard.
**3.** (a) — "familiarity is easily mistaken for understanding".
**4.** (c) — the passage compares recall with rereading.
:::

| Step | Action |

| 4 | eliminate extreme or unstated options |

## Chapter summary

- Read the questions first; answer only from the passage.
- The best title and main idea cover the whole passage.
- Vocabulary questions are settled by context.
- Technical passages: watch for numbers, definitions and cause–effect links.
- NOT/EXCEPT questions: three options are in the text, the fourth is not.

## Practice questions

**Set A — Recall (6 questions)**

1. In the passage on city transport, the writer says that new road lanes
   (a) never fill up (b) cost nothing (c) are always cycle lanes (d) fill up within a few years
2. In the passage on transformers, the primary and the secondary are
   (a) two coils of insulated wire (b) two cores (c) two currents (d) two frequencies
3. According to the passage on study habits, students who test themselves
   (a) forget faster (b) remember more after a week (c) read more often (d) feel more comfortable
4. A step-down transformer
   (a) has more turns in the secondary (b) raises the voltage (c) has fewer turns in the secondary (d) raises the power
5. In the transport passage, shops on pedestrian streets often report
   (a) fewer customers (b) no change (c) higher prices (d) more customers
6. The core of a transformer is made of
   (a) laminated sheets of soft iron (b) a solid copper block (c) insulating plastic (d) steel wire

::: note Answers and explanations — Set A
**1.** (d) — "new lanes fill up within a few years of opening".
**2.** (a) — "two coils of insulated wire, the primary and the secondary".
**3.** (b) — "remember far more a week later".
**4.** (c) — it does the opposite of step-up.
**5.** (d) — "more customers, not fewer".
**6.** (a) — "thin laminated sheets of soft iron".
:::

**Set B — Understanding (5 questions)**

Read the passage below and answer the questions.

> Nepal gets nearly all of its electricity from rivers, because steep mountains and monsoon rain give its rivers great energy. In the dry winter, however, the flow falls and some plants produce much less power. Engineers therefore build reservoirs that store water in the wet season, and they also connect the grid to India so that surplus power can be sold in summer and shortfalls covered in winter.

1. The main idea of the passage is
   (a) Nepal has no electricity problems (b) India produces power for Nepal (c) hydropower is Nepal's main source, with seasonal limits that engineers manage (d) the monsoon damages power plants
2. The word "shortfalls" most nearly means
   (a) surpluses (b) deficits (c) profits (d) floods
3. Reservoirs help because they
   (a) store wet-season water for the dry season (b) increase rainfall (c) cool the rivers (d) reduce the slope
4. The passage implies that in summer Nepal often has
   (a) surplus power (b) too little power (c) no rivers (d) a broken grid
5. Which is NOT mentioned as a way of handling the dry-season problem?
   (a) reservoirs (b) the grid link with India (c) solar farms (d) selling surplus in summer

::: note Answers and explanations — Set B
**1.** (c) — hydropower, winter low flow, reservoirs and the grid link.
**2.** (b) — shortfalls are deficits.
**3.** (a) — "store water in the wet season".
**4.** (a) — "surplus power can be sold in summer".
**5.** (c) — solar farms are not mentioned.
:::
