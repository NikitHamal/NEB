Physical chemistry carries the largest share of the Chemistry paper and is the most numerical part of it. The eight syllabus topics reduce to a handful of calculation types: mole and stoichiometry, a gas-law problem, quantum numbers and configurations, oxidation numbers, pH and buffers, titration, Faraday and cell potential, and rate or equilibrium. The official booklet's own chemistry samples (a limiting-reagent count of HCl molecules, the mass of copper deposited by 2 faradays, the number of electrons in a p-orbital) are all of these types.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- Mole concept: $n=\frac{m}{M}=\frac{N}{N_A}=\frac{V}{22.4}$ (STP), limiting reagent, empirical formula, equivalent mass.
- Gas laws: $PV=nRT$, Dalton's partial pressures, Graham's law, kinetic-theory averages.
- Atom: quantum numbers, orbitals per subshell, configuration (Fe²⁺ is 3d⁶), periodic trends.
- Redox: oxidation numbers, balancing; volumetric titration $N_1V_1=N_2V_2$.
- Ionic equilibrium: pH, buffer (Henderson), $K_{sp}$, hydrolysis, common-ion effect.
- Electrochemistry: Faraday's laws, $E^\circ_{cell}$, Nernst, $\Delta G=-nFE$.
- Energetics and kinetics: Hess, $\Delta H$, $\Delta G$, order, half-life, Arrhenius; VSEPR shapes and hybridisation.
:::

## 1.1 Chemical arithmetic

**Dalton's atomic theory:** atoms are indivisible (modified by subatomic particles and isotopes), atoms of one element are identical, compounds form in simple whole-number ratios. **Laws of chemical combination:** conservation of mass (Lavoisier); definite proportions (Proust); multiple proportions (Dalton: CO and CO₂ have O in 1 : 2 for a fixed C); reciprocal proportions (Richter); Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes; **Avogadro's hypothesis:** equal volumes of gases at the same $T$ and $P$ hold equal numbers of molecules.

Relative atomic mass is on the carbon-12 scale (1 u $=\frac{1}{12}$ of C-12 $=1.66\times10^{-27}$ kg). **Mole:** $6.022\times10^{23}$ entities. $n=\frac{m}{M}=\frac{N}{N_A}=\frac{V_{STP}}{22.4\ \text{L}}$; molar volume 22.4 L at STP (0 °C, 1 atm; 22.7 L at 1 bar) and 24.6 L at 27 °C, 1 atm. Gas density $\rho=\frac{PM}{RT}$; vapour density $=\frac{M}{2}$.

**Empirical formula:** divide the percentages by atomic masses, divide by the smallest, clear fractions. 40 % C, 6.7 % H, 53.3 % O gives CH₂O; molecular formula $=n\times$ empirical, $n=\frac{M}{\text{empirical mass}}$. **Limiting reagent:** compute the product from each reactant; the smaller yield decides. Percentage yield $=\frac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}}\times100$.

**Equivalent mass** $E=\frac{M}{n\text{-factor}}$: element $\frac{\text{atomic mass}}{\text{valency}}$; acid $\frac{M}{\text{basicity}}$ (H₂SO₄: 49); base $\frac{M}{\text{acidity}}$; oxidant/reductant $\frac{M}{\text{electrons exchanged}}$ (KMnO₄ in acid: $\frac{158}{5}=31.6$). Normality $N=\frac{w}{E\,V(L)}$, $N=M\times n$-factor. Concentration: molarity, molality $\frac{n}{\text{kg solvent}}$, mole fraction, % w/w, ppm.

::: example The booklet's HCl question
**Problem.** 21 g of MgCO₃ is treated with 50 mL of 12 M HCl. How many molecules of HCl remain?

**Solution.** MgCO₃ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. $n(\text{MgCO}_3)=\frac{21}{84}=0.25$ mol needs 0.50 mol HCl. Available $0.050\times12=0.60$ mol, so $0.10$ mol remains $=0.10\times6.02\times10^{23}=6.02\times10^{22}$ molecules.
:::

## 1.2 States of matter

**Gas laws.** Boyle $P_1V_1=P_2V_2$; Charles $\frac{V_1}{T_1}=\frac{V_2}{T_2}$; Gay-Lussac $\frac{P}{T}$ constant; combined; **ideal gas** $PV=nRT$, $R=0.0821$ L atm K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ $=8.314$ J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. **Dalton:** $P_{total}=\sum P_i$, $P_i=x_iP$ (2 mol He + 3 mol Ne at 5 atm: $P_{He}=2$ atm). **Graham:** $\frac{r_1}{r_2}=\sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}}$ (H₂ effuses $\sqrt{8}=2.83$ times as fast as CH₄). **Kinetic theory:** $\bar K=\frac{3}{2}kT$; $v_{rms}=\sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}$; $v_{mp}:v_{av}:v_{rms}=1:1.128:1.224$.

**Real gases:** van der Waals $\big(P+\frac{an^2}{V^2}\big)(V-nb)=nRT$; $a$ measures attraction, $b$ the excluded volume; deviations are greatest at high $P$ and low $T$; $Z=\frac{PV}{nRT}$; critical $T_c=\frac{8a}{27Rb}$; Joule–Thomson cooling. **Liquids:** vapour pressure rises with $T$; boiling at $P_{vap}=P_{ext}$; surface tension and viscosity fall with $T$; strength of intermolecular forces (hydrogen bonding in H₂O, HF, NH₃) raises boiling points. **Solids:** crystalline (ionic, covalent, metallic, molecular) and amorphous; unit cells: simple cubic (1 atom, 52 % packing), bcc (2 atoms, 68 %), fcc (4 atoms, 74 %); coordination 6, 8, 12; Bragg's $n\lambda=2d\sin\theta$; defects (Schottky, Frenkel).

## 1.3 Atomic structure and periodic classification

**Subatomic particles:** electron (Thomson), nucleus (Rutherford α-scattering), neutron (Chadwick). **Bohr:** $E_n=-\frac{13.6}{n^2}$ eV, $r_n=0.529\,n^2$ Å. **Quantum numbers:** $n$ (shell, 1, 2, …), $l=0\ldots n-1$ (subshell s, p, d, f), $m_l=-l\ldots+l$ ($2l+1$ orbitals: s 1, p 3, d 5, f 7), $m_s=\pm\frac{1}{2}$. A shell holds $2n^2$ electrons; a p-orbital holds at most two electrons of opposite spin. Radial nodes $n-l-1$, angular nodes $l$. de Broglie $\lambda=\frac{h}{m}v$, uncertainty principle.

**Filling rules:** Aufbau ($n+l$ rule: 4s before 3d), **Pauli** (no two electrons with four equal quantum numbers), **Hund** (maximise unpaired spins in degenerate orbitals). Exceptions: Cr 3d⁵4s¹, Cu 3d¹⁰4s¹. Cations lose 4s before 3d: **Fe²⁺ = [Ar]3d⁶**, Fe³⁺ = 3d⁵. Magnetic moment $\sqrt{n(n+2)}$ BM.

**Periodic table:** modern law (atomic number). s, p, d, f blocks. Trends: atomic radius falls across a period and rises down a group; ionisation energy rises across a period (exceptions Be > B, N > O) and falls down a group; electronegativity (Pauling) F 4.0 highest; electron affinity Cl > F; metallic character falls across. Isoelectronic ions: radius falls as $Z$ rises (N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺). Diagonal relationships: Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si.

## 1.4 Oxidation, reduction and equilibrium

**Oxidation number rules:** free element 0; H $+1$ (hydrides $-1$); O $-2$ (peroxides $-1$, OF₂ $+2$); sum equals the charge. Mn in KMnO₄ $+7$; Cr in K₂Cr₂O₇ $+6$; S in H₂SO₄ $+6$, Na₂S₂O₃ $+2$; N in HNO₃ $+5$, NH₄⁺ $-3$. **Redox:** oxidation is loss of electrons (increase in oxidation number); the oxidising agent is reduced. Disproportionation: Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O. Balancing: ion-electron (half-reaction) or oxidation-number method. MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O.

**Chemical equilibrium:** $K_c=\frac{[C]^c[D]^d}{[A]^a[B]^b}$; $K_p=K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}$; reaction quotient $Q$ vs $K$. **Le Chatelier:** increasing pressure favours fewer gas moles (N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ shifts right); heating favours the endothermic direction; a catalyst changes the rate only. N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ at 20 % dissociation of 1 mol L⁻¹: $K_c=\frac{0.4^2}{0.8}=0.2$.

## 1.5 Volumetric analysis

**Titration** finds an unknown concentration: $N_1V_1=N_2V_2$ (or $M_1V_1n_1=M_2V_2n_2$ by mole ratio). Primary standards: oxalic acid, Na₂CO₃, K₂Cr₂O₇. Indicators: phenolphthalein (8.3–10, weak acid–strong base), methyl orange (3.1–4.4, strong acid–weak base), methyl red. KMnO₄ is a self-indicator (acidify with dilute H₂SO₄ only). Iodometry with starch (blue at the end point). **Example:** 25 mL of 0.1 M NaOH neutralised by 20 mL H₂SO₄ means $M\times20\times2=2.5$, $M=0.0625$ M. Titration curve of a strong acid with a strong base has a sharp jump at pH 7. EDTA titrations measure hardness.

## 1.6 Ionic equilibrium, acid, base and salt

**Theories:** Arrhenius; Brønsted–Lowry (proton donor/acceptor, conjugate pairs); Lewis (electron-pair acceptor/donor). $K_w=[H^+][OH^-]=10^{-14}$ at 25 °C; pH $=-\log[H^+]$; pH + pOH $=14$. 0.01 M HCl: pH 2. **Weak acid:** $K_a=\frac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$, $[H^+]=\sqrt{K_aC}$ (0.1 M CH₃COOH, $K_a=1.8\times10^{-5}$: pH 2.87); $\alpha=\sqrt{\frac{K_a}{C}}$ (Ostwald). Mixtures: compute excess moles: 100 mL 0.1 M HCl + 100 mL 0.05 M NaOH leaves 0.005 mol in 0.2 L, pH $1.6$.

**Buffer** (weak acid + its salt): $\text{pH}=\text{p}K_a+\log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]}$ (Henderson); with ratio 10 and $pK_a=4.76$: 5.76. Best at pH $\approx pK_a$; blood buffer H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻. **Salt hydrolysis:** strong acid + weak base gives acidic solution (NH₄Cl); weak acid + strong base basic (CH₃COONa); strong + strong neutral. **Common-ion effect** lowers solubility. **Solubility product** $K_{sp}$: AgCl $s=\sqrt{K_{sp}}=1.34\times10^{-5}$ M for $K_{sp}=1.8\times10^{-10}$; precipitate forms when ionic product $>K_{sp}$; for A₂B $s=\sqrt{[}3]{K_{sp}/4}$.

## 1.7 Electrochemistry

**Faraday's laws:** $m=ZIt=\frac{E}{96500}It$; **one faraday** (96 485 C) deposits one equivalent (so 2 F deposits 63.5 g of Cu from Cu²⁺; 3 F per mole of Al). 2 A for 965 s (0.02 F) gives 0.635 g Cu. **Electrolysis products:** the species with the higher reduction potential is reduced at the cathode (copper over hydrogen from CuSO₄); in brine Cl₂ at the anode, H₂ at the cathode, NaOH in solution. **Conductance:** $\kappa$, $\Lambda_m=\frac{\kappa\times1000}{M}$, Kohlrausch's law for weak electrolytes.

**Galvanic cell:** anode (oxidation, negative), cathode (reduction, positive), salt bridge. $E^\circ_{cell}=E^\circ_{cathode}-E^\circ_{anode}$: Zn–Cu (Daniell) $=0.34-(-0.76)=1.10$ V. **Nernst:** $E=E^\circ-\frac{0.0591}{n}\log Q$ at 25 °C. $\Delta G^\circ=-nFE^\circ$ (for $n=2$, 1.10 V: $-212$ kJ); $\log K=\frac{nE^\circ}{0.0591}$. A more negative $E^\circ$ means a stronger reducing agent (Li, K, Na, Zn, Fe, H₂, Cu, Ag, Au). Batteries: dry cell, lead-acid (PbSO₄), Li-ion; corrosion (rusting) and its prevention (galvanising, sacrificial anode); fuel cells.

## 1.8 Energetics, kinetics and bonding

**First law, enthalpy:** $\Delta U=q+w$; $\Delta H=\Delta U+\Delta n_gRT$; **Hess's law** (path independence); $\Delta H^\circ_{rxn}=\sum\Delta H_f(\text{products})-\sum\Delta H_f(\text{reactants})$; for CH₄ + 2O₂: $-890=-393.5+2(-285.8)-\Delta H_f(\text{CH}_4)$, so $\Delta H_f=-75$ kJ mol⁻¹. Bond-energy method $\Delta H=\sum D(\text{broken})-\sum D(\text{formed})$. **Entropy and free energy:** $\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$; spontaneous if $\Delta G<0$; $\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K$.

**Kinetics:** rate $=k[A]^m[B]^n$, order $=m+n$ (rate $=k[A]^2[B]$ is third order); molecularity is a whole number for an elementary step. First order: $[A]=[A]_0e^{-kt}$, $t_{1/2}=\frac{0.693}{k}$ (for $k=10^{-3}$ s⁻¹: 693 s), independent of concentration; zero order $t_{1/2}\propto[A]_0$. **Arrhenius:** $k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}$, $\ln\frac{k_2}{k_1}=\frac{E_a}{R}\big(\frac{1}{T_1}-\frac{1}{T_2}\big)$; a catalyst lowers $E_a$; the rate roughly doubles per 10 K.

**Bonding:** ionic (lattice energy, Born–Haber), covalent (Lewis, octet and its exceptions: BF₃, PCl₅, SF₆), coordinate, metallic, hydrogen bond. **VSEPR / hybridisation:**

| Species | Electron pairs | Hybrid | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| BeCl₂, CO₂, C₂H₂ | 2 | sp | linear |
| BF₃, C₂H₄, SO₃ | 3 | sp² | trigonal planar |
| CH₄, NH₄⁺ | 4 | sp³ | tetrahedral |
| NH₃ | 3 + 1 lp | sp³ | pyramidal ($107^\circ$) |
| H₂O | 2 + 2 lp | sp³ | bent ($104.5^\circ$) |
| PCl₅ | 5 | sp³d | trigonal bipyramidal |
| SF₆ | 6 | sp³d² | octahedral |

Bond order from MO theory: O₂ has two unpaired electrons (paramagnetic), N₂ has the highest bond order (3). Ethyne has $3\sigma+2\pi$ bonds. Dipole moments: BF₃, CO₂, CH₄ are non-polar though bonds are polar; H₂O, NH₃ are polar.

## Chapter summary

- Convert everything to moles first; the limiting reagent decides the yield.
- Quantum numbers fix orbitals: $2l+1$ per subshell; cations lose 4s before 3d.
- Count oxidation-number change to find $n$-factor and equivalent mass.
- For acid–base mixtures find the excess; weak acids use $\sqrt{K_aC}$; buffers use Henderson.
- One faraday = one equivalent; $E^\circ_{cell}$ is cathode minus anode.
- Order is read from the rate law, not the equation; first-order half-life is constant.
- Shape comes from electron pairs including lone pairs.

### Model answer D1: Chemical arithmetic and gases (10 marks)

**Introduction.** A **mole** is the amount of substance that contains $6.022\times10^{23}$ particles (Avogadro's number); it has a mass equal to the molar mass in grams, and a gas occupies $22.4\ \text{L}$ at STP.

**Stoichiometry.** $\text{CaCO}_3 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$. For $10\ \text{g}$ of $\text{CaCO}_3$ ($M = 100$): $n = 0.1\ \text{mol}$, so $0.1\ \text{mol}$ of $\text{CO}_2$ forms and its volume at STP is $0.1\times22.4 = 2.24\ \text{L}$.

**Empirical and molecular formula.** A compound has C $40\%$, H $6.7\%$, O $53.3\%$.

| Element | % | ÷ atomic mass | ÷ smallest | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | 40.0 | 3.33 | 1 | 1 |
| H | 6.7 | 6.7 | 2 | 2 |
| O | 53.3 | 3.33 | 1 | 1 |

The empirical formula is $\text{CH}_2\text{O}$ (mass 30). If $M = 180$ then $n = 180/30 = 6$, so the molecular formula is $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$ (glucose).

**Gas laws.**

1. **Boyle:** $PV = $ constant (at constant $T$). **Charles:** $V/T = $ constant (at constant $P$). **Avogadro:** equal volumes of gases at the same $T$ and $P$ hold equal numbers of molecules.
2. **Ideal gas equation:** $PV = nRT$, with $R = 0.0821\ \text{L atm K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1} = 8.314\ \text{J K}^{-1}\text{mol}^{-1}$. For $2\ \text{mol}$ at $300\ \text{K}$ and $2\ \text{atm}$: $V = \frac{2\times0.0821\times300}{2} = 24.6\ \text{L}$.
3. **Dalton's law:** $P_{total} = p_1 + p_2 + \dots$, with $p_i = x_iP$.
4. **Graham's law:** the rate of diffusion is inversely proportional to the square root of the molar mass, so $\frac{r_{H_2}}{r_{O_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{32}{2}} = 4$.
5. **Real gases** deviate at high pressure and low temperature; the **van der Waals** equation $\left(P + \frac{an^2}{V^2}\right)(V - nb) = nRT$ corrects for attraction and molecular volume.

**Conclusion.** The mole links mass, number and gas volume, and the ideal gas equation describes gases when pressure is low and temperature is high.

### Model answer D2: Ionic equilibrium (10 marks)

**Introduction.** In water, acids and bases ionise, and weak electrolytes reach an equilibrium. $K_w = [\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-] = 10^{-14}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $\text{pH} = -\log[\text{H}^+]$.

**1. Strong and weak acids.** For $0.01\ \text{M}$ HCl, $\text{pH} = 2$. For a weak acid $[\text{H}^+] = \sqrt{K_aC}$; for $0.1\ \text{M}$ acetic acid ($K_a = 1.8\times10^{-5}$): $[\text{H}^+] = \sqrt{1.8\times10^{-6}} = 1.34\times10^{-3}$, so $\text{pH} = 2.87$. **Ostwald's dilution law:** the degree of ionisation $\alpha = \sqrt{K_a/C}$ increases on dilution.

**2. Buffer solutions.** They resist a change of pH when a little acid or base is added. **Henderson equation:** $\text{pH} = \text{p}K_a + \log\frac{[\text{salt}]}{[\text{acid}]}$. For $0.1\ \text{M}$ $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$ with $0.1\ \text{M}$ $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$, $\text{pH} = 4.74$; with $0.2\ \text{M}$ salt, $\text{pH} = 4.74 + 0.30 = 5.04$. Blood is a buffer ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3/\text{HCO}_3^-$, pH 7.4).

**3. Salt hydrolysis.**

| Salt of | Example | Solution |
|---|---|---|
| Strong acid + strong base | NaCl | Neutral |
| Strong acid + weak base | $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ | Acidic |
| Weak acid + strong base | $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$ | Basic |

For $0.1\ \text{M}$ $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ ($K_b = 1.8\times10^{-5}$): $K_h = \frac{K_w}{K_b} = 5.56\times10^{-10}$, $[\text{H}^+] = \sqrt{K_hC} = 7.45\times10^{-6}$, so $\text{pH} = 5.13$.

**4. Solubility product.** For $\text{AgCl}$, $K_{sp} = [\text{Ag}^+][\text{Cl}^-] = 1.8\times10^{-10}$, so the solubility is $s = \sqrt{K_{sp}} = 1.34\times10^{-5}\ \text{M}$. A salt precipitates when the ionic product exceeds $K_{sp}$. The **common-ion effect** lowers its solubility.

**Conclusion.** The same equilibrium ideas explain pH, buffers, the acidity of salts and the formation of precipitates.

### Model answer D3: Electrochemistry, kinetics and thermochemistry (10 marks)

**Introduction.** Electrochemistry links chemical change with electrical energy.

**1. Daniell cell.** $\text{Zn}\,|\,\text{Zn}^{2+}\,\|\,\text{Cu}^{2+}\,|\,\text{Cu}$: oxidation at the anode ($\text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2e^-$) and reduction at the cathode ($\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$). $E^\circ_{cell} = 0.34 - (-0.76) = 1.10\ \text{V}$. The salt bridge completes the circuit. The **Nernst equation:** $E = E^\circ - \frac{0.0592}{n}\log\frac{[\text{Zn}^{2+}]}{[\text{Cu}^{2+}]}$; with $0.1\ \text{M}$ and $1.0\ \text{M}$, $E = 1.10 + 0.0296 = 1.13\ \text{V}$. Also $\Delta G^\circ = -nFE^\circ = -2\times96500\times1.10 = -212\ \text{kJ}$.

**2. Faraday's laws.** The mass deposited is $m = \frac{M\,I\,t}{nF}$. A current of $0.5\ \text{A}$ for $1930\ \text{s}$ carries $965\ \text{C} = 0.01\ \text{mol}$ of electrons, so $0.005\ \text{mol}$ of copper (**0.318 g**) is deposited.

**3. First-order kinetics.** $\ln\frac{[A]_0}{[A]} = kt$ and $t_{1/2} = \frac{0.693}{k}$, independent of concentration. If $k = 0.0693\ \text{min}^{-1}$, then $t_{1/2} = 10\ \text{min}$, and after $30\ \text{min}$ the fraction left is $\left(\frac12\right)^3 = \frac18$. The **Arrhenius equation** $k = Ae^{-E_a/RT}$ shows that a catalyst lowers $E_a$.

**4. Hess's law.** The heat of a reaction is the same by any route. From $\Delta H_c(\text{CH}_4) = -890.3$, $\Delta H_f(\text{CO}_2) = -393.5$ and $\Delta H_f(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = -285.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$: $\Delta H_f(\text{CH}_4) = -393.5 + 2(-285.8) + 890.3 = -74.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$.

| Topic | Key equation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Cell emf | $E^\circ_{cathode} - E^\circ_{anode}$ | $1.10\ \text{V}$ |
| Faraday | $m = MIt/nF$ | $0.318\ \text{g}$ Cu |
| First order | $t_{1/2} = 0.693/k$ | $10\ \text{min}$ |
| Hess | Sum of steps | $-74.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ |

**Conclusion.** Cell potential, charge, rate and heat of reaction are all calculable from a few standard data and equations.
