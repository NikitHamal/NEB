## 12.1 Stars, galaxies, solar system

### The universe and its size

::: definition Universe
The universe is everything that exists: all matter, energy, space and time, including the stars, planets, galaxies and everything in between.
:::

The universe is so large that kilometres are too small to measure it. Astronomers use two bigger units.

- **Astronomical unit (AU):** the average distance from the Earth to the Sun, about $1.5\times10^{8}$ km. Used inside the solar system.
- **Light year (ly):** the distance travelled by light in one year in vacuum. Used for stars and galaxies.

$$ 1\ \text{ly} = 3\times10^{5}\ \text{km s}^{-1} \times 3.15\times10^{7}\ \text{s} \approx 9.46\times10^{12}\ \text{km} $$

::: caution A light year is a distance
The "year" in light year can mislead. A light year measures **distance**, not time. Light from a star 10 light years away takes 10 years to reach us.
:::

::: example Light travel time
**Problem.** The Sun is $1.5\times10^{8}$ km from the Earth. How long does sunlight take to reach the Earth? (Speed of light $= 3\times10^{5}$ km s⁻¹.)

**Solution.** $t = \dfrac{d}{v} = \dfrac{1.5\times10^{8}}{3\times10^{5}} = 500$ s $= 8$ min $20$ s.

So we see the Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago.
:::

::: example Distance of a star
**Problem.** The nearest star after the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is 4.24 light years away. Express this distance in kilometres.

**Solution.** $d = 4.24 \times 9.46\times10^{12} = 4.0\times10^{13}$ km.

Even the nearest star is about 40 trillion kilometres away.
:::

### Stars

::: definition Star
A star is a huge, hot, glowing ball of gas (mostly hydrogen and helium) that produces its own heat and light by nuclear fusion in its core.
:::

In the core of a star, the temperature is millions of degrees. Hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium and a vast amount of energy is released (nuclear fusion). The outward push of this energy balances the inward pull of gravity, so the star stays stable for billions of years.

- Our nearest star is the **Sun**. It is a medium-sized yellow star about 4.6 billion years old, and is expected to shine for about another 5 billion years.
- Stars **twinkle** because their light passes through the moving layers of the Earth's atmosphere and is refracted differently from moment to moment. Planets are nearer and look like tiny discs, so they do not twinkle much.
- The colour of a star shows its surface temperature: blue-white stars are the hottest, yellow (like the Sun) are medium, red stars are cooler.
- Stars differ in size, from tiny neutron stars to supergiants hundreds of times the Sun's diameter, and in brightness.

### Life cycle of a star

A star is born, lives and dies. The path depends on its mass.

1. **Nebula.** A huge cloud of gas (hydrogen) and dust.
2. **Protostar.** Gravity pulls the cloud together; it becomes denser and hotter.
3. **Main-sequence star.** Hydrogen fusion begins in the core; the star shines steadily for most of its life (about 10 billion years for the Sun).
4. **Red giant** (or red supergiant). When hydrogen in the core runs out, the star swells and cools on the outside.
5. **Death.**
   - A star like the Sun throws off its outer layers (a planetary nebula) and the core shrinks into a **white dwarf**, which cools slowly into a black dwarf.
   - A star much heavier than the Sun (about 8 or more solar masses) ends in a violent explosion called a **supernova**. The core then becomes a **neutron star**, or, if the star was very heavy, a **black hole**.

```photo id="sci10/star-life" caption="Life cycle of stars. A star is born in a nebula; a Sun-like star becomes a red giant and then a white dwarf, while a massive star explodes as a supernova." width="100"
```

::: example Time on the main sequence
**Problem.** The Sun is about 4.6 billion years old and will live about 10 billion years in total on the main sequence. How much longer will it shine as a main-sequence star?

**Solution.** Remaining life $\approx 10 - 4.6 = 5.4$ billion years, or roughly 5 billion years.
:::

### Constellations

A **constellation** is a group of stars that looks like a pattern in the sky. The stars in a constellation are not close together; they only appear so as we look from Earth. There are 88 official constellations.

- **Ursa Major (Great Bear, Saptarshi):** seven bright stars forming a ladle or plough. Two of its stars, the "pointers", point toward the Pole Star.
- **Orion (Mrigashira):** a hunter with three stars in a row as his belt; seen in the winter sky.
- **Cassiopeia:** a W-shaped group.
- **Pole Star (Polaris, Dhruva Tara):** appears almost fixed in the northern sky, because it lies almost exactly above the Earth's axis of rotation. Travellers once used it for finding north.

All stars appear to move from east to west during the night because the Earth spins from west to east.

### Galaxies

::: definition Galaxy
A galaxy is a huge system of billions of stars, gas, dust and dark matter, held together by gravity.
:::

There are hundreds of billions of galaxies in the observable universe. Galaxies are classified by shape:

| Type | Shape | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Spiral | Flat rotating disc with spiral arms and a central bulge | Milky Way, Andromeda |
| Elliptical | Round or oval ball of old stars | M87 |
| Irregular | No definite shape | Large and Small Magellanic Clouds |

**The Milky Way** is our home galaxy: a spiral galaxy about 100,000 light years across with at least 100 billion stars. Our Sun lies in one of its arms, about 26,000 light years from the centre, and takes about 230 million years to go once around it. From the Earth we see the Milky Way edge-on as a band of light across the sky. The nearest large galaxy is **Andromeda**, about 2.5 million light years away.

### The solar system

::: definition Solar system
The solar system is the Sun together with all the bodies that move around it: eight planets, their moons, dwarf planets, asteroids, comets and meteoroids.
:::

The Sun contains more than 99 % of the mass of the solar system, and its gravity holds everything in orbit. The solar system is about 4.6 billion years old.

**The planets in order from the Sun:** Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

**Kepler's third law.** For planets orbiting the Sun, the square of the time of one revolution is proportional to the cube of the average distance from the Sun: $T^2 \propto r^3$. If $T$ is in years and $r$ in AU, then $T^2 = r^3$.

::: example Using Kepler's third law
**Problem.** A planet orbits the Sun at an average distance of 4 AU. Find its time period.

**Solution.** $T^2 = r^3 = 4^3 = 64$, so $T = 8$ years.
:::

**Dwarf planets.** In 2006 the International Astronomical Union defined a planet as a body that orbits the Sun, is round, and has cleared its orbit of other bodies. Pluto does not clear its orbit, so it is a **dwarf planet**, as are Ceres, Eris, Haumea and Makemake.

**Moon.** The Moon is the Earth's only natural satellite. Its diameter is about one quarter of the Earth's, and it is about 384,000 km away. It takes about 27.3 days to orbit the Earth and shows the same face to us always. It has no air or water, so the daytime is hot (about 127 °C) and the night is very cold (about −173 °C). Its gravity is one sixth of the Earth's. The **phases** of the Moon (new moon to full moon) arise because we see different portions of the lit half as the Moon goes round the Earth. **Tides** in the sea are caused mainly by the Moon's gravity.

**Eclipses.** A **solar eclipse** occurs when the Moon comes between the Sun and the Earth (on a new moon day) and its shadow falls on the Earth. A **lunar eclipse** occurs when the Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon (on a full moon day) and the Moon moves into the Earth's shadow. We do not get an eclipse every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5° to the Earth's orbit.

**Other members.**

| Body | What it is |
|---|---|
| Asteroids | Rocky lumps, mostly in the **asteroid belt** between Mars and Jupiter |
| Comets | Balls of ice, dust and rock in long orbits. Near the Sun they develop a glowing head and a tail pointing **away** from the Sun. Halley's comet returns about every 76 years |
| Meteoroids | Small rocky pieces in space |
| Meteors ("shooting stars") | Meteoroids burning up as they enter the Earth's atmosphere |
| Meteorites | Meteors that reach the ground |

## 12.2 Formation of the universe

### The Big Bang theory

The most widely accepted explanation for the origin of the universe is the **Big Bang theory**.

::: definition Big Bang theory
According to the Big Bang theory, the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from an extremely hot and dense point, which expanded rapidly in a huge explosion-like event. The universe has been expanding and cooling ever since.
:::

It is not an explosion in space; it is the expansion **of space itself**. A useful picture is a balloon with dots drawn on it. As the balloon is blown up, every dot moves away from every other dot, and no dot is the centre.

Main stages:

1. **Beginning (time zero):** all matter and energy are packed in a tiny, hot, dense state.
2. **First fraction of a second:** rapid expansion; fundamental particles (quarks, electrons) form.
3. **First few minutes:** protons and neutrons join to form the nuclei of hydrogen and helium.
4. **About 380,000 years:** the universe has cooled enough for atoms to form; light is free to travel. This relic light is still seen today as the cosmic microwave background.
5. **After a few hundred million years:** gravity pulls gas together to form the first stars and galaxies.
6. **About 9 billion years after the Big Bang (4.6 billion years ago):** our Sun and solar system form from a cloud of gas and dust.

### Evidence for the Big Bang

- **Expanding universe (Hubble, 1929).** Edwin Hubble found that distant galaxies are moving away from us, and the farther the galaxy, the faster it recedes ($v = H_0 d$, where $H_0 \approx 70$ km s⁻¹ per megaparsec). The light of receding galaxies is shifted toward the red end of the spectrum (**red shift**), like a siren that sounds lower when the ambulance moves away (the Doppler effect). If the galaxies are moving apart now, they must have been together in the past.
- **Cosmic microwave background radiation.** Discovered by Penzias and Wilson in 1965: a faint microwave glow coming equally from all directions, at a temperature of about 2.7 K. It is the cooled afterglow of the early hot universe.
- **Abundance of light elements.** About 75 % hydrogen and 25 % helium by mass in the universe, as the theory predicts.

### Fate and other ideas

Observations show that the expansion is speeding up, driven by something called **dark energy**. Galaxies also contain much more mass than we can see; the unseen matter is called **dark matter**. Together dark matter and dark energy make up about 95 % of the universe, and their nature is not yet known. An older competing idea, the Steady State theory, said that the universe has no beginning and looks the same at all times, but it could not explain the microwave background and has been given up.

::: example Recession speed of a galaxy
**Problem.** A galaxy is 100 megaparsecs away. Using $H_0 = 70$ km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹, find the speed with which it moves away.

**Solution.** $v = H_0 d = 70 \times 100 = 7000$ km s⁻¹.
:::

## 12.3 Space exploration

### Why explore space?

To understand the origin and future of the universe, to find life elsewhere, to find new resources, to improve life on Earth with new technologies (weather forecasting, communication, GPS, medical imaging), and to study threats such as asteroids.

### Tools of space exploration

**Telescopes.** Optical telescopes collect visible light. Radio telescopes collect radio waves from space (Jodrell Bank, Arecibo). Space telescopes avoid the blurring and absorption of the atmosphere: the **Hubble Space Telescope** (1990) and the **James Webb Space Telescope** (launched 2021, infrared) have shown galaxies at enormous distances.

```photo id="sci10/saturn-v" caption="Launch of Apollo 11 on a Saturn V rocket in 1969. A rocket burns fuel to push gas down so that it can lift itself away from the Earth." width="56"
```

**Rockets.** A rocket works on Newton's third law: hot gas is thrown out backward at high speed, and the rocket moves forward with an equal momentum. To reach orbit, the rocket must reach about 8 km s⁻¹. Rockets are built in **stages**; each stage is dropped when its fuel is used up so that the remaining rocket is lighter.

::: example Speed needed to leave the Earth
**Problem.** An object must have a speed called the escape velocity $v = \sqrt{2gR}$ to leave the Earth for ever. Find it for $g = 9.8$ m s⁻² and $R = 6.4\times10^{6}$ m.

**Solution.** $v = \sqrt{2 \times 9.8 \times 6.4\times10^{6}} = \sqrt{1.254\times10^{8}} = 1.12\times10^{4}$ m s⁻¹ $= 11.2$ km s⁻¹.

(The speed needed to stay in a low orbit is smaller, $\sqrt{gR} \approx 7.9$ km s⁻¹.)
:::

### Artificial satellites

An **artificial satellite** is a man-made object that revolves around the Earth (or another body). It stays in orbit because the gravitational pull supplies the force needed to keep it moving in a circle; it is in a continuous free fall around the Earth.

| Type | Height | Period | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low Earth orbit (LEO) | 200–2,000 km | about 90 min | Earth observation, International Space Station, spy and remote-sensing satellites |
| Polar orbit | about 800 km | about 100 min | Map-making, weather, covers the whole Earth as it turns below |
| Geostationary orbit | 36,000 km above the equator | 24 h | Communication, TV broadcast, weather. It stays above the same point on the Earth |

A **geostationary satellite** revolves from west to east with the same period as the Earth's rotation (24 h), so it always appears fixed over one point of the sky.

Uses of satellites: communication (telephone, TV, internet), weather forecasting, navigation (GPS), remote sensing (forests, glaciers, floods, land use), military surveillance, and scientific research.

**Nepal in space.** In 2019 Nepal sent its first satellite, **NepaliSat-1**, a small cube about 10 cm on a side built by Nepali engineers in Japan as part of the BIRDS-3 project. It was launched to the International Space Station and released into orbit to take images of Nepal's land and mountains. Nepal also uses foreign satellites for weather and remote sensing, e.g. monitoring glaciers and glacial lakes.

### Space stations, probes and rovers

- **Space station.** The **International Space Station** is a large laboratory orbiting about 400 km above the Earth, built and run by the USA, Russia, Europe, Japan and Canada. Astronauts live there for months and carry out experiments in weightlessness.
- **Space probes** are unmanned spacecraft sent to study planets and other bodies from near or far: Voyager, Cassini (Saturn), New Horizons (Pluto).
- **Rovers** are vehicles that drive on another world: Curiosity and Perseverance on Mars.

### Problems and dangers

- **Space debris.** Thousands of broken satellites and pieces of rocket orbit the Earth at high speed and can damage working satellites.
- **Cost** and risk to human life; harmful radiation and weightlessness (muscle and bone loss).
- **Pollution** of the upper atmosphere from rocket launches.

::: tip Writing about satellites
When asked to "explain how a geostationary satellite is useful", give: orbit at 36,000 km above the equator, period 24 h, appears fixed in the sky, therefore a single dish antenna on the ground can keep pointing at it for TV and communication.
:::

## Chapter summary

- The universe is everything. 1 light year $= 9.46\times10^{12}$ km (distance); 1 AU $= 1.5\times10^{8}$ km. Sunlight takes 8 min 20 s to reach the Earth.
- A star is a ball of hot gas shining by nuclear fusion of hydrogen. Life cycle: nebula → protostar → main sequence → red giant → white dwarf (Sun-like) or supernova → neutron star / black hole (massive).
- A galaxy is a system of billions of stars. The Milky Way is a spiral galaxy. Constellations are patterns of stars; the Pole Star shows north.
- The solar system has eight planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars (terrestrial), Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune (giants), plus dwarf planets, asteroids, comets and meteors. $T^2 = r^3$ (years, AU).
- Big Bang: the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago and is still expanding. Evidence: red shift of galaxies, cosmic microwave background, abundance of H and He.
- Satellites stay in orbit because gravity supplies the circular-motion force. Geostationary satellites orbit at 36,000 km with a 24 h period.
- Milestones: Sputnik 1 (1957), Gagarin (1961), Apollo 11 (1969), Hubble (1990), ISS (1998), NepaliSat-1 (2019).
