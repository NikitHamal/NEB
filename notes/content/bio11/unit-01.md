Every living thing, from a *Spirogyra* filament in a Kathmandu pond to a blue
pine on a Manang hillside, is built from the same short list of molecules and
organised into the same basic unit — the cell. This unit does two jobs. First it
names the molecules of life and says what each one does. Then it takes the cell
apart organelle by organelle, and finally shows how one cell becomes two. Nearly
everything in the remaining Botany units — photosynthesis, respiration,
reproduction, genetics — is an application of what is set up here.

::: key What the examiner asks from this unit
Three things come almost every year: a **labelled diagram** (plant cell, animal
cell, mitochondrion or chloroplast) with 6–8 correct labels; a **difference
table** (prokaryote vs eukaryote, plant vs animal cell, mitosis vs meiosis, DNA
vs RNA); and a **stage-by-stage description** of mitosis or meiosis. Practise
drawing, not just reading.
:::

## 1.1 Biomolecules

A biomolecule is any organic molecule made by a living cell. Ninety-nine per cent
of the atoms in protoplasm are just four elements — carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and
nitrogen — and they are assembled into four great families: carbohydrates,
proteins, lipids and nucleic acids. Three of these four are **polymers**: long
chains built by joining small repeating units (monomers) with the loss of a water
molecule at each join. That single reaction, **condensation**, builds almost
everything; running it backwards with water, **hydrolysis**, takes it apart.

| Family | Monomer | Bond formed | Typical example | Chief role |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Carbohydrate | monosaccharide | glycosidic | starch, cellulose | fuel and structure |
| Protein | amino acid | peptide | enzymes, keratin | catalysis and structure |
| Lipid | glycerol + fatty acid | ester | triglyceride, lecithin | energy store, membranes |
| Nucleic acid | nucleotide | phosphodiester | DNA, RNA | information |

### Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates have the general formula Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ — literally "hydrates of carbon".
They are classified by how many sugar units they contain.

- **Monosaccharides** are single sugar units, sweet and soluble. Trioses have
  3 carbons, pentoses 5 (ribose C₅H₁₀O₅, deoxyribose C₅H₁₀O₄) and hexoses 6
  (glucose, fructose and galactose, all C₆H₁₂O₆). Glucose is the universal
  respiratory fuel.
- **Oligosaccharides** contain 2–10 units. The disaccharides matter most:
  sucrose (glucose + fructose, the transport sugar of plants), maltose
  (glucose + glucose, from starch digestion) and lactose (glucose + galactose,
  milk sugar).
- **Polysaccharides** are large, insoluble and tasteless. **Starch**, (C₆H₁₀O₅)ₙ,
  is the plant food store and is a mixture of unbranched amylose and branched
  amylopectin. **Glycogen** is the animal and fungal store. **Cellulose** forms
  the plant cell wall; its β-1,4 links make a straight, tough chain that human
  enzymes cannot digest. **Chitin** builds fungal walls and insect cuticle, and
  **inulin** is stored in *Dahlia* tubers.

::: definition Reducing and non-reducing sugars
A **reducing sugar** has a free aldehyde or ketone group and so reduces
Benedict's or Fehling's solution, giving a brick-red precipitate of Cu₂O on
heating. All monosaccharides, and maltose and lactose, are reducing. **Sucrose is
non-reducing** — it must first be hydrolysed with dilute acid before it responds.
:::

### Proteins

A protein is a polymer of **amino acids**. Every amino acid has the same
skeleton — a central carbon carrying an amino group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group
(–COOH), a hydrogen atom and a variable side chain R. Twenty different R groups
occur in proteins; nine of them cannot be made by the human body and must come
from food, and these are the **essential amino acids**.

Amino acids join by a **peptide bond** (–CO–NH–) formed by condensation between
the –COOH of one and the –NH₂ of the next. Two amino acids give a dipeptide,
many give a polypeptide.

Protein structure is described at four levels:

| Level | What it is | Held by | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Primary | the sequence of amino acids | peptide bonds | insulin chain |
| Secondary | local coiling: α-helix or β-pleated sheet | hydrogen bonds | keratin, silk fibroin |
| Tertiary | the whole chain folded into a 3-D shape | H-bonds, ionic, disulphide, hydrophobic | myoglobin, all enzymes |
| Quaternary | two or more folded chains together | same as tertiary | haemoglobin (2α + 2β) |

Heat, extremes of pH and heavy-metal ions break the weak bonds holding the
tertiary shape. The chain unfolds, the protein loses its function and usually
becomes insoluble — this is **denaturation**, and for most proteins it cannot be
reversed. Boiling an egg and curdling milk with lemon juice are the same process.
Proteins give a violet colour with the **biuret** reagent.

::: example Worked example 1.1 — counting peptide bonds
**Problem.** (a) A polypeptide contains 150 amino acid residues in a single
unbranched chain. How many peptide bonds does it contain, and how many water
molecules were released when it was made? (b) Glycine has relative molecular
mass 75 and alanine 89. Find the relative molecular mass of the dipeptide
glycylalanine.

**Solution.**

(a) Each peptide bond joins two neighbouring residues, so a chain of $n$
residues has $n - 1$ bonds:

$$ \text{peptide bonds} = 150 - 1 = 149 $$

One water molecule is lost per bond formed, so 149 molecules of water were
released.

(b) Condensation removes one water molecule (RMM 18):

$$ 75 + 89 - 18 = 146 $$

The dipeptide has a relative molecular mass of **146**.
:::

### Lipids

Lipids are the fatty substances of the cell: insoluble in water, soluble in
ether, chloroform and benzene. A **triglyceride** — the commonest lipid — is one
molecule of glycerol esterified with three fatty acids, with three molecules of
water removed.

Fatty acids whose hydrocarbon chains carry no C=C double bond are **saturated**
(palmitic, stearic acid); they pack tightly and are solid at room temperature —
ghee, butter. Chains with one or more double bonds are **unsaturated** (oleic,
linoleic acid); the kinks stop them packing, so they are liquid oils — mustard
oil, sunflower oil.

Other important lipids are **phospholipids** (one fatty acid replaced by a
phosphate group; the basis of every membrane), **glycolipids**, **steroids**
(cholesterol in animals, ergosterol in fungi) and **waxes** (cutin and suberin,
which waterproof plant surfaces).

::: key Why fat is the best energy store
Lipids release about **39 kJ per gram** on oxidation, against about **17 kJ per
gram** for carbohydrate or protein — more than twice as much, because lipid
carbon is far less oxidised to begin with. Lipids are also stored without water,
so a gram of fat carries no dead weight. That is why seeds such as mustard and
sesame store oil, not starch. Lipids give a red colour with Sudan III.
:::

### Nucleic acids

Nucleic acids carry information. Their monomer, the **nucleotide**, has three
parts: a phosphate group, a pentose sugar and a nitrogenous base. Sugar plus base
alone is a **nucleoside**; add the phosphate and it becomes a nucleotide.
Nucleotides join through phosphodiester bonds between the 3′ carbon of one sugar
and the 5′ carbon of the next.

The bases are of two kinds: **purines** (adenine and guanine) have two rings,
**pyrimidines** (cytosine, thymine and uracil) have one.

James Watson and Francis Crick proposed the structure of DNA in **1953**, using
Rosalind Franklin's X-ray photographs and Erwin Chargaff's analyses. Their model
has these features, all of which are examinable:

- Two polynucleotide strands wound into a **right-handed double helix** about a
  common axis.
- The strands are **antiparallel** — one runs 5′→3′, the other 3′→5′.
- The backbones are on the outside, the bases point inwards and lie flat,
  perpendicular to the axis.
- Pairing is **specific and complementary**: adenine pairs with thymine by two
  hydrogen bonds, guanine with cytosine by three. A purine always pairs with a
  pyrimidine, so the helix has a constant width of **2 nm**.
- Adjacent base pairs are **0.34 nm** apart and each complete turn is **3.4 nm**,
  so there are **10 base pairs per turn**.

| Feature | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | deoxyribose | ribose |
| Bases | A, G, C, **T** | A, G, C, **U** |
| Strands | double (usually) | single (usually) |
| Location | nucleus, mitochondria, plastids | nucleus and cytoplasm |
| Amount in a cell | constant | varies with activity |
| Function | stores the genetic message | expresses it (mRNA, tRNA, rRNA) |

::: example Worked example 1.2 — using Chargaff's rule
**Problem.** A sample of double-stranded DNA contains 22 % adenine. Calculate the
percentage of each of the other three bases, and find the ratio
$\dfrac{A+T}{G+C}$.

**Solution.** Chargaff's rule for double-stranded DNA is $A = T$ and $G = C$.

Since $A = 22\ \%$, thymine $T = 22\ \%$, so $A + T = 44\ \%$.

The four bases must total 100 %, so $G + C = 100 - 44 = 56\ \%$, and because
$G = C$,

$$ G = C = \frac{56}{2} = 28\ \% $$

Hence $A = T = 22\ \%$, $G = C = 28\ \%$, and

$$ \frac{A+T}{G+C} = \frac{44}{56} = 0.79 $$
:::

::: caution Chargaff's rule applies only to double-stranded DNA
In single-stranded RNA there is no pairing, so $A$ need not equal $U$. A question
that gives you "RNA with 30 % adenine" is not asking you to set $U = 30\ \%$.
:::

### Minerals

Besides the four main elements, a plant needs at least fourteen mineral elements
absorbed from the soil as ions. They are divided by the **quantity** needed, not
by importance — a micronutrient deficiency kills just as surely as a
macronutrient one.

| Class | Elements | Example of role | Deficiency sign |
|---|---|---|---|
| Macronutrients | N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S | N in amino acids and nucleotides | general yellowing, stunting |
| | | Mg is the central atom of chlorophyll | yellowing between veins of old leaves |
| | | Ca as calcium pectate in the middle lamella | death of growing points |
| | | K regulates stomatal opening | scorched leaf margins |
| Micronutrients | Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni | Fe in cytochromes and ferredoxin | yellowing between veins of young leaves |
| | | Mo in nitrate reductase | pale, cupped leaves |

### Enzymes

An **enzyme** is a biological catalyst — a globular protein that speeds up a
specific reaction by lowering its activation energy, and is itself unchanged at
the end. Wilhelm Kühne coined the word in 1878; James Sumner crystallised the
first enzyme, urease, in 1926 and proved that enzymes are proteins. (A few RNA
molecules, called **ribozymes**, are also catalytic, so "all enzymes are
proteins" is no longer strictly true.)

Properties worth learning as a list:

1. Needed in **trace amounts**; not used up.
2. **Highly specific** — usually one enzyme, one substrate.
3. Work fastest at an **optimum temperature**, about 37 °C in mammals and
   25–35 °C in most plants. Above this the protein denatures and activity falls
   sharply.
4. Work fastest at an **optimum pH** — pepsin at pH 2, salivary amylase at
   pH 6.8, trypsin at pH 8.
5. Activity rises with substrate concentration until all active sites are
   occupied, then levels off.
6. Many need a **cofactor**: a metal activator (Mg²⁺, Zn²⁺), a loosely bound
   **coenzyme** (NAD, FAD, coenzyme A — most are vitamin derivatives) or a
   tightly bound prosthetic group (haem).
7. Reversible in action and named by adding **-ase** to the substrate
   (sucrase, lipase, protease).

The substrate binds to a small pocket called the **active site**. Emil Fischer's
**lock-and-key** model (1894) treats the site as rigid; Daniel Koshland's
**induced-fit** model (1958) — the accepted one — says the site changes shape
slightly as the substrate arrives, closing round it like a hand round a ball.

::: memory The six classes of enzymes
**Ox – Tr – Hy – Ly – Is – Li**: **Ox**idoreductases (transfer H or electrons),
**Tr**ansferases (transfer a group), **Hy**drolases (split with water),
**Ly**ases (split without water, forming a double bond), **Is**omerases
(rearrange), **Li**gases (join two molecules using ATP).
:::

### Water

Water is 65–90 % of the fresh mass of protoplasm, and the odd properties that
follow from its polar, hydrogen-bonded structure are the reason life works at all.

- **Excellent solvent.** Its high dielectric constant pulls ionic compounds
  apart, so all cell reactions take place in solution.
- **High specific heat capacity** (4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹). A large body of water — or a
  leaf — changes temperature only slowly, buffering the cell against sudden heat.
- **High latent heat of vaporisation** (about 2260 J g⁻¹). Transpiration and
  sweating therefore remove a great deal of heat for a little water lost.
- **Cohesion and adhesion.** Hydrogen bonding holds the water column in the
  xylem together, which is how water reaches the top of a *Shorea robusta* tree.
- **Maximum density at 4 °C**, so ice floats and a Rara-lake fish survives the
  winter under the ice rather than in it.
- **Reactant.** Water is split in the light reaction of photosynthesis and is
  consumed in every hydrolysis.

## 1.2 Cell

Robert Hooke saw and named "cells" in a slice of cork in **1665**; Anton van
Leeuwenhoek was the first to see living cells. In **1838–39** Matthias Schleiden
and Theodor Schwann proposed the **cell theory**, and Rudolf Virchow completed it
in 1855 with *omnis cellula e cellula* — every cell arises from a pre-existing
cell.

::: definition Cell theory
1. All living organisms are composed of one or more cells.
2. The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life.
3. All cells arise from pre-existing cells by division.
:::

::: example Worked example 1.3 — magnification
**Problem.** (a) A microscope has a 10× eyepiece and a 45× objective. What is the
total magnification? (b) On a photomicrograph taken at ×600, a chloroplast
measures 2.7 mm. Find its actual length in micrometres.

**Solution.**

(a) Magnifications multiply:

$$ M = 10 \times 45 = 450\times $$

(b) Magnification $= \dfrac{\text{image size}}{\text{actual size}}$, so

$$ \text{actual size} = \frac{2.7\ \text{mm}}{600} = 0.0045\ \text{mm} $$

Since $1\ \text{mm} = 1000\ \mu\text{m}$, the chloroplast is
$0.0045 \times 1000 = 4.5\ \mu\text{m}$ long — a normal value.
:::

### Prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells

A **prokaryotic** cell has no nuclear envelope, so its DNA lies naked in the
cytoplasm as a **nucleoid**. It has no membrane-bound organelles at all. Bacteria
and cyanobacteria are the only prokaryotes.

A **eukaryotic** cell has a true nucleus bounded by a double envelope, and a
whole set of membrane-bound organelles. Protists, fungi, plants and animals are
eukaryotes.

| Feature | Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
|---|---|---|
| Size | 1–10 μm | 10–100 μm |
| Nuclear envelope | absent | present |
| Genetic material | single circular DNA, no histones | linear DNA bound to histones |
| Nucleolus | absent | present |
| Membrane-bound organelles | absent | mitochondria, plastids, ER, Golgi, lysosomes |
| Ribosomes | 70S | 80S in cytoplasm; 70S in mitochondria and plastids |
| Cell wall | peptidoglycan (murein) | cellulose in plants, chitin in fungi, absent in animals |
| Flagellum | one fibril of flagellin | 9 + 2 arrangement of microtubules |
| Division | binary fission, no spindle | mitosis or meiosis, with a spindle |

::: example Worked example 1.4 — why cells stay small
**Problem.** Treat two cells as cubes of side 10 μm and 30 μm. Calculate the
surface area, volume and surface-area-to-volume ratio of each, and comment.

**Solution.** For a cube of side $a$, surface area $= 6a^{2}$ and volume $= a^{3}$,
so the ratio is

$$ \frac{S}{V} = \frac{6a^{2}}{a^{3}} = \frac{6}{a} $$

For $a = 10\ \mu\text{m}$: $S = 600\ \mu\text{m}^{2}$,
$V = 1000\ \mu\text{m}^{3}$, $S/V = 0.6\ \mu\text{m}^{-1}$.

For $a = 30\ \mu\text{m}$: $S = 5400\ \mu\text{m}^{2}$,
$V = 27\,000\ \mu\text{m}^{3}$, $S/V = 0.2\ \mu\text{m}^{-1}$.

Trebling the side divides the ratio by three. Materials enter through the
surface but are used by the whole volume, so a large cell cannot supply its
interior fast enough by diffusion. That is why cells stay small, and why cells
that must absorb a lot — root hairs, intestinal cells — throw out extensions to
raise the surface area instead of growing bigger.
:::

### Cell wall

The plant cell wall is a non-living, freely permeable layer outside the plasma
membrane. It is laid down in three parts:

- the **middle lamella**, shared between neighbouring cells and made of calcium
  and magnesium pectate, which cements them together;
- the **primary wall**, thin and elastic, of cellulose microfibrils in a matrix
  of hemicellulose and pectin, laid down while the cell is still growing;
- the **secondary wall**, thick and rigid, deposited inside the primary wall when
  growth stops, often impregnated with lignin, suberin or cutin.

Thin unthickened spots called **pits** are crossed by fine cytoplasmic threads,
the **plasmodesmata**, which link the protoplasm of neighbouring cells into one
continuous system (the symplast). The wall gives shape, mechanical strength and
protection, prevents bursting when the cell takes up water, and permits the
turgor on which a herbaceous stem stands up. Fungal walls are chitin, bacterial
walls peptidoglycan, diatom walls silica.

A bilayer of phospholipids forms the continuous framework. Each phospholipid has
a hydrophilic phosphate "head" facing the watery surroundings and two hydrophobic
fatty-acid "tails" facing inwards. Protein molecules are scattered through it
like a mosaic: **intrinsic (integral)** proteins span the bilayer and act as
channels, carriers and pumps, while **extrinsic (peripheral)** proteins sit on one
surface. Cholesterol molecules wedged between the tails regulate fluidity, and
short carbohydrate chains attached to lipids and proteins (glycolipids and
glycoproteins) act as recognition markers on the outer face. Both layers can flow
sideways — hence *fluid*.

The membrane is **selectively permeable**. Small non-polar molecules (O₂, CO₂)
cross by simple diffusion; water by osmosis, largely through aquaporin channels;
glucose and amino acids by facilitated diffusion through carrier proteins; and
ions against their gradient by **active transport**, which needs ATP — the
sodium–potassium pump is the standard example. Bulk material enters by
endocytosis and leaves by exocytosis.

::: caution "Semi-permeable" is not the same as "selectively permeable"
A semi-permeable membrane (like visking tubing) lets solvent through and holds
solute back, purely by pore size. A living plasma membrane *chooses*: it can
take in K⁺ while rejecting Na⁺ of the same size. Always write **selectively
permeable** for a living membrane.
:::

A mitochondrion has **two membranes**. The outer is smooth and permeable; the
inner is folded inwards into shelves called **cristae**, which greatly increase
the area available for the electron transport chain. The space between the two
membranes is the outer chamber; the fluid inside the inner membrane is the
**matrix**. Studding the inner membrane are stalked **F₁ particles** (oxysomes),
which are the ATP synthase enzyme.

The matrix holds the enzymes of the Krebs cycle, and also **circular DNA and 70S
ribosomes** of its own — so a mitochondrion can make some of its own proteins and
divides by fission. It is therefore called a **semi-autonomous organelle**, and
this is the evidence for the endosymbiotic theory. Because aerobic respiration
and most ATP synthesis happen here, the mitochondrion is called the *powerhouse*
of the cell.

### Plastids

Plastids are double-membraned organelles found only in plants and some protists.
Three types are recognised by their pigment:

- **Chloroplasts** — green, containing chlorophyll; the site of photosynthesis.
- **Chromoplasts** — yellow, orange or red, containing carotenoids; they colour
  petals, carrot roots and ripe tomatoes and attract pollinators and dispersers.
- **Leucoplasts** — colourless storage plastids of underground organs:
  **amyloplasts** store starch, **elaioplasts** oil and **aleuroplasts** protein.

A chloroplast is a biconvex disc about 4–6 μm long. Inside the double envelope
lies a colourless ground substance, the **stroma**, containing circular DNA, 70S
ribosomes, starch grains and the enzymes of the Calvin cycle. Suspended in it is
a system of flattened membranous sacs, the **thylakoids**. Where thylakoids are
stacked like a pile of coins the stack is a **granum** (10–100 thylakoids, about
40–60 grana per chloroplast); the tubes connecting one granum to the next are the
**stroma lamellae**. Chlorophyll sits in the thylakoid membrane, so the light
reaction happens in the grana and the dark reaction in the stroma.

### Endoplasmic reticulum

Discovered by Porter, Claude and Fullam (1945), the ER is a network of membranous
tubules, vesicles and flattened sacs (cisternae) running through the cytoplasm
and continuous with the outer membrane of the nuclear envelope. It is absent from
prokaryotes and from mature red blood cells.

**Rough ER** carries ribosomes on its outer surface; it synthesises proteins for
export and channels them to the Golgi body. **Smooth ER** has no ribosomes and
makes lipids and steroids, stores calcium ions in muscle (as sarcoplasmic
reticulum) and detoxifies drugs in liver cells. The ER also gives the cytoplasm
mechanical support — it is often called the cell's skeletal framework — and its
membranes supply material for the nuclear envelope after division.

### Golgi apparatus

Described by Camillo Golgi in **1898**, it consists of a stack of 4–8 flattened,
slightly curved cisternae with tubules and vesicles at the edges. In plant cells
the separate stacks are called **dictyosomes**. The convex face towards the ER is
the **cis** (forming) face and the concave face is the **trans** (maturing) face.

Its work is packaging and dispatch. Proteins arrive from the rough ER in
vesicles, are chemically modified — glycosylation, the addition of sugars is
typical — sorted, concentrated and released at the trans face in secretory
vesicles. The Golgi body also forms **lysosomes**, secretes the polysaccharides
of the cell wall, and supplies the vesicles whose fusion builds the **cell plate**
in plant cytokinesis.

### Lysosomes

Isolated by Christian de Duve in **1955**, lysosomes are simple spherical sacs
bounded by a *single* membrane, 0.2–0.8 μm across, budded off the Golgi
apparatus. They contain some fifty **acid hydrolases** — proteases, lipases,
nucleases, phosphatases — which work best at about pH 5, maintained inside the
sac by proton pumps.

Their functions are digestive:

- **Heterophagy** — digestion of food particles or bacteria taken in by
  endocytosis.
- **Autophagy** — controlled digestion of the cell's own worn-out organelles, the
  parts being recycled.
- **Autolysis** — if the membrane bursts, the enzymes digest the whole cell. For
  this reason lysosomes are called the **suicide bags** of the cell. Autolysis is
  a normal part of development: it removes the tadpole's tail.

### Ribosomes

Ribosomes are the smallest organelles and the only ones with **no membrane**.
First seen by George Palade (1953), each is a dense particle of roughly equal
parts ribosomal RNA and protein, built of one large and one small subunit that
join only in the presence of Mg²⁺ and only when protein synthesis begins.

Eukaryotic cytoplasmic ribosomes are **80S** (60S + 40S). Prokaryotic ribosomes,
and those inside mitochondria and chloroplasts, are **70S** (50S + 30S). ("S" is
the Svedberg unit of sedimentation rate, which is why 60 + 40 does not give 100.)
Ribosomes occur free in the cytoplasm or attached to the rough ER; several
translating the same mRNA form a chain called a **polysome**. The ribosome is
where amino acids are joined in the order specified by mRNA, so it is called the
**protein factory** of the cell.

### Nucleus

Discovered by Robert Brown in **1831**, the nucleus is the control centre. It is
usually spherical, 5–25 μm across, and normally one per cell (though a *Rhizopus*
hypha is multinucleate and a mature sieve tube has none).

It has four parts. The **nuclear envelope** is a double membrane, the outer one
continuous with the ER, perforated by **nuclear pores** through which RNA and
ribosomal subunits leave and proteins enter. The **nucleoplasm** is the ground
fluid. The **chromatin** is a network of DNA-and-histone threads, staining
darkly; loose, active regions are euchromatin and condensed, inactive regions
heterochromatin. The **nucleolus**, a dense body without a membrane, is the site
of rRNA synthesis and ribosome assembly — cells that make a lot of protein have
large nucleoli.

### Chromosomes

At division the chromatin condenses into distinct **chromosomes**, first named by
Waldeyer (1888). Chemically a chromosome is about 35–40 % DNA, 50–55 % histone
protein, plus non-histone protein and a little RNA. The DNA is wound about 1¾
turns round an octamer of histones to form a **nucleosome**, the bead-like
repeating unit of chromatin.

A metaphase chromosome consists of two identical **sister chromatids** joined at
a narrow **primary constriction**, the **centromere**, on which the disc-shaped
kinetochore sits and to which spindle fibres attach. Some chromosomes have a
**secondary constriction** cutting off a small knob, the **satellite**; these are
SAT chromosomes and they organise the nucleolus. The ends are capped by
**telomeres**, which stop chromosomes fusing.

| Type | Position of centromere | Shape at anaphase |
|---|---|---|
| Metacentric | exactly in the middle | V |
| Sub-metacentric | slightly off centre | L or J |
| Acrocentric | near one end | J, with one very short arm |
| Telocentric | at the very end | I (rod) |

The number of chromosomes is constant for a species — 46 in humans, 16 in onion
(*Allium cepa*), 24 in pea, 20 in maize. Chromosomes that govern body characters
are **autosomes**; those governing sex are **sex chromosomes** or allosomes.

### Cilia and flagella

These are hair-like extensions of the cell surface used for movement, for
creating a current, or as sensory structures. **Cilia** are short (5–10 μm) and
very numerous; **flagella** are long (up to 150 μm) and few. Both have identical
internal structure.

A cross-section shows the **9 + 2 arrangement**: nine pairs (doublets) of
microtubules arranged in a ring round two single central microtubules, the whole
bundle called the **axoneme** and sheathed by an extension of the plasma
membrane. The microtubules are made of the protein tubulin. Each outer doublet
carries **dynein arms**, whose ATP-driven sliding against the neighbouring doublet
bends the cilium. Beneath the surface each cilium arises from a **basal body**
which has nine *triplets* and no central pair — a 9 + 0 arrangement, identical to
a centriole. Bacterial flagella are quite different: a single solid fibril of the
protein flagellin, with no 9 + 2 pattern.

### Cell inclusions

Cell inclusions are the **non-living** substances lying free in the cytoplasm or
vacuole — reserve food, secretions and waste. They are also called
ergastic substances.

| Group | Examples | Where found |
|---|---|---|
| Reserve food | starch grains, glycogen granules, oil drops, aleurone (protein) grains | potato tuber, liver, castor seed, wheat grain |
| Secretory | enzymes, hormones, nectar, essential oils | glandular cells |
| Excretory / waste | calcium oxalate crystals (raphides, druses), cystoliths of calcium carbonate, tannins, resins, gums, alkaloids, latex | *Colocasia* petiole, *Ficus* leaf, *Euphorbia* and *Calotropis* stems |
| Pigments | anthocyanin in the cell sap | red and blue petals |

## 1.3 Cell division

### The cell cycle

cell and its division into two. It has two unequal parts: a long **interphase**,
and a short **M phase** in which division actually occurs.

Interphase is not a resting stage — it is the busiest part of the cycle. It is
divided into:

- **G₁ (first gap)** — the cell grows, makes RNA, proteins and organelles. The
  restriction point at the end of G₁ decides whether the cell divides at all.
- **S (synthesis)** — DNA replicates. Each chromosome becomes two chromatids, so
  the DNA content doubles from 2C to 4C while the chromosome number stays 2n.
- **G₂ (second gap)** — more growth; spindle proteins (tubulin) are made and the
  cell is checked for damaged DNA before mitosis is allowed to start.

Cells that stop dividing — mature nerve and skeletal muscle cells — leave the
cycle at G₁ and enter a quiescent **G₀** state. In a typical human cell the
cycle takes about 24 hours, of which M phase is only about 1 hour.

::: caution The chromosome number does not change during S phase
After replication a cell has 2n chromosomes each of **two** chromatids — not 4n
chromosomes. The chromosome number is counted by centromeres, and each
chromosome still has exactly one.
:::

### Amitosis

**Amitosis**, or direct cell division, was first described by Remak (1841). The
nucleus simply elongates, a constriction appears in the middle and it pinches
into two, after which the cytoplasm divides. There is **no spindle, no
condensation of chromatin into visible chromosomes and no equal distribution of
the genetic material**, so the daughter cells may be genetically unequal.

Amitosis occurs in prokaryotes, in yeast, in *Amoeba* and other protozoa, in the
endosperm of some seeds, in foetal membranes, and in old or diseased tissue —
including cancerous cells. It is fast and cheap but unreliable, which is why
almost all normal eukaryotic division is mitotic.

### Mitosis

**Mitosis** (Flemming, 1882) is the division of a somatic cell that produces two
daughter cells with exactly the same chromosome number as the parent. It is
therefore called **equational division**. It occurs in the growing regions of a
plant — root and shoot apical meristems, cambium — and throughout animal tissue
during growth and repair. It has two steps: **karyokinesis** (division of the
nucleus) followed by **cytokinesis** (division of the cytoplasm).

**Prophase.** Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes, each already made of
two sister chromatids joined at the centromere. The chromosomes thicken and
shorten. In animals the two pairs of centrioles move to opposite poles and a
spindle of microtubules begins to form between them; plants form the spindle
without centrioles. By the end of prophase the nucleolus and the nuclear envelope
have disappeared.

**Metaphase.** The spindle is complete. The chromosomes move to the equator and
line up with their centromeres exactly on the equatorial plane, forming the
**metaphase plate**, with the arms pointing outwards. Spindle fibres attach to
the kinetochore of each chromatid from opposite poles. Chromosomes are at their
shortest and thickest, so this is the best stage for counting and studying them.

**Anaphase.** Each centromere splits. The two sister chromatids — now daughter
chromosomes — are pulled to opposite poles as the spindle fibres shorten. They
trail their arms behind the centromere, giving the V, L, J and I shapes that
reveal the centromere's position. This is the shortest stage.

**Telophase.** The daughter chromosomes reach the poles, uncoil and lengthen back
into chromatin. The nuclear envelope re-forms round each group from ER membranes,
the nucleolus reappears and the spindle breaks down. Two nuclei now exist in one
cell.

**Cytokinesis.** In animal cells a ring of actin filaments pinches the cell in
from outside — a **cleavage furrow**, growing centripetally. In plant cells the
rigid wall prevents furrowing, so Golgi vesicles line up at the equator and fuse
to form a **cell plate** that grows outwards (centrifugally) until it reaches the
side walls and becomes the new middle lamella.

Mitosis matters because it keeps the chromosome number constant generation after
generation of cells, provides growth and repair, replaces worn-out cells, and is
the basis of asexual and vegetative reproduction — which is why a cutting of
sugarcane or a potato tuber gives plants identical to the parent.

### Meiosis

**Meiosis** (Farmer and Moore, 1905) is the division in which one diploid cell
gives **four haploid cells**, the chromosome number being halved. It is therefore
called **reduction division**. In animals it occurs in the gonads during gamete
formation; in flowering plants it occurs in the anther (giving microspores) and
in the ovule (giving the megaspore). It consists of two successive divisions,
meiosis I and meiosis II, but the DNA replicates only **once**, before meiosis I.

**Meiosis I — the reduction division.** Prophase I is long and is divided into
five sub-stages:

| Sub-stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| Leptotene | chromosomes appear as long thin threads |
| Zygotene | homologous chromosomes pair lengthwise — **synapsis** — forming bivalents |
| Pachytene | each bivalent shows four chromatids (a tetrad); non-sister chromatids exchange segments — **crossing over** |
| Diplotene | homologues start to separate but stay joined at X-shaped **chiasmata** |
| Diakinesis | chiasmata slip to the ends (terminalisation); nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear |

::: memory Prophase I in order
**L Z P D D** — *Lazy Zebras Prefer Dry Deserts*: Leptotene, Zygotene,
Pachytene, Diplotene, Diakinesis. Remember that **crossing over happens at
pachytene** but the **chiasmata become visible at diplotene**.
:::

In **metaphase I** the bivalents line up at the equator with the two homologues
of each pair on *either side* of the plane — a double metaphase plate. Which
homologue faces which pole is decided independently for every pair; this is
**independent assortment** and it is the second source of variation. In
**anaphase I** whole chromosomes, each still made of two chromatids, move to
opposite poles: the centromeres do **not** split. This is where the number is
halved. **Telophase I** re-forms two haploid nuclei, and a short **interkinesis**
follows with no S phase.

**Meiosis II** is essentially a mitotic division of each haploid cell. In
prophase II the envelope breaks down again, in metaphase II the chromosomes line
up singly, in anaphase II the centromeres split at last and sister chromatids
separate, and in telophase II four haploid nuclei are formed. Cytokinesis gives
four haploid cells, none of them genetically identical to any other.

| Feature | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Where | somatic (body) cells | reproductive cells |
| Number of divisions | one | two |
| Daughter cells | 2 | 4 |
| Chromosome number | unchanged (2n → 2n) | halved (2n → n) |
| Pairing of homologues | absent | present (synapsis) |
| Crossing over | absent | present |
| Centromere splits in | anaphase | anaphase II only |
| Daughter cells are | genetically identical | genetically different |
| Purpose | growth, repair, asexual reproduction | gamete/spore formation, variation |

::: key Why meiosis is necessary
If gametes were made by mitosis, fertilisation would double the chromosome
number every generation. Meiosis halves it first, so fusion restores exactly 2n
and the species number stays constant. At the same time, crossing over and
independent assortment shuffle the parental genes, producing the variation on
which natural selection acts.
:::

::: example Worked example 1.5 — counting chromosomes through division
**Problem.** The onion *Allium cepa* has $2n = 16$. For a root-tip cell,
state (a) the number of chromosomes and of chromatids at mitotic metaphase,
(b) the number of chromosomes moving to each pole at mitotic anaphase, and
(c) the number of bivalents at metaphase I and the chromosome number of each cell
at the end of meiosis in an anther.

**Solution.**

(a) DNA has replicated in S phase, so there are still **16 chromosomes**, but
each has 2 chromatids:

$$ 16 \times 2 = 32\ \text{chromatids} $$

(b) At anaphase each centromere splits, so all 32 chromatids become separate
chromosomes and half go to each pole:

$$ \frac{32}{2} = 16\ \text{chromosomes to each pole} $$

— which is why each daughter cell is again $2n = 16$.

(c) A bivalent is a pair of homologues, so

$$ \text{bivalents} = \frac{16}{2} = 8 $$

Meiosis halves the number, so each of the four microspores has
$n = 8$ chromosomes.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Condensation joins monomers into polymers with the loss of water; hydrolysis
  reverses it. Carbohydrates $\to$ glycosidic bonds, proteins $\to$ peptide
  bonds, lipids $\to$ ester bonds, nucleic acids $\to$ phosphodiester bonds.
- Lipids yield about 39 kJ g⁻¹ against 17 kJ g⁻¹ for carbohydrate and protein.
- DNA is an antiparallel right-handed double helix: A=T (2 H-bonds), G≡C
  (3 H-bonds), 0.34 nm per base pair, 3.4 nm and 10 base pairs per turn,
  diameter 2 nm. Chargaff: $A = T$ and $G = C$.
- Enzymes are protein catalysts that lower activation energy; they are specific
  and have an optimum temperature and pH. Induced fit, not lock and key, is the
  accepted model.
- Prokaryotes lack a nuclear envelope and membrane-bound organelles and have 70S
  ribosomes; eukaryotes have both and 80S cytoplasmic ribosomes.
- Mitochondria and chloroplasts are double-membraned and semi-autonomous, with
  circular DNA and 70S ribosomes; cristae and thylakoids increase membrane area.
- The plasma membrane is a fluid mosaic (Singer and Nicolson, 1972), about
  7.5 nm thick and selectively permeable.
- Cell cycle $=$ interphase (G₁, S, G₂) $+$ M phase. DNA doubles 2C $\to$ 4C in
  S phase but the chromosome number does not change.
- Mitosis is equational: 1 cell $\to$ 2 identical cells, $2n \to 2n$. Meiosis is
  reductional: 1 cell $\to$ 4 different cells, $2n \to n$, with crossing over at
  pachytene and reduction at anaphase I.
- Surface-area-to-volume ratio for a cube is $6/a$; it falls as cells grow, which
  sets an upper limit on cell size.

::: note Answer outline to Group C question 1
Prophase I with the five sub-stages (leptotene — thin threads; zygotene —
synapsis forming bivalents; pachytene — tetrads and crossing over; diplotene —
chiasmata visible; diakinesis — terminalisation, envelope and nucleolus gone);
metaphase I — bivalents on a double metaphase plate; anaphase I — whole
homologous chromosomes separate, centromeres intact, number halved; telophase I —
two haploid nuclei, then interkinesis with no DNA replication.

Variation arises from two independent sources: **crossing over** at pachytene,
which exchanges segments between non-sister chromatids of homologues and creates
new gene combinations on a single chromosome; and **independent assortment** at
metaphase I, since each bivalent orients on the plate independently, giving
$2^{n}$ possible gamete types from $n$ pairs — $2^{23}$, over 8 million, in
humans. Random fertilisation then multiplies the variation further.
:::
