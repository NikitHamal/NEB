Every kind of living being is different from the others, yet all of them depend
on one another and on their surroundings. People get fodder, firewood, fruit,
medicine, timber and water from forests; these benefits are called **ecosystem
services**. If we use them too much, their sources are destroyed. So every
social, economic and development activity must use nature wisely and conserve
it. This unit explains biodiversity, its importance, why it is declining, how it
is conserved, and how development can be made **sustainable**.

## 4.1 Biodiversity: meaning, levels and importance

Organisms living in water, deserts, cold places and hot, moist places have
different body structures. Even plants of the same species in one field differ
in height, number of leaves and size of cobs.

::: definition Biodiversity
**Biodiversity** is the variety of living beings — plants, animals and
microorganisms — found in different habitats of the earth, including the
differences within a species, between species and between ecosystems. It shows
the **species richness** of a place.
:::

The **Earth Summit** held in **Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, in 1992** defined
biodiversity as the variation among organisms of the same or different kinds in
their genes, species and ecosystems. The **Convention on Biological Diversity
(CBD)** describes it as the variety among all land and water organisms.

The variety of plants in a place is **floral diversity**; the variety of animals
is **faunal diversity**.

### 4.1.1 Types (levels) of biodiversity

| Type | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Genetic diversity** | differences among individuals of the same species, caused by differences in their genes | all humans are *Homo sapiens*, but hair, skin and eye colour, height and nose shape differ; many varieties of rice |
| **Species diversity** | the number and kinds of species living in a place | producers, herbivores and carnivores of a forest; the frog genus *Rana* has more than ten species |
| **Ecosystem diversity** | the variety of ecosystems (land and water; tropical to alpine) with their physical conditions and organisms | Terai grassland, sal forest, alpine meadow, lake, river |

::: note Activity 4.1 — quadrat study of a grassland
Mark a 1 m × 1 m square (a **quadrat**) on grassland with a measuring tape, four
nails and a cord. Collect and count every kind of plant and animal inside it,
keep them in separate bags and fill a table: name of plant / number, name of
animal / number. The more kinds you find, the higher the species diversity.
Return living animals to the grass afterwards.
:::

::: example Using quadrat counts
**Problem.** In a 1 m² quadrat a class found 36 grass plants, 4 clover plants and 3
grasshoppers. Estimate the number of clover plants in a grassy field of 250 m², assuming the
quadrat is typical. How many kinds of organisms were found?
**Solution.** Clover per m² = 4. In 250 m²: $4\times250=1000$ clover plants (an estimate).
Kinds found = 3 (grass, clover, grasshopper) — this is the species count of the sample.
More quadrats at different spots give a better estimate.
:::

### 4.1.2 Importance of biodiversity

1. It provides food, shelter and livelihood (nutrition and income) for every organism.
2. It keeps the environment balanced and increases the productivity of ecosystems.
3. It allows the sustainable use of biological resources (timber, medicine, fibre).
4. It helps in environmental conservation and sustainable development.
5. It promotes **ecotourism** and strengthens the national economy.
6. A rich variety helps living beings adapt to difficult conditions such as
   climate change.

### 4.1.3 Present status of biodiversity in Nepal

Nepal covers only about **0.1 %** of the world's land, yet it is very rich in
biodiversity because its altitude rises from about **59 m** (Kechana Kalan,
Jhapa) to **8848.86 m** (Sagarmatha) within a short distance, giving tropical to
alpine climates, fertile soils and monsoon rain.

- Nepal ranks about **27th in the world**, 10th in Asia and 2nd in South Asia in biodiversity.
- It has about **3.2 %** of the world's plant species and **1.1 %** of its animal species.
- There are more than **118 types of ecosystems** and more than 15 types of forest.
- The **spiny babbler** is found only in Nepal (endemic). The one-horned rhinoceros,
  Bengal tiger and wild yak are rare animals found here.
- Nepal has about **32 species of rhododendron**, and Jumla's **Marshi rice** grows at
  about 3050 m, one of the highest rice fields in the world.

## 4.2 Threats to biodiversity and its conservation

**Biodiversity degradation** is the decline in the number and kinds of plants and
animals caused by natural disasters and human activities. Some species are close
to extinction; the snow leopard of Nepal is threatened, and the pygmy hog is no
longer found in Nepal's wild.

**Causes of biodiversity degradation**

1. **Overgrazing** in forests damages seeds and seedlings, so new plants cannot grow.
2. **Industrial pollution** of air, water and soil.
3. **Unplanned mining and blasting** cause noise, landslides and soil erosion.
4. **Unplanned construction** of roads, buildings, dams and bridges destroys habitats.
5. **Excess collection of fodder and firewood** leads to deforestation and dries water sources.
6. **Clearing forests** for farmland and houses as the population grows.
7. **Forest fires** kill plants and animals.
8. **Floods and landslides.**
9. **Global warming** caused by climate change.
10. **Poaching and illegal trade** of wild animals and their parts.
11. **Invasive plants**: *banmara* (*Mikania*, *Lantana*) on land and water hyacinth
    in water crowd out native plants.
12. **Illegal extraction of sand, gravel and stones** from rivers causes bank
    cutting and dries nearby farms.

### 4.2.1 Conservation of biodiversity

| In-situ conservation | Ex-situ conservation |
|---|---|
| conserving organisms **in their natural habitat** | conserving organisms **outside** their natural habitat, in a small, controlled area |
| animals interact freely with nature | animals live in cages or enclosures; diet and conditions must be managed |
| large area; all species protected, endangered or not | small area; best for **endangered** species |
| cheaper and natural | costly and laborious |
| hard to control poaching, fire and disease | easy to look after, treat and study |
| national parks, wildlife reserves, hunting reserve, conservation areas | zoo, botanical garden, nursery, safari park, aquarium, seed bank, gene bank |

**Protected areas of Nepal** (as listed in the textbook): **12 national parks**
(Chitwan, Sagarmatha, Bardiya, Rara, Khaptad, Langtang, Makalu-Barun,
Shey-Phoksundo, Shivapuri-Nagarjun, Parsa, Banke, Shuklaphanta), **1 wildlife
reserve** (Koshi Tappu), **1 hunting reserve** (Dhorpatan) and **6 conservation
areas** (Annapurna, Kanchenjunga, Manaslu, Krishnasar, Gaurishankar, Api-Nampa).
Examples of ex-situ conservation are the Central Zoo (Jawalakhel) and the
National Botanical Garden (Godawari).

**Other measures:** control poaching and illegal trade; make more protected areas;
control overgrazing, fire, pollution and unplanned urbanisation; make and enforce
strong laws; and educate people about the value of biodiversity. Nepal's
**community forest** programme has increased forest cover by handing forests to
local user groups.

::: example Why it is hard to conserve a native species ex-situ
**Question.** Give a reason: it is difficult to conserve a native species by the
ex-situ method.

**Answer.** In ex-situ conservation the organism is kept away from its natural
habitat in a small area. Its natural food, climate and companions must be supplied
artificially, which is costly and laborious; wild animals dislike the prison-like
life and may not breed. So it is used mainly for endangered species, while in-situ
conservation is better for most native species.
:::

## 4.3 Ecosystem, food chain and food web

An **ecosystem** is a community of living beings together with the non-living
surroundings (soil, water, air, light, temperature) with which they interact.

A **food chain** is the series of organisms through which food (energy) passes,
each one eating the one before it. Every chain starts with a green plant
(producer).

**Grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → eagle**

| Level | Name | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| makes its own food | producer | grass, rice, sal tree |
| eats plants | primary consumer (herbivore) | grasshopper, deer, goat |
| eats herbivores | secondary consumer | frog, jackal |
| eats other carnivores | tertiary consumer | snake, eagle, tiger |
| breaks down dead bodies | decomposer | bacteria, fungi |

In a real ecosystem many food chains are joined because one animal eats many
kinds of food. This network of food chains is a **food web**. If one species is
lost, many others are affected — this is why biodiversity keeps an ecosystem stable.

```figure caption="A simple food web of the Terai. Each arrow means 'is eaten by'. Follow one line of arrows to get a food chain."
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.0, 2.8))
ax.set_xlim(0, 10)
ax.set_ylim(0, 5)
ax.axis("off")
nodes = {"grass": (1.0, 1.4), "rice plant": (1.0, 3.6), "deer": (3.8, 3.6),
         "grasshopper": (3.8, 1.4), "frog": (6.2, 0.7), "tiger": (6.8, 3.6),
         "snake": (6.2, 2.2), "eagle": (9.0, 1.4)}
for name, (x, y) in nodes.items():
    ax.text(x, y, name, ha="center", va="center", fontsize=7.8, color=INK,
            bbox=dict(boxstyle="round,pad=0.28", fc="white",
                      ec=SERIES[2] if x < 2 else ACCENT, lw=1.0))
half = {"grass": 0.55, "rice plant": 0.95, "deer": 0.5, "grasshopper": 1.05, "frog": 0.5,
        "snake": 0.6, "tiger": 0.55, "eagle": 0.6}
for a, b in [("grass", "deer"), ("grass", "grasshopper"), ("rice plant", "deer"),
             ("rice plant", "grasshopper"), ("grasshopper", "frog"), ("deer", "tiger"), ("frog", "snake"), ("snake", "eagle"), ("frog", "eagle")]:
    xa, ya = nodes[a]
    xb, yb = nodes[b]
    dx, dy = xb - xa, yb - ya
    L = (dx ** 2 + (dy * 2.0) ** 2) ** 0.5
    sa, sb = (half[a] + 0.1) / L, (half[b] + 0.15) / L
    sa = max(sa, 0.32 / L) if abs(dx) < 0.1 else sa
    sb = max(sb, 0.4 / L) if abs(dx) < 0.1 else sb
    if abs(dx) < 0.1:
        sa, sb = 0.38 / abs(dy), 0.38 / abs(dy)
    ax.annotate("", xy=(xb - dx * sb, yb - dy * sb), xytext=(xa + dx * sa, ya + dy * sa),
                arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="->", color=MUTED, lw=0.9))
fig.tight_layout()
```

::: caution The arrow direction
The arrow in a food chain points from the food to the eater ("is eaten by"):
grass → deer, not deer → grass.
:::

## 4.4 Sustainable development: meaning, aspects and principles

::: definition Sustainable development
According to the **Brundtland Commission (1987)**, sustainable development is
"development that meets the needs of the present generation without
compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs." In
practice it means considering the **economic, social, cultural and environmental**
sides of every project so that its benefits last a long time.
:::

*Example.* A bridge or road built at a landslide-prone place lasts only if
landslide control (retaining walls, drainage, planting grass and trees on the
slope) is done at the same time. Then many generations benefit. In the textbook
story, Norbu's father could not repair a broken paddy-field ridge with mud alone
while water was flowing; diverting the water first, and binding the mud with
leafy twigs and grass, makes the repair last.

| Development | Sustainable development |
|---|---|
| aims at quick progress now | aims at progress now **and** in the future |
| may ignore the environment and society | balances economy, society and environment |
| may exhaust resources | uses resources wisely and conserves them |
| benefits may be short-lived | benefits last for generations |

### 4.4.1 Aspects (three pillars) of sustainable development

1. **Economic aspect** — steady, balanced economic growth of people and the nation
   through fair distribution and use of resources, without exploitation.
2. **Social aspect** — fair progress of the whole community: ending poverty, equal
   access to resources, empowerment of women and marginalised groups, people's
   participation, preservation of culture, peace and **social justice**.
3. **Environmental aspect** — conserving the fertility of land, water, air,
   minerals, climate and biodiversity; development projects must not destroy
   habitats or cause landslides and river-bank erosion.

```figure caption="The three pillars of sustainable development. Development is sustainable only where all three overlap."

```

### 4.4.2 Principles of sustainable development

1. **Principle of ecosystem and biodiversity conservation** — keep in mind the
   carrying capacity of the earth; conserve natural resources, use renewable
   energy, search for alternatives, control pollution, conserve biodiversity.
2. **Principle of population control and human resource management** — humans use
   far more resources than any other organism, so population must be kept within a
   limit, and people's knowledge and skills must be developed by education and training.
3. **Principle of cultural conservation and public participation** — conserve
   cultural heritage and involve people; when villagers build their own irrigation
   canal they feel ownership and look after it, so it lasts long.

**Importance:** it balances the environment and controls pollution; conserves
resources and teaches their wise use; guides the building of big projects; reduces
inequality and brings social justice and harmony; and gives economic opportunity
to everyone fairly.

::: tip "Skilled human resource is the foundation as well as the destructor"
Skilled people build safe roads, hydropower and industries — the foundation of
development. The same skills, used only for profit (careless blasting, cutting
forests, polluting rivers), can destroy the environment. So skills must be used
with responsibility.
:::

## 4.5 Sustainable development goals, environment protection and local initiatives

The **Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)** were adopted by the **70th General
Assembly of the United Nations** with **193 countries** in **2015** for the period
**2016–2030**. They have **17 goals, 169 targets and 232 indicators**. **Goal 15
(Life on land)** deals with terrestrial ecosystems, forests, desertification and
biodiversity. Other goals cover food security, quality education, drinking water and
sanitation, infrastructure, employment, climate action (goal 13) and life below
water (goal 14). Nepal has prepared its own roadmap and indicators.

**Nepal's efforts**

- A **Climate Change Council** (since 2009) runs programmes on water resources,
  watersheds and biodiversity. To draw world attention to climate change, Nepal's
  cabinet met at **Kalapatthar** near Everest base camp in **2009**.
- The water level of the glacial lake **Tsho Rolpa** (Rolwaling, Dolakha) has been
  lowered through a channel to reduce the risk of a glacial lake outburst flood.
- The **community forest** programme, strengthened by the Forest Act of 1993, has
  increased forest area and density by giving management to local people.
- Periodic plans focus on poverty reduction, vocational education, employment and a
  "neat, clean and green" environment.
- The **National Environment Policy, 2076** and the **Environment Protection Act,
  2076** give people the right to a clean environment, make polluters pay
  compensation and aim to reduce the effects of climate change.

**Local initiatives a school or ward can take:** tree planting and caring for the
saplings, eco-clubs, separating and composting waste, banning plastic bags,
protecting springs and ponds, building terraces and planting grass on slopes,
using improved cooking stoves and biogas, and building roads with drains and
retaining walls.

::: example Three suggestions for a sustainable road
**Question.** A road is being built in your village. Give three suggestions to the
construction company.

**Answer.** (1) Do an environmental study first and avoid steep, landslide-prone
slopes and water sources. (2) Build side drains, culverts and retaining walls, and
plant grass and trees on the cut slopes. (3) Dump the excavated soil at a safe place
(not into rivers or fields), avoid uncontrolled blasting, and employ local people so
that they take ownership of the road.
:::

## Chapter summary

- **Biodiversity** = variety of organisms; types: genetic, species and ecosystem
  diversity (Earth Summit, Rio de Janeiro, 1992).
- Importance: food and livelihood, balanced environment, sustainable resources,
  ecotourism, adaptation to climate change.
- Nepal: 0.1 % of world area, altitude 59 m – 8848.86 m, 3.2 % of plant and 1.1 %
  of animal species, 118 ecosystems; spiny babbler is endemic.
- Degradation: overgrazing, pollution, mining, unplanned construction,
  deforestation, fire, floods, global warming, poaching, invasive species.
- **In-situ** (national parks, reserves, conservation areas) and **ex-situ**
  (zoo, botanical garden, seed bank) conservation.
- Food chains start with producers; joined food chains form a food web.
- **Sustainable development** (Brundtland, 1987) has economic, social and
  environmental pillars, and principles of conservation, population control and
  public participation.
- **SDGs**: 2016–2030, 17 goals, 169 targets, 232 indicators; goal 15 is about
  life on land and biodiversity.
