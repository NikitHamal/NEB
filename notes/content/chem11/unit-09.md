## 9.1 Hydrogen

Hydrogen has the simplest possible atom — one proton, one electron, 1s¹. It sits
awkwardly in the periodic table because it resembles both group 1 (one valence
electron, forms H⁺) and group 17 (one short of a duplet, forms H⁻ in NaH).

### Atomic and nascent hydrogen

Ordinary hydrogen is **molecular**, H₂, and is unreactive at room temperature
because the H–H bond enthalpy is a large 436 kJ mol⁻¹.

- **Atomic hydrogen** is free H atoms, made by passing H₂ through an electric arc
  between tungsten electrodes at about 4000 °C. It lasts a fraction of a second
  and recombines, releasing enough heat for the *atomic hydrogen torch*.
- **Nascent hydrogen** is hydrogen released *inside* a reacting mixture, for
  example by adding zinc to dilute sulphuric acid in the presence of the
  substance to be reduced. It turns acidified KMnO₄ colourless and reduces FeCl₃
  to FeCl₂, whereas bubbling H₂ through the same solutions does nothing.

The difference is energy: nascent hydrogen carries the energy released by the
generating reaction and never has to pay the 436 kJ mol⁻¹ to split H₂.

### Isotopes of hydrogen

| Isotope | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Mass number | Abundance | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protium | ¹₁H | 1 | 0 | 1 | 99.985 % | ordinary hydrogen |
| Deuterium | ²₁H or D | 1 | 1 | 2 | 0.015 % | stable, heavy water |
| Tritium | ³₁H or T | 1 | 2 | 3 | trace | radioactive, β-emitter, half-life 12.3 yr |

**Uses.** Deuterium is a tracer for reaction mechanisms and, as D₂O, a reactor
moderator; tritium is a radioactive tracer in hydrology and is used in luminous
signs.

### Preparation of hydrogen

| Method | Equation |
|---|---|
| Active metal + dilute acid (**the lab method**, Kipp's apparatus) | Zn + H₂SO₄(dil) → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑ |
| Very active metal + cold water (violent) | 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂↑ |
| Amphoteric metal + alkali | Zn + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂↑ |
| Steam over red-hot coke (*water gas*) | C + H₂O --1273 K--> CO + H₂ |
| Steam reforming of methane (main industrial route) | CH₄ + H₂O --Ni, 1100 K--> CO + 3H₂ |
| Electrolysis of acidified water (purest H₂) | 2H₂O --electricity--> 2H₂↑ + O₂↑ |

### Properties of hydrogen

| Physical | Chemical |
|---|---|
| Colourless, odourless, tasteless, neutral to litmus | Burns in air: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, ΔH = −286 kJ per mol H₂O |
| Lightest known gas, 0.09 g L⁻¹ at STP | With halogens: H₂ + Cl₂ --sunlight--> 2HCl (explosive) |
| Very slightly soluble; b.p. −252.8 °C | With N₂: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (Haber process); with Na: 2Na + H₂ → 2NaH |
| Diffuses faster than any other gas | Reduces hot metal oxides: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O |

### Hydrogen as a fuel

Hydrogen has the highest energy content per gram of any chemical fuel — about
143 kJ g⁻¹, against 48 kJ g⁻¹ for petrol — and its only combustion product is
water. A **hydrogen fuel cell** runs the same reaction electrochemically and
delivers electricity far more efficiently than a petrol engine.

| Advantages | Problems |
|---|---|
| Highest calorific value per unit mass; product is only water — no CO₂, no SO₂, no soot | Very low density, so storage needs compression, liquefaction at −253 °C or metal hydrides |
| Renewable if electrolysed using hydropower; fuel cells are silent and efficient | Wide explosive range in air (4–75 %); most hydrogen today still comes from fossil methane |

For Nepal the attraction is clear: surplus monsoon hydro-electricity could
electrolyse water and be stored as hydrogen instead of being spilled.

### Heavy water, D₂O

Heavy water is deuterium oxide, D₂O, present to about 1 part in 6000 in ordinary
water. It is obtained by **prolonged electrolysis** of alkaline water: H₂O is
electrolysed faster than D₂O, so D₂O concentrates in the residue — about 30
litres of water leaves roughly 1 mL of nearly pure D₂O.

| Property | H₂O | D₂O |
|---|---|---|
| Molar mass (g mol⁻¹) | 18 | 20 |
| Melting point | 0 °C | 3.8 °C |
| Boiling point | 100 °C | 101.4 °C |
| Density at 25 °C | 0.997 g cm⁻³ | 1.104 g cm⁻³ |

**Uses.** As a **moderator** in nuclear reactors — it slows fast neutrons without
absorbing them; as a source of deuterium compounds
(Ca₃N₂ + 6D₂O → 3Ca(OD)₂ + 2ND₃); and as a tracer. It is *not* hard water or
water containing heavy metals — only the hydrogen is the heavier isotope.

::: example Volume of hydrogen from zinc
**Problem.** 6.5 g of granulated zinc is completely dissolved in excess dilute
sulphuric acid. Calculate the volume of hydrogen liberated at STP.
(Zn = 65, molar volume at STP = 22.4 L mol⁻¹.)

**Solution.**
Equation: Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑

Moles of zinc:
$$ n(\mathrm{Zn}) = \frac{6.5}{65} = 0.10\ \mathrm{mol} $$

From the equation, 1 mol Zn gives 1 mol H₂, so $n(\mathrm{H_2}) = 0.10$ mol.

$$ V = n \times 22.4 = 0.10 \times 22.4 = 2.24\ \mathrm{L} $$

**Answer: 2.24 L (2240 cm³) of hydrogen at STP.**
:::

## 9.2 Allotropes of Oxygen

### Allotropy

**Allotropy** is the existence of an element in two or more forms in the same
physical state, differing in physical properties but giving the same chemical
products. Oxygen has two allotropes: colourless, odourless, stable dioxygen O₂
(b.p. −183 °C) and pale blue, pungent, unstable ozone O₃ (b.p. −112 °C), which is
a far stronger oxidising agent. They differ in the number of atoms per molecule,
so this is *molecular* allotropy; diamond and graphite show *crystalline*
allotropy.

### Types of oxides

An **oxide** is a binary compound of oxygen with another element. Oxides are
classified by how they behave towards acids and alkalis.

| Type | Behaviour | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Acidic** | dissolve in water to give acids; react with alkalis | CO₂, SO₂, SO₃, N₂O₅, P₄O₁₀; SO₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₃ + H₂O |
| **Basic** | metal oxides; react with acids to give salt and water | Na₂O, CaO, MgO, FeO; CaO + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O |
| **Amphoteric** | react with **both** acids and alkalis | ZnO, Al₂O₃, PbO, SnO; ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O and ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O |
| **Neutral** | neither acidic nor basic; no salt with acid or base | CO, NO, N₂O, H₂O |
| **Peroxide** | contain the O–O linkage (O₂²⁻); give H₂O₂ with dilute acid | Na₂O₂, BaO₂, H₂O₂; BaO₂ + H₂SO₄ → BaSO₄↓ + H₂O₂ |
| **Mixed** | behave as a mixture of two simpler oxides | Fe₃O₄ (FeO·Fe₂O₃), Pb₃O₄ (2PbO·PbO₂), Mn₃O₄ |

::: memory Sorting an oxide in three seconds
Metal on the left → **basic**; non-metal on the right → **acidic**; the diagonal
strip Be, Al, Zn, Sn, Pb → **amphoteric**; an O–O bond → **peroxide**; an odd
ratio like M₃O₄ → **mixed**.
:::

### Applications of hydrogen peroxide

Hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂, is a pale blue syrupy liquid sold as a 3 % or 6 %
solution or labelled by **volume strength**. Its only reduction product is water,
which makes it an environmentally clean oxidant.

| Application | Basis |
|---|---|
| Antiseptic for wounds (3 %) | liberates nascent oxygen, kills anaerobic bacteria |
| Bleaching silk, wool, hair, ivory | mild oxidant, does not damage delicate fibre |
| Restoring old oil paintings | black PbS + 4H₂O₂ → white PbSO₄ + 4H₂O |
| Rocket propellant; effluent treatment; lab oxidant | decomposes to hot O₂; oxidises cyanides; 2KI + H₂O₂ → 2KOH + I₂ |

::: example Volume strength of hydrogen peroxide
**Problem.** A sample of hydrogen peroxide is labelled "10 volume". Express its
strength in grams per litre and in mol L⁻¹.
(H₂O₂ = 34, molar volume at STP = 22.4 L mol⁻¹.)

**Solution.**
"10 volume" means 1 volume of the solution gives 10 volumes of oxygen at STP on
complete decomposition, so 1 L of solution gives 10 L of O₂ at STP.

$$ n(\mathrm{O_2}) = \frac{10}{22.4} = 0.4464\ \mathrm{mol} $$

From 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂, one mole of O₂ comes from two moles of H₂O₂:

$$ n(\mathrm{H_2O_2}) = 2 \times 0.4464 = 0.8929\ \mathrm{mol\ per\ litre} $$

Mass per litre = 0.8929 × 34 = **30.4 g L⁻¹**, and the concentration is
**0.893 mol L⁻¹**.

*Useful shortcut:* strength in g L⁻¹ = volume strength × 3.04.
:::

### Medical and industrial applications of oxygen

| Medical | Industrial |
|---|---|

| Oxygen cylinders for high-altitude climbing — above 8000 m on Everest, supplementary oxygen is standard | Oxy-hydrogen flame (≈2800 °C) for melting quartz and platinum |

## 9.3 Ozone

### Occurrence

Ozone occurs only in traces near the ground but forms a layer in the
**stratosphere between about 15 km and 35 km**, densest near 25 km, where
ultraviolet light splits O₂ into atoms that attach to further O₂ molecules.
Brought to sea-level pressure all of it would form a layer only 3 mm thick — and
that 3 mm absorbs the ultraviolet that would otherwise sterilise the land.

### Preparation from oxygen

In the laboratory ozone is made by passing cold, dry oxygen through a **silent
electric discharge** in a Siemens' ozoniser. A spark is deliberately avoided: the
heat of a spark would decompose the ozone as fast as it formed.

3O₂ ⇌ 2O₃,  ΔH = +284 kJ

Three features of the equation control the yield: the reaction is
**endothermic**, so a low temperature favours ozone; the number of molecules
**falls** (3 → 2), so higher pressure favours ozone; and the discharge must be
silent so the product is not destroyed as it forms. Even so the product is only
about 10 % ozone by volume — **ozonised oxygen** — from which pure ozone is
separated by liquefaction (b.p. −112 °C against −183 °C for O₂).

### Structure of ozone

The molecule is **angular** with a bond angle of 116.8°; the central oxygen is
sp² hybridised and carries a positive formal charge. Experimentally **both O–O
bonds are identical at 128 pm**, between a single bond (148 pm) and a double bond
(121 pm). No single Lewis structure explains this, so ozone is a **resonance
hybrid** of the two structures shown above.

### Test for ozone

| Test | Observation | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Moist starch–iodide paper | turns blue-black | 2KI + H₂O + O₃ → 2KOH + I₂ + O₂ |
| Shaken with mercury | mercury loses its meniscus and sticks to glass ("tailing") | a surface film of Hg₂O forms |

::: caution The starch–iodide test is not proof on its own
Chlorine and nitrogen dioxide also liberate iodine from KI and turn the paper
blue. To distinguish, add the gas to starch–iodide paper *moistened with dilute
alkali*: only ozone still gives the colour, because Cl₂ is absorbed by the alkali.
NEB answers should mention this qualification.
:::

### Ozone layer depletion

Chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs, e.g. CF₂Cl₂ used as refrigerants and aerosol
propellants) are inert in the troposphere and drift into the stratosphere. There
ultraviolet light breaks a C–Cl bond and releases chlorine free radicals:

CF₂Cl₂ --UV--> •CF₂Cl + •Cl

The chlorine radical then destroys ozone in a **chain reaction** in which it is
regenerated, so one Cl radical can destroy of the order of 10⁵ ozone molecules:

•Cl + O₃ → •ClO + O₂
•ClO + O → •Cl + O₂

Nitric oxide from high-flying aircraft acts in the same way. The thinning is
worst over Antarctica each spring — the "ozone hole" — and lets through UV-B that
causes skin cancer, cataracts, weakened immunity and reduced crop yields. The
**Montreal Protocol (1987)**, which Nepal has ratified, phased out CFCs, and the
layer is slowly recovering.

### Uses of ozone

| Use | Why ozone is chosen |
|---|---|
| Sterilising drinking water | powerful germicide; unlike chlorine it leaves no taste or smell |
| Bleaching flour, ivory, wax, delicate fabrics | strong but leaves only O₂ behind |
| Purifying air in cinemas, tunnels and cold stores | oxidises odours and kills microbes |
| Ozonolysis; manufacture of KMnO₄, artificial silk, camphor | locates C=C double bonds; general oxidising agent |

::: example Percentage of ozone in ozonised oxygen
**Problem.** 100 cm³ of ozonised oxygen, when heated so that all the ozone
decomposes, expands to 110 cm³ at the same temperature and pressure. Find the
percentage by volume of ozone in the original sample.

**Solution.**
Let the volume of ozone be *x* cm³; the rest, (100 − *x*) cm³, is oxygen.

2O₃ → 3O₂

By Avogadro's law, volumes react in the ratio of the coefficients, so *x* cm³ of
ozone gives $\frac{3x}{2}$ cm³ of oxygen.

Final volume = (100 − x) + $\frac{3x}{2}$ = 100 + $\frac{x}{2}$

Increase in volume = $\frac{x}{2}$ = 110 − 100 = 10 cm³

$$ x = 20\ \mathrm{cm^3} $$

**Answer: the sample contains 20 cm³ of ozone, i.e. 20 % ozone by volume.**

*Check:* 20 cm³ O₃ → 30 cm³ O₂; total = 80 + 30 = 110 cm³.
:::

## 9.4 Nitrogen

### Inertness and active nitrogen

Nitrogen is 78 % of the air by volume, yet almost nothing reacts with it. The
reason is the **triple bond N≡N**, dissociation enthalpy 941 kJ mol⁻¹ — the
strongest bond between two like atoms. Nitrogen therefore reacts only under
forcing conditions: with Mg at red heat, with H₂ at 450 °C over a catalyst, with
O₂ in a lightning flash.

**Active nitrogen** is made by passing nitrogen at about 1 mm Hg through a
high-voltage discharge; a **golden-yellow afterglow** persists for minutes after
the discharge stops. It contains free N atoms and combines directly with S, P,
Hg, Na and I₂ at room temperature to give nitrides such as Hg₃N₂ and Na₃N, which
ordinary nitrogen never forms.

### Ammonia

**Laboratory preparation.** Ammonia is prepared by heating any ammonium salt with
a strong, non-volatile base — usually slaked lime:

The tube slopes down at the mouth so that condensed water cannot run back and
crack the hot glass. Ammonia is dried over **quicklime, CaO** and collected by
**downward displacement of air**, being lighter than air and very soluble.

::: caution Never dry ammonia with conc. H₂SO₄, P₄O₁₀ or anhydrous CaCl₂
All three are the "obvious" drying agents and all three are wrong. The first two
are acidic and neutralise ammonia (2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄); calcium chloride
forms the addition compound CaCl₂·8NH₃. Only quicklime, a basic drying agent,
leaves ammonia untouched — a favourite "give reasons" question.
:::

**Manufacture: the Haber process.** N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃, ΔH = −92 kJ, run at about
200 atm and 450–500 °C over finely divided iron promoted with molybdenum or
K₂O/Al₂O₃.

**Chemical properties of ammonia.**

| Reaction | Equation | Observation / use |
|---|---|---|
| Basic character | NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ | turns red litmus blue; weak base, Kb = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ |
| With hydrogen chloride | NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl | **dense white fumes — the standard test** |
| Combustion in oxygen | 4NH₃ + 3O₂ → 2N₂ + 6H₂O | burns with a pale yellow flame; will not burn in air |
| Catalytic oxidation (Ostwald) | 4NH₃ + 5O₂ --Pt, 1073 K--> 4NO + 6H₂O | first step in nitric acid manufacture |
| With copper(II) sulphate solution | CuSO₄ + 4NH₃ → [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄ | **deep blue** solution |
| With Nessler's reagent | 2K₂[HgI₄] + NH₃ + 3KOH → HgO·Hg(NH₂)I↓ + 7KI + 2H₂O | **brown precipitate** — test for NH₄⁺ |
| Reducing action | 3CuO + 2NH₃ → 3Cu + N₂ + 3H₂O | black CuO turns reddish-brown |

**Applications.** Nitrogenous fertilisers — urea, ammonium sulphate and DAP, used
across the Terai; nitric acid by the Ostwald process; refrigerant in ice plants
(high latent heat of vaporisation); the Solvay process; cleaning agents;
explosives and rayon.

**Harmful effects.** The gas is intensely pungent and corrosive, inflaming eyes,
nose and lungs, and is fatal above about 2500 ppm; liquid-ammonia leaks in cold
stores are a recognised hazard. Excess ammonium fertiliser washed into rivers
causes **eutrophication** — algal blooms, oxygen depletion and fish kills — and
ammonia is directly toxic to fish below 1 mg L⁻¹.

### Oxy-acids of nitrogen

| Name | Formula | Oxidation state of N | Anhydride | Salt |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hyponitrous acid | H₂N₂O₂ | +1 | N₂O | Na₂N₂O₂ |
| Nitrous acid | HNO₂ | +3 | N₂O₃ | NaNO₂ |
| Nitric acid | HNO₃ | +5 | N₂O₅ | NaNO₃ |

Only nitric acid can be isolated pure; nitrous acid exists only in cold dilute
solution and disproportionates on warming, 3HNO₂ → HNO₃ + 2NO + H₂O.

### Chemical properties of nitric acid

Nitric acid is a colourless fuming liquid (b.p. 83 °C) that turns yellow on
standing through dissolved NO₂ from its own decomposition,
4HNO₃ → 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O.

| Behaviour | Equation and note |
|---|---|
| Strong monobasic acid | NaOH + HNO₃ → NaNO₃ + H₂O |
| With copper, **dilute** | 3Cu + 8HNO₃ → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO↑ + 4H₂O (colourless NO, browns in air) |
| With copper, **concentrated** | Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂↑ + 2H₂O (brown NO₂) |
| With magnesium, very dilute (1 %) | Mg + 2HNO₃ → Mg(NO₃)₂ + H₂↑ — the **only** case giving hydrogen |
| Passivity | Fe, Al and Cr are coated with an impervious oxide film by conc. HNO₃ |
| With non-metals | C + 4HNO₃ → CO₂↑ + 4NO₂↑ + 2H₂O;  S + 6HNO₃ → H₂SO₄ + 6NO₂↑ + 2H₂O;  P₄ + 20HNO₃ → 4H₃PO₄ + 20NO₂↑ + 4H₂O |
| Aqua regia (3 conc. HCl : 1 conc. HNO₃) | HNO₃ + 3HCl → NOCl + 2H₂O + 2[Cl]; the nascent chlorine dissolves Au and Pt |

::: caution Nitric acid is an oxidising acid, so metals give oxides of nitrogen — not hydrogen
Writing "Cu + 2HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + H₂" loses the whole mark. The nitrate ion is a
better oxidising agent than H⁺, so any hydrogen formed is at once oxidised to
water while NO₃⁻ is reduced to NO₂ (conc.) or NO (dil.). The only exception is
very dilute HNO₃ with Mg or Mn.
:::

### Ring test for the nitrate ion

Add freshly prepared **iron(II) sulphate** solution to the unknown, then pour
**concentrated sulphuric acid slowly down the side of the inclined tube** so that
it forms a separate lower layer. A **dark brown ring** at the junction of the two
liquids confirms a nitrate.

6FeSO₄ + 3H₂SO₄ + 2HNO₃ → 3Fe₂(SO₄)₃ + 4H₂O + 2NO
FeSO₄ + NO → [Fe(NO)]SO₄  (brown ring, a nitrosyl complex)

The ring disappears on shaking or warming, as the loosely bound NO escapes.

## 9.5 Halogens

### General characteristics

The halogens are group 17: fluorine, chlorine, bromine, iodine and astatine. Each
has the valence configuration ns²np⁵, one electron short of the noble-gas octet,
so all are strong oxidising agents and all are diatomic.

Melting and boiling points **increase** down the group as the molecules become
larger and more polarisable, strengthening the van der Waals forces.
Electronegativity and oxidising power **decrease** down the group as the atom
grows and the incoming electron sits further from the nucleus.

::: key The two fluorine anomalies
(i) The F–F bond enthalpy (158 kJ mol⁻¹) is **lower** than Cl–Cl (242 kJ mol⁻¹):
the F atom is so small that the lone pairs repel across the very short bond and
weaken it. (ii) Fluorine also has a *lower* electron affinity than chlorine, yet
is still the strongest oxidising agent, because of that weak bond and the very
high hydration energy of F⁻.
:::

### Comparative preparation of Cl₂, Br₂ and I₂

All three are made by oxidising the halide ion, and the oxidising agent needed
gets milder down the group as the halide becomes easier to oxidise.

### Comparative chemical properties

| Reaction | Chlorine | Bromine | Iodine |
|---|---|---|---|
| With hydrogen | explosive in sunlight: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl | needs 200 °C and a catalyst | reversible and incomplete: H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI |
| With water | Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HOCl (bleaching) | slow, similar | almost insoluble; no reaction |
| With **cold dilute** NaOH | Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O | Br₂ + 2NaOH → NaBr + NaOBr + H₂O | I₂ + 2NaOH → NaI + NaOI + H₂O |
| With **hot concentrated** NaOH | 3Cl₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O | 3Br₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaBr + NaBrO₃ + 3H₂O | 3I₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaI + NaIO₃ + 3H₂O |
| Displacement | displaces Br₂ and I₂ (and gives bleaching powder with slaked lime, 2Cl₂ + 2Ca(OH)₂ → Ca(OCl)₂ + CaCl₂ + 2H₂O) | displaces I₂ only | displaces none |
| Bleaching action | strong, **permanent** (oxidation by HOCl) | weaker | negligible |

All three reactions with alkali are **disproportionations**: the halogen is
simultaneously reduced (to −1) and oxidised (to +1 or +5).

### Tests and uses

| Substance | Test | Observation |
|---|---|---|
| Cl₂ gas | moist blue litmus | turns red, then bleached white |
| Br₂ vapour | colour and smell | reddish-brown, choking |
| I₂ | starch solution | deep blue-black |
| Cl⁻ / Br⁻ / I⁻ | AgNO₃ solution, then NH₄OH | curdy **white** AgCl, **soluble** in NH₄OH; pale **yellow** AgBr, **sparingly soluble**; deep **yellow** AgI, **insoluble** |

**Uses.** *Chlorine*: disinfecting drinking water, bleaching paper and textiles,
manufacture of HCl, PVC and bleaching powder. *Bromine*: AgBr for photographic
film, dyes, 1,2-dibromoethane. *Iodine*: tincture of iodine as an antiseptic,
iodised salt to prevent goitre — the programme that has almost eliminated iodine
deficiency in the Nepali hills — AgI emulsions and iodoform.

### Haloacids: HCl, HBr, HI

| Property | HCl | HBr | HI |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bond enthalpy (kJ mol⁻¹) | 431 | 366 | 299 |
| Boiling point (°C) | −85 | −67 | −35 |
| Thermal stability | high | moderate | low, decomposes to I₂ |
| Acid strength / reducing power | least | intermediate | greatest |

Down the group the H–X bond lengthens and weakens, so the acid ionises more
readily, is more easily oxidised and is thermally less stable. HCl is made by
warming a chloride with concentrated sulphuric acid:
NaCl + H₂SO₄ --Δ--> NaHSO₄ + HCl↑.

::: caution Conc. H₂SO₄ cannot be used to prepare HBr or HI
Concentrated sulphuric acid is an oxidising agent, and HBr and HI are good
reducing agents, so the product is oxidised the moment it forms:
2HBr + H₂SO₄ → Br₂ + SO₂ + 2H₂O and 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O.
Use **non-volatile, non-oxidising phosphoric acid** instead:
NaBr + H₃PO₄ → NaH₂PO₄ + HBr↑.
:::

::: example Mass of manganese dioxide needed for a given volume of chlorine
**Problem.** Calculate the mass of manganese dioxide and the volume of 11.6 M
concentrated hydrochloric acid required to prepare 5.6 litres of chlorine at STP.
(Mn = 55, O = 16.)

**Solution.**
MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + Cl₂↑ + 2H₂O

$$ n(\mathrm{Cl_2}) = \frac{5.6}{22.4} = 0.25\ \mathrm{mol} $$

From the equation, 1 mol Cl₂ needs 1 mol MnO₂ and 4 mol HCl.

Molar mass of MnO₂ = 55 + 2(16) = 87 g mol⁻¹

$$ m(\mathrm{MnO_2}) = 0.25 \times 87 = 21.75\ \mathrm{g} $$

$$ n(\mathrm{HCl}) = 4 \times 0.25 = 1.0\ \mathrm{mol},\qquad V = \frac{1.0}{11.6} = 0.0862\ \mathrm{L} $$

**Answer: 21.75 g of MnO₂ and about 86 cm³ of the concentrated acid.**
:::

Graphite layers are held together only by weak van der Waals forces, so they
slide over one another — hence the lubricating action and the mark a pencil
leaves. Within a layer each carbon uses three of its four valence electrons for σ
bonds; the fourth is delocalised and carries current. **Fullerenes** were
discovered in 1985; C₆₀ is football-shaped and is the only allotrope of carbon
that dissolves in ordinary solvents such as benzene, giving a magenta solution.
Coal, coke, charcoal and lamp black are called *amorphous* carbon, but X-ray
study shows them to be microcrystalline graphite.

## 9.8 Sulphur

### Allotropes of sulphur and uses

| Allotrope | Crystal form | Stable range | Density (g cm⁻³) | Made by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Rhombic (α)** | octahedral, yellow | below 95.6 °C | 2.06 | slow evaporation of a solution of S in CS₂ |
| **Monoclinic (β)** | long needles, pale yellow | 95.6 °C to 119 °C | 1.98 | slow cooling of molten sulphur, pouring off the liquid |
| **Plastic (γ)** | amorphous, rubbery | unstable | 1.95 | pouring boiling sulphur into cold water |

95.6 °C is the **transition temperature**: below it monoclinic sulphur changes to
rhombic, above it the change reverses. Both crystalline forms are built from the
same puckered **S₈ crown ring** and differ only in packing, which is why their
chemistry is identical.

**Uses.** Manufacture of sulphuric acid (by far the largest use); vulcanisation
of rubber; gunpowder and matches; fungicides and insecticides; sulphur ointments
for skin disease; bleaching.

### Hydrogen sulphide and Kipp's apparatus

H₂S is prepared by the action of dilute sulphuric acid on iron(II) sulphide:

FeS + H₂SO₄(dil) → FeSO₄ + H₂S↑

generated in **Kipp's apparatus**, a three-bulb vessel in which the acid is
pushed away from the solid the moment the stopcock is closed.

### Sulphuric acid — the Contact process

1. **SO₂** is produced by burning sulphur or roasting iron pyrites.
2. **Purification** in a dust chamber, washing tower, drying tower and arsenic
   purifier — arsenic compounds would poison the catalyst.
3. **Catalytic oxidation**: 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, ΔH = −196 kJ, over V₂O₅ at 450 °C
   and about 2 atm. The reaction is exothermic and 3 molecules become 2, so a low
   temperature and a raised pressure both favour SO₃; 450 °C is the compromise
   with rate.
4. **Absorption** in 98 % H₂SO₄ to give **oleum**, SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇; direct
   absorption in water gives an unmanageable acid mist.
5. **Dilution**: H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄.

| Property of H₂SO₄ | Illustration |
|---|---|
| Strong dibasic acid | H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O |
| High boiling point (338 °C), non-volatile | displaces volatile acids: NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl↑ |
| Powerful **dehydrating** agent | C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ --H₂SO₄--> 12C + 11H₂O (sugar chars to a black column); CuSO₄·5H₂O → CuSO₄ |
| **Oxidising** agent when hot and concentrated | Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂↑ + 2H₂O; C + 2H₂SO₄ → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O |
| Uses | fertilisers (superphosphate, ammonium sulphate), lead–acid batteries, petroleum refining, dyes, detergents, pickling of steel — its consumption is taken as a measure of a country's industrial development, hence "**king of chemicals**" |

### Sodium thiosulphate, Na₂S₂O₃·5H₂O

Commonly called **hypo**, it is made by boiling sodium sulphite solution with
flowers of sulphur: Na₂SO₃ + S --boil--> Na₂S₂O₃

| Reaction | Equation | Use |
|---|---|---|
| With dilute acid | Na₂S₂O₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + S↓ + SO₂↑ + H₂O | milky turbidity; a kinetics experiment |
| With iodine | 2Na₂S₂O₃ + I₂ → 2NaI + Na₂S₄O₆ | **iodometric titration**; the blue starch colour vanishes at the end point |
| With silver bromide | AgBr + 2Na₂S₂O₃ → Na₃[Ag(S₂O₃)₂] + NaBr | **fixing** a photographic film — unexposed AgBr is dissolved away |
| With chlorine | Na₂S₂O₃ + 4Cl₂ + 5H₂O → 2NaHSO₄ + 8HCl | **antichlor** — removes excess chlorine after bleaching |

It is also a cyanide antidote in medicine and is used to extract silver from its
ores.

## Chapter summary

- **Hydrogen**: nascent hydrogen is more reactive than H₂ because the 436 kJ mol⁻¹
  H–H bond need not be broken. Isotopes protium, deuterium (D₂O, reactor
  moderator) and tritium. As a fuel: 143 kJ g⁻¹, only water, but hard to store.
- **Oxides**: acidic (CO₂, SO₂), basic (Na₂O, CaO), amphoteric (ZnO, Al₂O₃),
  neutral (CO, NO), peroxide (Na₂O₂, BaO₂), mixed (Fe₃O₄, Pb₃O₄). H₂O₂ strength
  in g L⁻¹ = volume strength × 3.04.
- **Ozone**: 3O₂ ⇌ 2O₃, ΔH = +284 kJ, made by silent electric discharge; angular,
  116.8°, both O–O bonds 128 pm, a resonance hybrid; turns starch–iodide paper
  blue; destroyed catalytically by Cl radicals from CFCs.
- **Nitrogen** is inert because of the 941 kJ mol⁻¹ N≡N bond; active nitrogen is
  atomic and very reactive. Ammonia: 2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2NH₃↑ + 2H₂O,
  dried over **quicklime only**, tested with HCl (white fumes) and Nessler's
  reagent (brown ppt).
- **Nitric acid** gives NO₂ (conc.) or NO (dil.) with metals, never H₂. Nitrate is
  confirmed by the **brown ring** [Fe(NO)]SO₄. Oxy-acids: H₂N₂O₂, HNO₂, HNO₃.
- **Halogens**: oxidising power and electronegativity fall down the group; the
  F–F bond is anomalously weak (158 kJ mol⁻¹). MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O.
  Cold dilute alkali gives X⁻ + XO⁻, hot concentrated alkali X⁻ + XO₃⁻. Acid
  strength and reducing power HCl < HBr < HI, so conc. H₂SO₄ cannot make HBr/HI.
- **Carbon**: diamond sp³ network, graphite sp² layers 335 pm apart, C₆₀ cage of
  20 hexagons and 12 pentagons. CO is neutral, a strong reducing agent
  (Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂) and a poison through carboxyhaemoglobin.
- **Phosphorus**: white, red, scarlet, violet and black allotropes. PH₃ from
  P₄ + 3NaOH + 3H₂O → PH₃ + 3NaH₂PO₂; weaker base than NH₃; Holme's signals.
- **Sulphur**: rhombic ⇌ monoclinic at 95.6 °C, both S₈ rings. H₂S from Kipp's
  apparatus blackens lead-acetate paper; SO₂ is a temporary bleach and turns
  acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ green; Contact process 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ over V₂O₅ at 450 °C,
  then oleum; hypo is the photographic fixer and the iodometric titrant.

1. Distinguish between atomic hydrogen and nascent hydrogen. Why is nascent

   acid does not liberate hydrogen with most metals. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. Classify the following oxides and justify each answer with one equation:
   CO, ZnO, N₂O₅, Na₂O₂, Pb₃O₄. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
5. Describe the working of Kipp's apparatus for the preparation of hydrogen
   sulphide, with the equation, and give the test for the gas. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Answers to Group B
**1.** *Atomic hydrogen* is free H atoms from passing H₂ through an electric arc
at about 4000 °C; it lasts a fraction of a second and recombines with great heat
(atomic hydrogen torch). *Nascent hydrogen* is hydrogen at the moment of
generation inside a reacting mixture, e.g. Zn + dil. H₂SO₄. It reduces acidified
KMnO₄ and FeCl₃, which H₂ gas will not, because it need not first supply the
436 kJ mol⁻¹ H–H bond enthalpy and retains the energy of the generating reaction.

**2.** Cold dry oxygen is passed through a Siemens' ozoniser — a silent electric
discharge between tin-foil electrodes on concentric glass tubes:
3O₂ ⇌ 2O₃, ΔH = +284 kJ. A spark would heat the gas and destroy the ozone as fast
as it formed, so the discharge must be silent; and since the reaction is
endothermic, a **low temperature** shifts the equilibrium right (Le Chatelier).
The product is about 10 % ozonised oxygen.

**3.** (i) Conc. H₂SO₄ is acidic and ammonia basic:
2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄; quicklime is used instead. (ii) Conc. H₂SO₄ oxidises
the HI as it forms: 8HI + H₂SO₄ → 4I₂ + H₂S + 4H₂O; non-oxidising phosphoric acid
is used instead. (iii) NO₃⁻ is a better oxidising agent than H⁺, so hydrogen is
oxidised to water while NO₃⁻ is reduced to NO₂ (conc.) or NO (dil.).

**4.** CO — **neutral**. ZnO — **amphoteric**: ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O and
ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O. N₂O₅ — **acidic**: N₂O₅ + H₂O → 2HNO₃. Na₂O₂ —
**peroxide**: Na₂O₂ + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + H₂O₂. Pb₃O₄ — **mixed**, 2PbO·PbO₂.

**5.** Lumps of FeS rest on the constriction in the middle bulb; dilute H₂SO₄
poured into the top funnel collects in the bottom bulb. Opening the stopcock lets
acid rise into the middle bulb: FeS + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂S↑. Closing it lets the
gas pressure force the acid back down out of contact with the solid, so the
reaction stops automatically. **Test:** the gas blackens lead-acetate paper,
Pb(CH₃COO)₂ + H₂S → PbS↓ + 2CH₃COOH.

::: note Answers to Group C
**1. (a)** MnO₂ + 4HCl(conc) --Δ--> MnCl₂ + Cl₂↑ + 2H₂O;
MnO₂ + 2NaBr + 2H₂SO₄ → MnSO₄ + Na₂SO₄ + Br₂ + 2H₂O;
MnO₂ + 2KI + 2H₂SO₄ → MnSO₄ + K₂SO₄ + I₂ + 2H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄ alone oxidises I⁻).
Down the group the halide ion is larger and holds its outer electron less firmly,
so it is oxidised more easily and a milder oxidant suffices.
**(b)** Cold dilute: Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O. Hot concentrated:
3Cl₂ + 6NaOH → 5NaCl + NaClO₃ + 3H₂O. Both are **disproportionations** — chlorine
goes from 0 to −1 and simultaneously to +1 (or +5).
**(c)** Add AgNO₃ solution: NaCl gives curdy white AgCl, readily soluble in
NH₄OH; NaBr pale yellow AgBr, sparingly soluble; NaI deep yellow AgI, insoluble.

**2. (a)** Burn sulphur or roast pyrites, 4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂; purify
the gas of dust, moisture and arsenic (a catalyst poison); then in the contact
tower 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃, ΔH = −196 kJ, over **V₂O₅** at **450 °C** and about
**2 atm**. Exothermic, so a low temperature favours SO₃ but the rate would be too
slow — 450 °C is the compromise; 3 molecules become 2, so pressure helps, but
only a modest pressure is needed since the yield is already about 97 %. Finally
SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇ and H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄.
**(b)** Water absorption is strongly exothermic and gives a dense acid mist that
is neither condensed nor absorbed, so most of the product would be lost.
**(c)** 1 mol S gives 1 mol H₂SO₄, i.e. 32 g S gives 98 g acid.
Theoretical mass = 1.00 × 10⁶ × 98/32 = 3.0625 × 10⁶ g = 3062.5 kg;
actual = 0.90 × 3062.5 = **2756 kg ≈ 2.76 tonne**.

**3. (a)** Hyponitrous acid H₂N₂O₂ (N = +1); nitrous acid HNO₂ (+3); nitric acid
HNO₃ (+5).
**(b)** 3Cu + 8HNO₃(dil) → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO↑ + 4H₂O;
Cu + 4HNO₃(conc) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂↑ + 2H₂O;
C + 4HNO₃ → CO₂↑ + 4NO₂↑ + 2H₂O;
S + 6HNO₃ → H₂SO₄ + 6NO₂↑ + 2H₂O.
**(c)** Add fresh FeSO₄ solution, then pour conc. H₂SO₄ gently down the side of
the inclined tube so that it forms a separate lower layer; a dark brown ring
appears at the junction:
6FeSO₄ + 3H₂SO₄ + 2HNO₃ → 3Fe₂(SO₄)₃ + 4H₂O + 2NO, then
FeSO₄ + NO → [Fe(NO)]SO₄.
