Food is more than fuel: it is memory, culture and sometimes courage. This unit takes you on a tour of the world's strangest dishes, then to the story of a Nepali boy who became a celebrated chef in Britain. In grammar you will study how to give **reasons and purposes** and how to use the **present continuous**; in writing you will write a **recipe** and an **opinion paragraph**.

::: key What the SEE paper asks from this unit
Reading: matching words, idioms and meanings; completing sentences from the text; true/false/not given; short answers. Grammar: connectives of reason and purpose (*because, as, since, so that, in order to*), present continuous with time expressions. Writing: a recipe or process, and an argument paragraph (*To what extent do you agree?*).
:::

## 6.1 Strange Food from Around the World

### About the text

"Strange Food from Around the World" is an informal, personal essay about eating unusual dishes while travelling. The writer begins with a general idea — food can be an adventure and even a challenge — and then gives examples country by country, mixing description with personal reactions ("delicious", "disappointing", "too much"). It uses a friendly, conversational voice, short opinions and an idiom, *a bridge too far*.

### What the text says

Travel gives us the chance to try foods we would never think of eating at home, and for some people eating something unfamiliar is a kind of challenge: you take a risk, and if you enjoy the food you feel you have *won*. The writer then describes a number of dishes.

| Dish | Country / region | What it is | The writer's view |
|---|---|---|---|
| Haggis | Scotland | The parts of a sheep that people usually do not want — heart, liver and lungs — ground up, mixed with mutton fat, oatmeal and spices, packed in a sheep's stomach and boiled for a few hours | A traditional dish; surprising ingredients, but tasty |
| Shiokara | Japan | Raw squid mixed with salted, fermented squid guts | Strong, salty; you may begin to like it once you are used to it, especially when eating with friends |
| Bush food (witchetty grubs, bush plants) | Australia | Foods from the "bush": large wood-eating larvae, native plants | Nutty-tasting; can be enjoyed in Australia |
| Fermented horse milk | Central Asia (Mongolia, Kazakhstan) | Milk from mares that has fermented into a sour, slightly alcoholic drink | The writer was disappointed; it was really bad |
| Jellyfish | China | A sea animal that scares many people, turned into a crunchy dish | Eaten in China as a normal dish |
| Snails (escargots) | France | Served as a main dish, fried or baked with plenty of garlic and butter | Very tasty |
| Bún riêu | Vietnam | A noodle soup made with crab | A well-liked noodle dish |
| Large insects | Many countries | Fried or roasted insects | The writer finds the larger ones hard because they cannot be eaten in one bite |

*(Some details of where the writer drank the horse milk are not clear from the textbook extract; "Central Asia" is general background.)*

### Vocabulary

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| ferment | to go through a chemical change (often into alcohol or acid) | Yoghurt and gundruk are fermented foods. |
| squid | a sea creature with a long soft body, eight arms and two tentacles | Fried squid is popular on the coast. |
| witchetty | a large whitish larva that eats wood (an Australian bush food) | Aboriginal people eat witchetty grubs. |
| myrtle | a bush with shiny leaves, pink or white flowers, blue-black berries | Myrtle berries have a spicy taste. |
| congeal | to change from a fluid to a solid state | The fat congealed in the cold. |
| nutty | tasting like nuts | The bread has a nutty flavour. |

**Idioms** (the text uses *a bridge too far*: something that goes beyond what is acceptable):

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| get out of hand | to become difficult to control |
| miss the boat | to be too late to get what you want |
| break the ice | to say or do something to make people feel comfortable |
| cry over spilt milk | to worry over past events that cannot be changed |
| look before you leap | to think about the possible bad results before acting |

### Themes

- **Food as challenge and adventure.** Trying a strange dish is a small act of courage.
- **Culture decides taste.** What disgusts one person is a delicacy to another — jellyfish in China, snails in France.
- **Opinion and honesty.** The writer is honest even when the food is "really bad".

## 6.2 You Inspire Others by Learning, Not by Teaching

### About the text

This text is an **interview** with **Santosh Shah**, a chef born in Nepal who works in the United Kingdom and became widely known when he took part in **MasterChef: The Professionals** (2020) on BBC One. The title is his philosophy: a person inspires others not by lecturing but by never stopping learning. An interview is a question-and-answer text; its voice is spoken, direct and personal.

### What the text says

- **A humble start.** Shah comes from a poor family. He did not have a normal childhood; he started working while still a boy, at about nine or ten, to support his family.
- **From the kitchen floor to the top.** He began in the lowest jobs in restaurant kitchens, learned by watching and practising, and rose step by step. He worked at the London restaurant **Cinnamon Kitchen**, and is very proud of what he learned there. He did not receive special favours; he took every task and asked for more.
- **MasterChef.** Appearing on the TV show raised his popularity.
- **Experimenting with Nepali food.** He wants to show the world that Nepali cuisine is unique and rich and not just "a copy of Indian food". He uses classic ideas with Nepali flavours — for example, he experimented with *sukuti* (dried meat), dehydrating it for different times to make it soft without losing its taste, and he cooked octopus in Nepali style.
- **Philosophy.** Two things make a person successful: asking "what is next?" and refusing to settle in a comfortable position; and continuous learning. He believes in learning from everyone, even junior staff.
- **Dream.** He hopes his restaurant **Ayla** will win a **Michelin star**, which, he believes, would raise the profile of Nepali food worldwide. He is hopeful about the Nepali food market because food trends are always changing.

*(The interview was not held in Nepal. For other details such as the exact years of his career, rely only on what your textbook says.)*

### Vocabulary

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| tandoori | cooked in a cylindrical clay oven (tandoor) | Tandoori chicken is a popular dish. |
| executive (chef) | a person with power to manage an organisation | The executive chef plans the menu. |
| skewer | a long pin for holding meat while roasting | Chicken was grilled on skewers. |
| entirety | the whole of something | He read the book in its entirety. |
| nostalgia | a mixture of pleasure and sadness when remembering the past | The smell of dal bhat filled him with nostalgia. |
| elevate | to raise to a more important level | The chef wants to elevate Nepali food. |
| dehydrate | to remove water from food to preserve it | Sukuti is meat that has been dehydrated. |

### Themes

- **Humility and learning.** Teaching impresses people only if the teacher is still a student.
- **Perseverance.** Shah rose through struggle and hard work, not privilege.
- **Pride in identity.** He wants Nepali food to be recognised on its own terms.
- **Innovation and tradition together.** New techniques (dehydrating, plating) with old flavours.

## 6.3 Grammar and writing tasks of the unit

### Grammar I: Reasons and purposes

Connectives link ideas. **Reason** connectives answer *Why did it happen?*; **purpose** connectives answer *What for?*

```figure caption="Reason links look backwards at a cause; purpose links look forwards at a goal."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.patches import FancyBboxPatch
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4.6, 2.3))
def box(x, y, w, h, t, fc, ec, fs=8):
    ax.add_patch(FancyBboxPatch((x, y), w, h, boxstyle="round,pad=0.02,rounding_size=0.1", fc=fc, ec=ec, lw=0.9))
    ax.text(x + w/2, y + h/2, t, ha="center", va="center", fontsize=fs, color=INK)
box(0.1, 1.9, 2.3, 0.8, "CAUSE\n(why?)", "#eef3f9", ACCENT)
box(3.6, 1.9, 2.3, 0.8, "RESULT", "#eef3f9", ACCENT)
ax.annotate("", xy=(3.55, 2.3), xytext=(2.45, 2.3), arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", color=MUTED, lw=1.2))
ax.text(3.0, 2.55, "so", ha="center", fontsize=8, color=INK)
box(0.1, 0.3, 2.3, 0.8, "ACTION", "#f7f3ea", "#b8860b")
box(3.6, 0.3, 2.3, 0.8, "AIM\n(what for?)", "#f7f3ea", "#b8860b")
ax.annotate("", xy=(3.55, 0.7), xytext=(2.45, 0.7), arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle="-|>", color=MUTED, lw=1.2))
ax.text(3.0, 0.95, "to / so that", ha="center", fontsize=7.5, color=INK)
ax.text(0.1, 3.0, "because / as / since  \u2190 reason", fontsize=8, color=ACCENT, weight="bold")
ax.set_xlim(0, 6.0); ax.set_ylim(0, 3.4)
ax.axis("off")
```

| Meaning | Connectives | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| reason | **because** | because + clause | I bought a raincoat because the monsoon started. |
| reason | **as**, **since** | as/since + clause (usually at the start; known reason) | As I am stuck in traffic, I won't reach school on time. |
| reason | **because of**, **due to** | + noun / noun phrase | The flight was delayed because of the bad weather. |
| purpose | **to** | to + base verb | The city planted trees to reduce traffic noise. |
| purpose | **in order to**, **so as to** | + base verb (more formal) | We queued in order to buy tickets. |
| purpose | **so that** | so that + clause (often with *can/could/will/would*) | We put a table in the room so that Samip can study in private. |
| result | **so** | clause, so clause | It rained, so we stayed home. |

::: caution Common mistakes
Do not use *because* and *so* in the same sentence (*Because it rained, so we stayed* is wrong). Do not write *because of* + clause (*because of it rained*). Use *so that* before a full clause and *to* before a verb: *He works hard so that he can pass* / *to pass*.
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Join the sentences with the connective given: "He ran fast. He wanted to catch the bus." (to)

**Solution.** *He ran fast to catch the bus.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Fill in: "The flight was delayed ____ the bad weather." (because / because of)

**Solution.** A noun phrase follows → *because of*. *The flight was delayed because of the bad weather.*
:::

### Grammar II: The present continuous and time expressions

**Form:** *am/is/are + verb-ing*. **Uses:**

| Use | Time expressions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| an action happening **now** | now, at the moment, right now, currently, still | The boys are playing cricket now. |
| an action **around now** (temporary, not exactly this moment) | this week, this month, these days, nowadays | The local governments are announcing their policies this month. |
| a **planned future** action | tonight, tomorrow, later today, next week, on Wednesday | We're meeting Nabina later today. |
| a developing change | getting, becoming, growing | Prices are rising. |

Time words such as *yesterday, last week, usually, sometimes* do **not** go with the present continuous.

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Choose: "What are you doing (this / last) afternoon?"

**Solution.** The present continuous looks ahead to a plan. *this afternoon.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** NOW, AROUND or FUTURE? "I'm sorry, I don't have time. I'm attending a meeting."

**Solution.** Happening at this moment → **NOW**.
:::

### Writing I: A recipe and a process

A **recipe** has a title, a list of **ingredients** with quantities, and **method** steps in order. Use imperatives and sequence words (*first, then, next, after that, finally*) and give times (*for seven minutes*).

::: example Model recipe — Gajar ka halwa (carrot pudding)
**Ingredients:** 1 kg grated carrots, 1 litre milk, 200 g sugar, 4 green cardamoms (powdered), 3 tablespoons ghee.

**Method:**
1. Switch on the stove and melt the ghee in a pan.
2. Add the grated carrots and fry for about seven minutes until slightly golden brown.
3. Pour in the milk and stir.
4. Cook until all the milk has dried up (about twenty minutes).
5. Add the sugar and cardamom powder and cook for four more minutes.
6. Turn off the stove. The halwa is ready to serve.
:::

::: example Model recipe — Vegetable momo
**Ingredients:** 2 cups flour, a little water and salt; for the filling: 2 cups finely chopped cabbage, 1 chopped onion, 1 carrot (grated), 1 teaspoon ginger-garlic paste, 1 teaspoon cumin powder, salt, 1 tablespoon oil.

**Method:**
1. Knead the flour with water and salt into a soft dough and rest it for twenty minutes.
2. Mix all the filling ingredients well.
3. Roll the dough into thin small circles.
4. Put one spoon of filling in the centre of each circle and fold into a half-moon or round shape, sealing the edges.
5. Grease a steamer and arrange the momos in it.
6. Steam for 10 to 15 minutes. Serve hot with tomato achar.
:::

### Writing II: An opinion paragraph

When asked *To what extent do you agree or disagree?*, state your position clearly, give two or three **reasons** with examples, mention the other side briefly, and end with a firm conclusion. Connectives: *firstly, moreover, however, therefore, in conclusion*.

**Task.** Many Nepalis now prefer fast food such as noodles, pizza and burgers to typical Nepali food. This harms their health. To what extent do you agree?

::: example Model answer
I largely agree that the growing taste for fast food is harming the health of Nepalis. Firstly, fast food contains a great deal of oil, salt, sugar and refined flour but very little fibre or protein, so people who eat it often gain weight and are more likely to suffer from diabetes and heart disease. Moreover, children who fill up on noodles and fizzy drinks may not eat the vegetables, pulses and grains that they need. Traditional meals such as dal, bhat, tarkari and achar are balanced and fresh.

However, fast food is not bad when eaten occasionally, and some Nepali foods, such as deep-fried snacks, are also unhealthy. Therefore, the problem lies in eating too much of it rather than in the food itself. In conclusion, we should enjoy fast food rarely and make home-cooked Nepali meals our main diet.
:::

## Chapter summary

- *Strange Food from Around the World* describes haggis (Scotland), shiokara (Japan), bush food (Australia), fermented horse milk, jellyfish (China), snails (France), bún riêu (Vietnam) and large insects; food is an adventure and taste is cultural.
- Idiom: *a bridge too far* — too extreme.
- *You Inspire Others by Learning, Not by Teaching* is an interview with Nepali chef Santosh Shah (MasterChef: The Professionals 2020, BBC One): humble start, hard work, pride in Nepali food, ambition for a Michelin star for Ayla.
- Reasons: *because, as, since* + clause; *because of, due to* + noun. Purposes: *to, in order to, so that*.
- Present continuous: now (*now, at the moment*), around now (*this week, these days*) and planned future (*tonight, tomorrow, next week*).
- A recipe lists ingredients and numbered steps; an opinion paragraph states a view, gives reasons and concludes.
