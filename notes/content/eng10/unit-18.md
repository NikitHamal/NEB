Films, television, music and social media fill a large part of our free time, and they shape what we think and feel. This last unit of the book looks at both sides of the media. The first text, "Hachi: A Dog's Tale", is a review of a film based on the true story of a dog who waited for his dead master for ten years. The second, "Interesting Social Media Stories", shows the good that social media can do. In grammar you will study **how to ask questions** and **causative verbs** (*have, get, make, let*). In writing you will compose a **film review** and an **essay on the impact of social media**.

::: key What the examiner wants from this unit
From the review: facts about the film (director, story, themes, strengths and weaknesses) and your own opinion. From the social-media text: the four or five stories and what each shows. In grammar: correct word order in questions and the right causative construction. In writing: a review with a title, rating, summary, evaluation and recommendation, and a balanced essay that gives both advantages and disadvantages.
:::

## 18.1 Hachi: A Dog's Tale

### About the text

"Hachi: A Dog's Tale" is a **film review**: a piece of writing in which a critic describes a film, judges it and tells readers whether it is worth watching. The film is the 2009 American drama ***Hachi: A Dog's Tale***, directed by **Lasse Hallström** and starring **Richard Gere** as a college professor. It is an American remake of a Japanese film, *Hachikō Monogatari* (1987), and both films are based on a **true story**.

### The true story behind the film

Hachikō (1923–1935) was an Akita dog in Tokyo, owned by Professor **Hidesaburō Ueno** of the University of Tokyo. Each day the dog accompanied his master to **Shibuya Station** in the morning and met him there when his train returned in the evening. In May 1925 the professor died suddenly at work and never came back. Hachikō kept returning to the station every evening for nearly **ten years**, until he died in March 1935. His loyalty touched the people of Tokyo, who fed him. In 1934, during his lifetime, a bronze statue of Hachikō was erected at the station. The present statue, made in 1948, stands there today, and Shibuya's "Hachikō exit" is a popular meeting point.

### Summary of the review

**The story.** In the film, Parker Wilson, a college professor, finds a lost Akita puppy at a railway station in a small American town. Nobody comes to claim the dog, and Parker, although his wife is at first reluctant, adopts him. He names the dog **Hachi**, the Japanese word for "eight", a lucky number. The two become inseparable. Every day the dog goes with Parker to the station and waits for him to return, and every evening they walk home together. One day the professor dies suddenly at the college. Hachi waits at the station that evening, and the next, and for years after, in rain, snow and sun, until the day he himself dies.

**What the reviewer admires.** The reviewer praises the **strong bond between the dog and his master**, which touches the hearts of the audience, and the **faithfulness** that the dog shows. The film is an emotional story that deals with **love, devotion and loyalty**, shown through a dog. It is a family film, yet it is **different** from other family films because it avoids cheap comedy and builds its power from quiet scenes. The reviewer also admires the way the film upholds **traditional values of marriage and family**.

**What the reviewer dislikes.** The reviewer criticises a few details of the film, such as the exchanges of kisses between adult characters and the sipping of champagne, which some parents may not want their children to see.

**Conclusion.** The statue of Hachikō was made to show faithfulness and loyalty, and the film honours that message. Overall, the review treats the film as a moving, family-friendly story whose strengths outweigh its small faults.

## 18.3 Grammar and writing tasks

### Grammar I — Asking questions

There are three main kinds of question in English.

| Type | Answer | Word order | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Yes/no question** | yes or no | **auxiliary + subject + verb** | **Did** they have a nice time? **Was** the hotel near the beach? |
| **Wh- question** | information | **wh-word + auxiliary + subject + verb** | **What** did he find on the beach? |
| **Subject question** | the subject itself | **wh-word + verb** (no auxiliary) | **Who** found the dog? |

**The wh-words**

| Word | Asks about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| what | thing, action | What are the kids doing? |
| who / whom | person | Who lives here? Whom did you meet? |
| whose | possession | Whose bag is this? |
| which | choice | Which school do you go to? |
| where | place | Where does Ram live? |
| when | time | When did it happen? |
| why | reason | Why didn't you phone me? |
| how | manner | How did you feel? |
| how many / how much | number / amount | How many books do you have? |
| how often | frequency | How often does she cook? |
| how long / how old / how far | duration / age / distance | How old are you? |

**Rules to remember**

1. In a question the **auxiliary comes before the subject**: *What are the kids doing?* not *What the kids are doing?*
2. If there is no auxiliary in the statement (simple present or past), add **do / does / did** and put the main verb back to its base form: *Why didn't you phone me?*
3. In **subject questions** (the wh-word is the subject), do **not** use *do/does/did*: *Who called you?* not *Who did call you?*
4. Polite requests use *may, could, would, shall*: *May I sit here? Shall we go to a movie?*
5. In **indirect (embedded) questions** the word order is that of a statement: *Do you know where he lives?* not *where does he live?*

::: example Worked example 18.1 — build the question
**Problem.** Make a question to ask your friend how often her father cooks.

**Solution.** Frequency is asked with *how often*. The auxiliary *does* comes next, then the subject, then the base verb: *How often does your father cook?*
:::

::: example Worked example 18.2 — put the words in order
**Problem.** Arrange: *did / find / what / he / on the beach?*

**Solution.** wh-word, auxiliary, subject, verb, the rest: *What did he find on the beach?*
:::

::: caution Do not double the auxiliary
*Did you went there?* is wrong. After *did* the verb is in the base form: *Did you go there?*
:::

### Grammar II — Causative verbs

A **causative verb** shows that the subject **causes** something to happen rather than doing it directly. The main causative verbs are **have, get, make, let**.

| Verb | Pattern | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **have** (active) | have + person + **base verb** | arrange for someone to do something | I **had a plumber fix** the pipe. |
| **have** (passive) | have + thing + **past participle** | arrange for something to be done | Ram **is having his house painted**. |
| **get** (active) | get + person + **to + verb** | persuade someone to do | My friend **got me to help** her. |
| **get** (passive) | get + thing + **past participle** | arrange for something to be done | I **got my hair trimmed**. |
| **make** | make + person + **base verb** | force or cause | The police **made him pay**. |
| **let** | let + person + **base verb** | allow | I don't **let my children play** video games. |
| **be made to** | passive of *make* | was forced | I **was made to wash** the dishes. |
| **be allowed to** | passive of *let* | was permitted | They were allowed to leave. |

```figure caption="Causative patterns at a glance."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.patches import FancyBboxPatch
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.0,2.4))
rows=[('have','+ person + base verb','I had a plumber fix it.'),('have / get','+ thing + past participle','I had my hair cut.'),('get','+ person + to + verb','She got me to help.'),('make','+ person + base verb','He made me wait.'),('let','+ person + base verb','She let him go.')]
for i,(a,b,c) in enumerate(rows):
    y=len(rows)-1-i
    ax.add_patch(FancyBboxPatch((0,y*0.5),1.1,0.4,boxstyle='round,pad=0.01,rounding_size=0.06',fc='#DCE8F8',ec=INK,lw=0.7))
    ax.text(0.55,y*0.5+0.2,a,ha='center',va='center',fontsize=7.4,fontweight='bold',color=INK)
    ax.text(1.25,y*0.5+0.2,b,ha='left',va='center',fontsize=7.2,color=INK)
    ax.text(3.45,y*0.5+0.2,c,ha='left',va='center',fontsize=7.2,color=ACCENT)
ax.set_xlim(-0.1,5.6); ax.set_ylim(-0.1,2.6); ax.axis('off')
```

**Rules to remember**

1. After **make** and **let**, use the **base verb** (no *to*): *She made me cry.* In the passive, *to* appears: *I was made to cry.*
2. After **get** (+ person), use **to + verb**: *I got him to repair it.*
3. After **have** (+ person), use the **base verb**: *I had him repair it.*
4. For a service done by someone else, the thing is followed by the **past participle**: *I had my car washed.* Compare *I washed my car* (I did it myself).
5. The tense is shown by the causative verb: *is having, had, will get*.

::: tip Who does the action?
*I cut my hair* → I did it. *I had my hair cut* → someone else did it. The causative with a past participle tells us the action was done *for* us.
:::

::: example Worked example 18.3 — choose the form
**Problem.** "Ram is having his house ______ (paint) next weekend."

**Solution.** The house will be painted by someone else, so *have + thing + past participle*: *painted*. Answer: *Ram is having his house painted next weekend.*
:::

::: caution Make / let take no "to"
*She made me to go* is wrong. Write *She made me go.* But *I was made to go.*
:::

### Writing I — A film review

A **review** gives information about a film (or book) **and** your judgement. Be fair: mention strengths and weaknesses.

| Part | What to write |
|---|---|
| **Title and rating** | name of the film, director, main actors, a rating (e.g. ★★★★☆) |
| **Introduction** | type of film, year, a one-line summary |
| **Summary** | the main story (without telling the ending) |
| **Evaluation** | acting, story, music, photography, message — strengths and weaknesses |
| **Conclusion** | your opinion and whom you recommend it to |

Useful words: *gripping, moving, predictable, well-directed, breathtaking cinematography, memorable performance, powerful message, a must-see*.

**Model review.**

**Title:** *Hachi: A Dog's Tale* (2009) **Rating:** ★★★★☆ (4/5)

*Hachi: A Dog's Tale* is a moving family drama directed by Lasse Hallström, based on the true story of an Akita dog in Japan who waited for his master at a railway station for years. The film follows a college professor who adopts a lost puppy, and the deep friendship that grows between them.

The film's greatest strength is its emotional honesty. Richard Gere plays the professor with warmth and restraint, and the dog's patient waiting at the station scenes are unforgettable. The photography of the small town through the four seasons is beautiful, and the music is gentle and never forces our feelings. The story upholds the values of family and loyalty. On the other hand, the film is slow in places and some viewers may find the ending very sad.

Overall, this is a film that proves that love and loyalty need no words. I recommend it to all families, but keep a handkerchief ready.

### Writing II — An essay on the impact of social media

Many people say that social networking sites have a negative effect on individuals and society. Others believe that they have connected the world. An essay on this topic should give **both sides** and then your own opinion.

| Part | Content |
|---|---|
| Introduction | what social media is; the debate |
| Advantages | communication, information, education, business, awareness |
| Disadvantages | time-wasting, addiction, false news, cyber-bullying, loss of privacy |
| Opinion | balanced view: depends on how it is used |
| Conclusion | a strong closing sentence |

**Model essay — The Impact of Social Media**

Social media, such as Facebook, TikTok, YouTube and Instagram, has become a part of everyday life. People of all ages use it to talk to friends, to share photos and to follow the news. Some say that it brings the world together; others say that it harms individuals and society. In my opinion it has both good and bad effects.

Social media has many advantages. It lets us keep in touch with family members living abroad, which is very valuable for a country like Nepal where so many people work overseas. It spreads information quickly, so people learn about disasters, health warnings and government announcements within minutes. Students use it for learning, teachers share lessons online, and small businesses sell their products to customers across the country. Stories like that of a skateboarder who became famous through one video, or a dog that received a wheelchair thanks to donations, show how powerful social media can be for good.

However, there are also serious disadvantages. Many users waste hours scrolling and become addicted, which harms their studies and sleep. False news and rumours spread faster than the truth. Cyber-bullying and the sharing of private photos can damage people's lives, and hackers and cheats find victims online. Comparing ourselves with the perfect lives of others can also make us unhappy.

In my view, social media is like a knife: it can be used to cut vegetables or to hurt someone. The harm comes from careless use, not from the tool itself. We should limit our time, check news before sharing, protect our passwords, be polite online and use social media for learning and help.

In conclusion, social media is a gift when used wisely and a danger when used badly. The choice is ours.
