Every society sets aside special days to remember its past, thank nature and enjoy life together. This unit takes you from an orange-throwing carnival in northern Italy to harvest thanksgivings on five continents. You will meet vivid travel writing and a clear piece of factual description, then learn how to report **questions**, and practise writing a festival essay and a news story.

::: key What the SEE paper asks from this unit
Reading: true/false, matching words to meanings, "what does X symbolise / how does the narrator feel" short answers. Grammar: turning direct questions into reported questions (yes/no and wh-questions). Writing: an essay about a festival, and a news story from clues.
:::

## 2.1 Battle of the Oranges

### About the text

"Battle of the Oranges" is a first-person travel account by Margherita, published on the travel blog *The Crowded Planet* (the textbook adapts it). It describes the **Carnevale di Ivrea**, held every year in the town of Ivrea, about an hour north of Turin in northern Italy, on the last days of Carnival. The writer goes as a visitor and ends up a participant. It is a **narrative of personal experience**: it uses *I*, the present tense for immediacy, vivid sensory detail and dialogue.

### What the text says

The narrator is hit in the chest by an orange and falls into a heap of orange mush. Her first thought is to check that her **red hat** is still on her head. During Carnival, public notices tell everyone, especially tourists, to wear the red hat; people without it count as "fair targets" for orange-throwing. The hat, the *berretto frigio*, is the symbol of the carnival itself and of **freedom**: in Roman times it was worn by freed slaves, and in the Middle Ages by peasants who rose against their feudal lords.

She is hit again on the forehead. When she wipes her face she is horrified to see red — she thinks it is blood and fears she will need stitches or has a concussion — then she remembers that some oranges are **blood oranges**. Relief follows the shock.

Retreating to the back of the square, she watches the battle: nine teams of **aranceri** (orange-throwers) on foot, in uniform, hurl oranges at opponents riding in horse-drawn carts, who wear padded costumes and leather helmets that remind her of the television hero Doctor Who. After an hour the square is covered with a sludge of orange pulp and horse manure that smells "sharp, sweet-and-sour". Whenever a cart approaches, the aranceri charge and bombard it; the horses halt for a moment, oranges explode, then the cart moves off and the aranceri chase it, throwing their last oranges. They wear no protection; some have broken noses or sore ribs, yet they hold their heads high and shout their team's war cry.

She talks to **Silvia**, who holds half an orange on her swollen eye. This, says Silvia, is "the moment we wait for all year"; she believes the carnival is good for psychological health because for three days people can "let it all out" — the frustrations of the year. The narrator wonders whether to join in. Volunteers advise visitors to stay behind protective nets, but **Massi**, an arancere of the *Morte* team, says "Forget the nets": to live the carnival, you must be in the middle of the battle, and a black eye is a small price for the rush. He invites her to throw away her hat and join him; she is tempted but **declines**.

At the end of the full article Silvia later hands her a bag of oranges, and she does throw — and feels light, free and "liberated" (this part is outside the textbook extract, so do not use it in an exam answer unless the question asks for it).

### Background

The battle is said to re-enact a medieval revolt against a tyrant. According to local legend, a miller's daughter, Violetta, refused to submit to a feudal lord and killed him, which led the townspeople to burn his castle. In the battle the aranceri on foot stand for the **people**, and the carts stand for the **lord's soldiers**. Historians think the orange-throwing itself grew from a nineteenth-century custom of tossing oranges at parades as a sign of respect. Thousands of tonnes of oranges are used, and afterwards they are turned into compost. (Treat these background facts as extra information, not as textbook content.)

### Themes and ideas

- **Freedom and rebellion.** The red hat and the story of the revolt make the festival a celebration of freedom from tyranny.
- **Release of tension.** Silvia treats it as a safety valve for frustration; the festival lets people be wild in a safe, agreed way.
- **Insider and outsider.** The narrator starts as a nervous observer; Massi and Silvia invite her to belong. Her decision to refuse shows how hard it is to cross that line.
- **Danger and fun together.** Broken noses and black eyes are accepted as "a small price".

## 2.2 Thanksgiving Around the World

### About the text

This is an **informative article**. It shows that Thanksgiving, which many people think of as an American holiday, has cousins across the world: people everywhere thank nature or God for the harvest, enjoy food together and honour their ancestors. The text takes you country by country, giving for each the name of the festival, the date, its reason and its special food or activity. Such **description by example** is a common reading task.

### What the text says

| Country | Festival | When / why | Special features |
|---|---|---|---|
| USA | Thanksgiving | Fourth Thursday of November; remembers the feast the Pilgrims held after the first harvest in 1621 (attended, the text says, by 90 Native Americans and 53 Pilgrims) | Family dinner; roast turkey (about 45 million eaten each year), mashed and sweet potatoes, corn; parades such as Macy's in New York with floats and giant balloons |
| South Korea | Chuseok (Harvest Moon Festival) | 15th day of the 8th lunar month; thanks for the fall harvest and respect for ancestors | People return to their hometowns; food and wine offered to ancestors; *songpyeon* (crescent-shaped rice cakes steamed on pine needles); visits to graves; games such as tug-of-war, archery and Korean wrestling; hanbok worn |
| Brazil | Dia de Ação de Graças | Started in 1949 by President Gaspar Dutra after he admired the American holiday | Mass of thanks, lively carnival, crowds go to the beach; turkey with a sauce of *jabuticaba*, the "Brazilian grape", since cranberries do not grow there |
| Liberia | Thanksgiving | Begun in the 19th century by freed American slaves, giving thanks for freedom | Church service; roast chicken, green-bean casserole and mashed cassava; an evening of dancing and singing |
| Barbados | Crop Over | May to August, ending with the Grand Kadooment; began in the 1780s to mark the sugar-cane harvest | Carnival, calypso bands, flamboyant dancers |
| Ghana | Homowo ("to hoot at hunger") | May to August–September; celebrates the end of a famine | Palm-nut soup with fish and *kpokoi* (corn-dough grits); a ban on drumming first, then a night of loud drumming and dancing |
| Iran | Mehregan | 196th day of the Iranian year; dates back at least to the 4th century BC; celebrates harvest and the goddess Mehr | New clothes, community blessings, a feast on a table with marjoram; sherbet; kohl rubbed round the eyes for luck; seeds thrown over each other |

### Themes and ideas

- **Food and family.** Feasts, home-coming and sharing strengthen community.
- **Borrowing and adapting.** Brazil took the idea from the USA but changed the sauce; traditions travel and change.

## 2.3 Grammar and writing tasks of the unit

### Grammar: Reported questions

A direct question becomes a **statement-shaped** clause when reported: no question mark, no auxiliary verb in front, no inversion.

#### Rules

1. Use the reporting verb **asked** (or *wanted to know, enquired, inquired*), not *said*.
2. **Yes/No questions** (those that begin with a verb such as *is, are, do, did, have, can, will*) take **if** or **whether**.
3. **Wh-questions** (*what, where, when, why, how, who*) keep the question word.
4. Use **statement word order**: subject first, then verb. Drop *do/does/did*.
5. Backshift the tense and change pronouns, time and place words as for statements.
6. End with a **full stop**, not a question mark.

| Direct question | Reported question |
|---|---|
| "Are you working tonight?" | She asked if/whether I was working that night. |
| "Is this the road to the station?" | He asked if/whether that was the road to the station. |
| "Did you do your homework?" | The teacher asked if/whether I had done my homework. |
| "Have you been to Paris?" | She asked if/whether I had been to Paris. |
| "Where will you stay tonight?" | He asked where I would stay that night. |
| "How long have you been working in this company?" | She asked how long I had been working in that company. |
| "What flavour ice cream did you have at the party?" | He asked what flavour ice cream I had had at the party. |

::: caution Common mistakes
*He asked me where did I live* — wrong. Write *He asked me where I lived.* Do not use *if* with wh-questions (*He asked if where I lived*). Do not put a question mark at the end of a reported question.
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Report: Kritika asked me, "Why did you go out last night?"

**Solution.** Wh-question → keep *why*; statement order: *I went*; backshift *went → had gone*; *you → I*; *last night → the night before*. *Kritika asked me why I had gone out the night before.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Report: "Do you live in Patan?" said the Principal.

**Solution.** Yes/no question → *if/whether*; remove *do*; *live → lived*. *The Principal asked me whether I lived in Patan.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Report: Anusha said to Melisha, "What were you doing when I saw you?"

**Solution.** *were doing → had been doing*; *you → she*; *I → she*. *Anusha asked Melisha what she had been doing when she had seen her.*
:::

### Writing I: An essay on a festival

An **essay** has an introduction, a body of three or four paragraphs, and a conclusion. When describing a festival, plan your paragraphs around the points the task lists: **history**, **people involved**, **main activities**, **religious or social importance**, **duration**, and **drawbacks**.

::: example Model essay — Tihar
**Tihar: The Festival of Lights**

Tihar, also called Deepawali, is the second biggest festival of Nepal after Dashain. It falls in Kartik (October–November) and is celebrated for five days by Hindus in all parts of the country.

The festival honours Yama, the god of death, and Lakshmi, the goddess of wealth. According to tradition, Yamaraj's sister Yamuna put a protective *tika* on his forehead, which is why sisters put tika on their brothers' foreheads on the last day.

Every family takes part. On the first day crows are fed, on the second dogs are worshipped, on the third cows are worshipped and Lakshmi puja is held at night, on the fourth day oxen are honoured and the Newars perform Mha Puja, and on the fifth day brothers and sisters celebrate Bhai Tika. Houses are cleaned, lit with oil lamps and candles and decorated with rangoli. Children and young people go from door to door singing *Deusi* and *Bhailo* and are given money and sweets.

Tihar is important because it teaches kindness to animals, thankfulness for wealth and love between brothers and sisters. It also brings families and neighbours together.

There are some drawbacks. Firecrackers cause noise and air pollution and sometimes injuries or fires, and gambling spoils the festival for some families. People should celebrate safely, with fewer crackers and more lamps.

In short, Tihar is a joyful festival of light, family and gratitude, and we must preserve it while avoiding its harmful habits.
:::

### Writing II: A news story from clues

A **news story** answers *who, what, where, when, why and how* in its first paragraph, then adds details in order of importance. It has a **headline**, a **dateline**, is written in the **past tense** and in the third person, and uses reported speech for quotations.

**Task.** Write a news story from these clues: *Vitamin A capsules given to children — health assistants give Vitamin A capsules, 19–20 October — children from six months to five years — deworming tablets to children between 1 and 5 — announced by the Department of Health Services — programme continuing since 2050 BS.*

::: example Model news story
**Health Workers Give Vitamin A Capsules to Children**

KATHMANDU, 21 October — Health assistants gave Vitamin A capsules to children across the country on 19 and 20 October.

The Department of Health Services announced that children from six months to five years received the capsules. Children between one and five years were also given deworming tablets.

Vitamin A protects the eyes and helps children fight disease. The department said that the programme had been running since 2050 BS and that it had greatly reduced night-blindness. Officials added that parents who had missed the camp could take their children to the nearest health post.
:::

2. Write a news story: *Students clean the river bank — Nepal Scouts, 5 June, World Environment Day — 300 students — 20 sacks of plastic collected — mayor praised.* <span class="marks">[4]</span>

::: note Answers
**1.** Use the plan in the Tihar model: introduction (name, date, who celebrates); history/belief; people and activities; importance; duration; drawbacks and conclusion.
**2.** *Model:* **Students Clean the River Bank.** BHAKTAPUR, 5 June — About 300 students and Scouts cleaned the river bank on World Environment Day. They collected 20 sacks of plastic waste. The mayor praised their effort and said that the municipality would put up bins along the river.
:::
