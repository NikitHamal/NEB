What we do for pleasure in our free time says a good deal about us — and, as this unit shows, it can even shape our careers. The first text surveys popular hobbies and what each one gives you. The second is a short poem about a child climbing an apple tree, which turns out to be about never giving up. In grammar you will learn the **passive voice**; in writing, an **essay**, an **advertisement** and **paraphrasing**.

::: key What the SEE paper asks from this unit
Reading: synonyms and antonyms, words in sentences, true/false/not given, short answers; for the poem, words from meanings, multiple choice, imagery and message. Grammar: active ↔ passive voice, including *being* and *having* forms. Writing: essay on hobbies, job advertisement, paraphrase.
:::

## 8.1 Common Hobbies

### About the text

"Common Hobbies" is an **informative article** with a lively, magazine-style voice. It lists hobbies popular around the world — reading, dancing, painting, collecting antiques, photography, writing and journaling, knitting — and for each tells you what you need, what you gain and how to start. Its underlying argument is that a hobby is not a waste of time: it relaxes the mind, builds skills and can lead to a career.

### What the text says

- **Why hobbies matter.** A hobby is something we do for pleasure in our free time. It lets us let off steam, ignite a passion and give our life a new structure. Which hobbies are popular depends on individual taste and on culture.
- **Reading.** The most popular hobby, because it is fun *and* a way of improving yourself. You can choose among many kinds of books and need no special equipment.
- **Dancing.** Put on some music and let your body move to the beat. You can practise at home, join a dance studio or take an online course. Not only trained dancers choose dancing as a hobby.
- **Painting.** Visit an art-supply shop, buy paints and brushes and begin. Paintings let you express your feelings.
- **Collecting and refurbishing antiques.** Old objects can be refurbished and used for interior decoration, and they carry stories.
- **Photography.** Preserves memories and fills the walls with beautiful pictures. It does not need professional gear, and amateurs, not just professionals, enjoy it.
- **Writing and keeping a journal.** Writing down thoughts and plans helps people become more successful; a journal or a blog is a record and a way to think. (Many people also start a blog to share experiences.)
- **Knitting.** Its best feature is the flexibility of time and place; you can knit while hanging out with friends or travelling.

The article closes by saying that a hobby can change into a **career**: someone passionate about a hobby may become an in-demand professional.

## 8.2 Climbing

### About the poet and the poem

```photo id="eng10/amy-lowell" caption="Amy Lowell (1874–1925), American poet, photographed in 1916." width="58"
```

**Amy Lowell** (1874–1925) was an American poet from a famous Massachusetts family in Brookline, near Boston. She was a leader of the **Imagist** movement, which wanted clear, concrete pictures in short poems instead of vague, ornamental language. She also wrote and lectured widely about poetry; after her death she was awarded the Pulitzer Prize for Poetry (1926) for her collection *What's O'Clock*. "Climbing" is a short, simple poem for a young reader, written in four **rhyming couplets**.

### The poem

> High up in the apple tree climbing I go,
> With the sky above me, the earth below.
> Each branch is the step of a wonderful stair
> Which leads to the town I see shining up there.
>
> Climbing, climbing, higher and higher,
> The branches blow and I see a spire,
> The gleam of a turret, the glint of a dome,
> All sparkling and bright, like white sea foam.
>
> On and on, from bough to bough,
> The leaves are thick, but I push my way through;
> Before, I have always had to stop,
> But to-day I am sure I shall reach the top.
>
> To-day to the end of the marvelous stair,
> Where those glittering pinnacles flash in the air!
> Climbing, climbing, higher I go,
> With the sky close above me, the earth far below.

### Meaning, stanza by stanza

1. **Stanza 1.** The speaker, a child, climbs an apple tree. Between sky above and earth below, each branch seems a **step of a wonderful staircase** leading to a shining town. The child imagines the tree as a way to a magic place.
2. **Stanza 2.** As the child climbs, the wind moves the branches and the child glimpses a **spire**, a **turret** and a **dome**, bright "like white sea foam". The poem moves from the real tree to an imagined fairy-tale city.
3. **Stanza 3.** The leaves are thick, but the child pushes through. In the past the child always stopped part-way. **Today is different**: "I am sure I shall reach the top".
4. **Stanza 4.** The child imagines reaching the end of the staircase where **pinnacles** flash. Now the sky is "close above" and the earth is "far below". The climb has brought the child to a new height.

### Message

Determination and imagination help us reach our goals. Failure in the past (*Before, I have always had to stop*) need not decide the future; if we keep going, we may succeed. The climb is also a symbol for any hobby or ambition. (A hobby like climbing a tree is also a way of enjoying nature and testing yourself.)

### Poetic devices

| Device | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Rhyme (couplets) | go / below; stair / there; higher / spire | Musical, child-like rhythm |
| Repetition | "Climbing, climbing" | Shows long, steady effort and gives rhythm |
| Simile | "like white sea foam" | A picture of the bright domes |
| Metaphor | "Each branch is the step of a wonderful stair" | The tree becomes a staircase |
| Imagery (light) | gleam, glint, sparkling, glittering, flash | Makes the imagined town shine |
| Symbolism | sky = aspiration; earth = starting point | The climb stands for striving upwards |
| Contrast | sky above / earth below | Shows how far the child has come |

### Vocabulary

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| gleam | a soft, steady light (here, a reflection of light) |
| glint | a flash of light reflected from something shiny |
| turret | a small tower on a building or castle |
| stair | a set of steps between floors |
| marvellous | causing great wonder; extraordinary |
| bough | a main branch of a tree |
| spire | a tall pointed structure on a church tower |
| dome | a rounded roof |
| pinnacle | a pointed top of a tower or roof; the highest point |

## 8.3 Grammar and writing tasks of the unit

### Grammar I: The passive voice

In an **active** sentence the doer (agent) is the subject: *Ram feeds the dogs.* In a **passive** sentence the receiver of the action is the subject: *The dogs are fed by Ram.* Use the passive when the doer is unknown, unimportant or obvious, or when you want to stress the action or the receiver.

**Formula:** object of the active → subject; **be (in the tense of the active verb) + past participle**; subject of the active → **by + agent** (often dropped).

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present | Ram feeds the dogs. | The dogs are fed by Ram. |
| Present continuous | Ram is feeding the dogs. | The dogs are being fed by Ram. |
| Present perfect | Ram has fed the dogs. | The dogs have been fed by Ram. |
| Simple past | Ram fed the dogs. | The dogs were fed by Ram. |
| Past continuous | Ram was feeding the dogs. | The dogs were being fed by Ram. |
| Past perfect | Ram had fed the dogs. | The dogs had been fed by Ram. |
| Simple future | Ram will feed the dogs. | The dogs will be fed by Ram. |
| Modal | Ram can feed the dogs. | The dogs can be fed by Ram. |


::: caution Common mistakes
Use the **past participle**, not the past simple (*was eaten*, not *was ate*). Keep the tense of the active verb: *Ram fed → were fed*, not *are fed*. Do not use the passive with **intransitive verbs** (*arrive, die, happen, go*) because they have no object. The passive with *by* is often unnecessary: *The thief was caught* (not *by the police*) is natural.
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Change into the passive: "The police catch a lot of criminals in a city."

**Solution.** Object *a lot of criminals* → subject; simple present → *are caught*; the agent *the police* may be dropped. *A lot of criminals are caught in a city.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Change into the passive: "Someone has stolen my bicycle."

**Solution.** Present perfect → *has been stolen*. *My bicycle has been stolen.* (The agent *someone* is unknown, so it is omitted.)
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Change into the passive: "They were repairing the road."

**Solution.** Past continuous → *was being repaired*. *The road was being repaired.*
:::

### Grammar II: Passive with *being* and *having*; passive to active

The **-ing forms** of the passive are used after prepositions and verbs such as *enjoy, avoid, mind, like*: **being + past participle** (present) and **having been + past participle** (earlier action).

| Active | Passive |
|---|---|
| She hates people telling her what to do. | She hates being told what to do. |
| I remember my father taking me to the zoo. | I remember being taken to the zoo by my father. |
| He was angry because they had not invited him. | He was angry about not having been invited. |
| Having finished the work, the staff left. | The work having been finished, the staff left. |

#### Passive to active

To change a passive sentence back to active: make the *by-agent* the subject (add a suitable subject if the agent is missing: *someone, people, they*), keep the tense, and make the old subject the object.

| Passive | Active |
|---|---|
| The match was won by Nepal. | Nepal won the match. |
| A new school is being built. | They are building a new school. |
| The letter has been posted. | Someone has posted the letter. |
| English is spoken here. | People speak English here. |

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Rewrite using *being*: "Nobody likes people laughing at them."

**Solution.** *Nobody likes being laughed at.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Change into active: "The prize was given to Maya by the principal."

**Solution.** *The principal gave the prize to Maya.*
:::

### Writing I: An essay on hobbies and career

**Task.** People have different hobbies and interests, which may lead them to their careers. Write an essay on your hobbies explaining how they might influence your career.

A good essay has an **introduction** (state your hobby and the idea), **body paragraphs** (one point each, with examples), and a **conclusion**.

::: example Model essay
**My Hobbies and My Future**

A hobby is an activity we enjoy in our free time. My hobbies are reading and drawing, and I believe they will influence my career.

Reading is my first hobby. I read story books, newspapers and science magazines. It has improved my vocabulary and my writing and has made me curious about the world. If I become a teacher or a journalist, these skills will help me explain things clearly.

My second hobby is drawing. I draw buildings, trees and houses in my village. Drawing has taught me patience, careful observation and the use of shapes and proportion. If I become an engineer or an architect, I will use these skills to design buildings that are useful and beautiful.

My hobbies also help me relax after hard study, so I do my school work with a fresh mind. A relaxed person works better in any job.

In conclusion, hobbies are not a waste of time. Mine have shown me what I enjoy, and I would like to choose a career in which I can use them.
:::

### Writing II: An advertisement and paraphrasing

An **advertisement (vacancy announcement)** is short and formal. It gives: **name of the institution**, **the post and number of vacancies**, **qualifications**, **experience**, **age/other conditions**, **how to apply and the deadline**, and **contact details**. Use a box or a bold heading and a list.

::: example Model advertisement
**VACANCY ANNOUNCEMENT**

**Shree Saraswati Secondary School, Pokhara-9**

Applications are invited from qualified Nepali citizens for the following post:

**Post:** Secondary-level English Teacher (1 vacancy)

**Qualifications:** Bachelor's degree in English Education (B.Ed.) or Master's degree in English; teaching licence preferred.

**Experience:** At least two years of teaching experience at secondary level.

**Other:** Good command of spoken and written English; computer skills.

**How to apply:** Send a written application with copies of your citizenship, academic certificates and experience letters and two passport-size photographs to the school office.

**Deadline:** 15 Ashwin 2082 (5 pm). Only shortlisted candidates will be called for an interview.

Phone: 061-000000 — Headteacher
:::

#### Paraphrasing

To **paraphrase** is to say the same thing in **your own words**, keeping the meaning but changing the words and the structure. A paraphrase is not a summary (it is usually about as long as the original). Steps: (1) read and understand; (2) replace key words by synonyms; (3) change the order or the structure (active ↔ passive, join or split sentences); (4) compare with the original to check that the meaning is the same.

| Original | Paraphrase |
|---|---|
| Hobbies help us to relax after a hard day. | Doing something we love in our spare time helps us to rest our minds after tiring work. |
| Reading is a fun way of improving yourself. | Books entertain us and make us wiser at the same time. |
| The more determined you are, the better your chances. | If you are very determined, you will have a greater likelihood of success. |

::: caution Common mistake
Do not change only one or two words, and do not change the meaning. Do not copy whole phrases without quotation marks.
:::

## Chapter summary

- *Common Hobbies* describes reading, dancing, painting, antiques, photography, journaling and knitting; hobbies relax us, build skills and can become careers.
- *Climbing* by Amy Lowell (American Imagist poet): a child climbs an apple tree; the staircase of branches leads to a shining imagined town; message — determination can carry us to the top.
- Devices in the poem: rhyming couplets, repetition, simile, metaphor, light imagery, symbolism.
- Passive: *be + past participle* in the same tense as the active verb; use *by + agent* only when it matters; *being / having been* for -ing forms.
- An essay has an introduction, body paragraphs and a conclusion; an advertisement gives post, qualifications, experience, how to apply and the deadline; a paraphrase says the same in your own words.
