Health is not only about doctors and medicine; it is also about kindness, communication and the food we eat. In this unit a worried mother complains, in a letter, about the way a hospital treated her sick baby, and a European speaker explains why healthy eating needs research and teamwork. You will practise reporting **commands and requests**, using a wider range of **reporting verbs**, writing an **apology email** and drafting a **speech**.

::: key What the SEE paper asks from this unit
Reading: matching words and meanings, true/false, short answers about the writer's complaint and the speaker's message. Grammar: reported commands, requests and advice (*told, asked, requested, advised + person + to / not to*). Writing: an email, a speech, or a formal letter.
:::

## 3.1 A Letter from a Patient

### About the text

"A Letter from a Patient" is a personal letter from the mother of a very sick baby to the people who looked after her in hospital. Its purpose is not to attack health workers but to remind them how their behaviour feels to a frightened family. It is written in the **first person** and in an emotional but controlled voice. It is useful as a model of a *letter of complaint* that wants improvement, not revenge.

### What the text says

The writer's nightmare begins when her baby, only a few weeks old, develops a very high fever and has to be admitted to the paediatric (children's) ward. She is exhausted and frightened and needs help, but she finds that the staff are more concerned with their own affairs than with the child.

She lists the weaknesses she saw.

- **Bad-mouthing.** Staff of different departments and units spoke ill of one another in front of patients' families instead of working harmoniously as a team.
- **Lack of priority.** When she asked for help, a nurse said she was "so busy today". The nurse did not say, "Your daughter is not a priority", but the writer felt exactly that.
- **Repeated questions and careless paperwork.** She was asked the same questions again and again ("Was the delivery normal?" "Was the baby premature?"), which showed that nobody read the chart; the chart even confused the baby's gender, and people referred to her daughter by the wrong sex.
- **No human warmth.** Staff talked about their own problems and wishes but did not show interest in her feelings.

Even so, the writer ends with respect. She knows that health work is a noble, precious career. Patients and their families, she says, are *vulnerable*; they trust health workers to be honest, serious and caring. She asks the hospital staff to remember that, for the family, each patient is the most important person in the world.

### Vocabulary

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| nightmare | a very unpleasant or frightening experience | The long night in the ward was a nightmare. |
| paediatric | relating to the medical care of children | The paediatric ward was full of toys. |
| intravenous | into or connected to a vein | An intravenous drip gave the baby fluids. |
| priority | something regarded as more important than other things | A patient in danger is a priority. |
| medication | treatment using drugs | She gave the baby her medication on time. |
| confidante | a person with whom private matters are discussed | The nurse became her confidante. |
| deprivation | lack of something considered a necessity | Sleep deprivation made her weak. |
| vulnerable | in need of special care, support or protection | Newborn babies are vulnerable. |
| dreadful | very bad or unpleasant | The waiting room was dreadful. |

### Themes and ideas

- **Empathy.** Health workers must care about how patients *feel*, not only about treatment.
- **Teamwork and professionalism.** Staff who gossip about colleagues lose the trust of families.
- **Accuracy.** Careless records are dangerous: a wrong chart can mean a wrong treatment.
- **Vulnerability.** A frightened parent notices every word and tone.

## 3.2 A Healthy Diet for a Healthy Life

### About the text

This text is a **speech**, not an essay. It was delivered to an audience of European officials and researchers at a conference about a research-and-innovation programme called the **Joint Programming Initiative "A Healthy Diet for a Healthy Life"**. The speaker's aim is to persuade the member states of the European Union (EU) to cooperate. Notice the typical features of a speech: it greets the audience, uses *we* and *you*, repeats key ideas, asks for action and ends by thanking the listeners.

### What the speaker says

She begins with a subject that is "never out of the media": food and health. **Obesity** is, she says, the greatest public-health challenge today. Poor diet and low physical activity are on the rise, and the trend among children in the EU has been getting worse for three decades. Poor diet leads to diseases such as **type 2 diabetes**, **cardiovascular diseases**, some cancers and, later in life, **neurological disorders**. People can prevent many of these by adopting better lifestyles and healthier diets.

Research is the key to this. Scientists must learn how diet affects health, how to measure it (through **biomarkers**), and how to change people's habits. Because the problem has many sides — biology, food industry, economics, psychology — a **multidisciplinary approach** is necessary. The speaker explains that the innovation chain begins with researchers, goes through companies and ends with consumers and patients.

The Joint Programming Initiative brings together the knowledge of member states so that they do not repeat each other's work. Europe's research programme **Horizon 2020**, worth about 80 billion euros, can fund top-level research on nutrition, health, diet-related diseases and ageing. If it is used effectively, the results will be new products, treatments, services and strategies, healthier citizens and opportunities for competitive European businesses. She ends by saying she expects the conference to take this work forward and thanks the audience.

### Vocabulary

| Word | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| wonderfood | a food thought to be very good for health | Many people call blueberries a wonderfood. |
| cardiovascular | relating to the heart and blood vessels | Walking reduces cardiovascular disease. |
| bioeconomy | the part of the economy that uses renewable biological resources | Farming is part of the bioeconomy. |
| demographic change | change in the structure of a population | Demographic change means more old people. |
| multidisciplinary | involving people from different subjects | The team is multidisciplinary. |
| inequality | difference in income or wealth | Economic inequalities affect diet. |
| neurological | relating to the brain, spine and nerves | Epilepsy is a neurological disorder. |
| biomarker | a measurable sign of a biological condition | Blood sugar is a biomarker for diabetes. |
| unprecedented | never known or done before | Unprecedented floods hit the town. |
| dramatically | suddenly and very greatly | Prices rose dramatically. |
| diagnosis | identification of a disease | Early diagnosis saves lives. |

## 3.3 Grammar and writing tasks of the unit

### Grammar I: Reported commands, requests and advice

A **command** or **request** in direct speech uses the imperative mood (*Do it!*, *Please help me.*). In reported speech it becomes **reporting verb + person + to-infinitive** (positive) or **+ not to + infinitive** (negative). There is no backshift because there is no tense to shift.

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| "Do your homework!" | The teacher told me to do my homework. |
| "Don't make a mess!" | My mother told me not to make a mess. |
| "Please help me fix this machine." | She requested me to help her fix the machine. |
| "Don't switch on the fan, please." | The electrician requested me not to switch on the fan. |
| "You should stop smoking." / "Don't smoke!" | The doctor advised him not to smoke. |
| "Be not afraid, noble prince," said the Guru. | The Guru told the prince not to be afraid. |
| "Let me do my work." | He asked/requested me to let him do his work. |
| "Let's play a friendly football match," said the coach. | The coach suggested/proposed playing a friendly football match. |

#### Choose the verb that matches the tone

| Direct sentence has | Reporting verb |
|---|---|
| a strong order | ordered, commanded, told |
| *please*, *would you*, *could you* | requested, asked, begged |
| advice (*you should*, *if I were you*) | advised, suggested, urged |
| a warning | warned |
| *Let's...* | suggested / proposed + -ing |

::: caution Common mistakes
Do not write *told that* with a command (*He told that I go*). Do not keep *please* (use *requested* instead). With a negative, write **not to** + verb (*told me not to run*), never *to not*.
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Report: Madan said to his friend, "Please help me with money."

**Solution.** The word *please* shows a request, so use *requested*; the person helped is *his friend*; positive imperative → *to help*. *Madan requested his friend to help him with money.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Report: The teacher said to the boys, "Read silently."

**Solution.** A plain command → *told*. *The teacher told the boys to read silently.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Report: My teacher said to me, "Don't look behind."

**Solution.** Negative command → *told + person + not to*. *My teacher told me not to look behind.*
:::

### Grammar II: Reporting verbs in different kinds of sentences

Real reports mix statements, questions and commands. The right reporting verb tells the reader what the speaker was doing.

| Sentence type | Typical reporting verbs and patterns |
|---|---|
| statement | said / told + *that*; explained, replied, added, announced, admitted |
| question | asked / wanted to know / inquired + *if, whether, wh-word* |
| command, request | told, ordered, requested, asked + object + *to* |
| advice | advised, suggested, urged + object + *to* |
| warning | warned + object + *(not) to* |
| promise, offer, refusal | promised, offered, refused + *to* |
| thanks, apology, congratulation | thanked / apologised / congratulated + object + *for -ing* |

*Said* is followed by *that* or *to someone* (*said to me*); *told* needs an object (*told me*). Use *advised*, *warned* and *told* with an object.

### Writing I: An email of apology

An **email** has a **subject line**, a salutation, a short body and a closing. Formal emails are polite, use full sentences and say clearly *why* you are writing. An **apology email** should (1) say sorry plainly, (2) state what went wrong, (3) say what you will do to put it right, and (4) end politely.

**Task.** Imagine you are one of the hospital staff. Write an email to the patient's mother apologising for your behaviour and assuring her of improvement.

::: example Model email
**To:** the patient's mother
**Subject:** Our sincere apology and our promise to improve

Dear Madam,

I am writing on behalf of the staff of the children's ward. We have read your letter with great concern, and I want to apologise sincerely for the way you and your baby were treated.

You were right to expect honesty, patience and care. Instead, our staff were too busy to help you quickly, asked you the same questions repeatedly, recorded your baby's details wrongly and spoke badly of one another in front of you. This was unprofessional, and we are deeply sorry for the worry it caused you at such a difficult time.

We have taken the following steps. We are training all staff in communication and patient care. Each patient's chart will be checked by two people. Team meetings will be held every week so that departments cooperate. A nurse will be appointed to speak to every family each day.

We wish your baby a speedy recovery. If you would like to talk to us about any other matter, please contact me directly.

Yours sincerely,
A member of staff, Children's Ward
:::

### Writing II: A speech

A **speech** is written to be *heard*. Open with a greeting and a hook; state the topic; use short, clear sentences, repetition and rhetorical questions; give two or three main points; close with a summary or appeal and a thank-you.

**Task.** Obesity has become a matter of concern. Draft a speech on obesity answering: What are its primary causes? What are its consequences? What should an individual do to avoid it?

::: example Model speech
Respected principal, teachers and dear friends,

Good morning. Today I want to talk about a quiet problem that is growing in our country and in the world: obesity.

What causes it? Mainly two things: eating too much unhealthy food, such as fast food, sweets and fried snacks, and moving too little. Many of us sit for hours with mobile phones and travel even short distances by motorbike.

What are the consequences? Obesity can lead to diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease. It makes us tired, lowers self-confidence and costs families a great deal in medical bills.

What can each of us do? First, eat a balanced diet with more vegetables, fruit and pulses and fewer sugary drinks. Second, play or walk for at least thirty minutes every day. Third, sleep well and drink enough water.

Friends, a healthy body is the first wealth. Let us start today. Thank you.
:::

## Chapter summary

- *A Letter from a Patient*: a mother's respectful complaint about a hospital — bad-mouthing between staff, a busy nurse who made the baby feel "not a priority", repeated questions and a wrong chart. Message: health workers must be honest, serious and caring.
- *A Healthy Diet for a Healthy Life*: a speech asking EU member states to cooperate on research; obesity is the greatest public-health challenge; Horizon 2020 (about 80 billion euros) can fund the research; a multidisciplinary approach is needed.
- Reported commands: reporting verb + object + (not) to + infinitive; no backshift; choose *told, ordered, requested, advised, warned, suggested* by tone.
- *Said* + *that*; *told* + object + *that*; *asked* + *if/whether*.
- An apology email: sorry, what went wrong, what you will do, polite close. A speech: greeting, topic, main points, appeal, thanks.
