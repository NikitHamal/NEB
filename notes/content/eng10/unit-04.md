Work feeds a family and builds a country; leisure rests the mind. This unit asks what honest work really means. A short story shows two children whose first job goes comically wrong. A famous poem by Rabindranath Tagore says that the best form of worship is honest labour. In grammar you will study **modal verbs** and **imperatives**; in writing, a **job application** and a set of **rules and regulations**.

::: key What the SEE paper asks from this unit
Reading: vocabulary matching, true/false/not given, short answers on character and plot; for the poem, meanings of words, archaic words, and what the poet advises. Grammar: functions of modal verbs and imperatives. Writing: job application, rules and regulations, notice.
:::

## 4.1 Cabbage White

### About the text

"Cabbage White" is a short humorous story adapted from *Happy Days and Short Stories* by the English-language teacher and writer Jake Allsop. Its title is the name of a common butterfly, *Pieris rapae* (the small white), whose caterpillars eat cabbage leaves. The tone is light and comic, but the story still carries a lesson about planning, patience and the way a hasty "clever idea" can wreck a job.

```photo id="eng10/cabbage-white" caption="A cabbage white (small white) butterfly, Pieris rapae. Its caterpillars are a pest of cabbage fields." width="62"
```

### What the story says

Sarah (12) and her brother Jamie (11) stand on tiptoe to hand their school card to a tall man at a farm counter. He frowns: they should be at school. They explain that they are on holiday and want to earn money to **support their parents**. The man says the work is hard and he needs somebody big and strong, but Sarah pleads, and he asks whether they know what a Cabbage White is. Sarah answers correctly: a white butterfly that lays eggs on cabbages, which become caterpillars that eat the leaves. Jamie shouts the last part. Since neither is afraid of caterpillars, he offers them a job: check every cabbage and **remove the caterpillars**, putting them in a pail. He will decide the pay after he sees the work.

In the cabbage patch they are overwhelmed. Jamie thinks there must be a million cabbages; Sarah calculates that with ten caterpillars on each there must be a **billion** caterpillars. Picking is slow and disgusting: the caterpillars wriggle and the first row takes ages. Meanwhile cabbage-white butterflies fill the air and seem to mock them — however many caterpillars the children kill, the butterflies can lay millions more eggs. The children become desperate.

Then Sarah has a "brilliant" idea, "as brilliant as Einstein coming up with E = mc²". Caterpillars come from eggs and eggs come from butterflies, so if they kill the **butterflies** there will be no more. She pulls up two bamboo bean-poles (killing two bean plants), gives one to Jamie, and they rush about swinging the poles. Butterflies are hard to hit in the air but easy to hit when they settle on cabbages — and it is impossible to hit a butterfly on a cabbage without hitting the cabbage. At last, exhausted, they look at their work. Few butterflies are left, and **hardly any cabbages**: the field looks like a battlefield. Without a word they put down the poles and **tiptoe away**. Jamie says, "He knows our names," and Sarah answers, "But he doesn't know where we live."

### Characters

| Character | Qualities | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Sarah | confident, quick, bossy, overconfident | Answers the man, leads the "plan", does the arithmetic |
| Jamie | younger, loyal, cheerful, less sure | Doubts her arithmetic "but knew better than to dispute"; follows her "game" |
| The farmer | strict, practical, fair-minded | Frowns at their age, but gives them a chance and promises fair pay |

**Verbs of walking** (the story uses *tiptoe*): **sneak** — to move quietly so as not to be noticed; **stroll** — to walk slowly for pleasure; **lurch** — to move unsteadily with sudden jerks; **stagger** — to walk unsteadily as if about to fall; **stride** — to walk with long, confident steps; **stumble** — to trip or lose balance while walking.

### Themes

- **Children and work.** The children want to help their family. The story is gentle about it but shows that real work needs patience and skill.
- **Shortcuts backfire.** The "brilliant idea" solves the wrong problem; the cabbages are the farmer's real wealth.
- **Humour from exaggeration.** A million cabbages and a billion caterpillars are comic overstatement (hyperbole).
- **Fear of consequences.** The last lines turn a funny story into a small lesson about responsibility.

## 4.2 Leave This Chanting and Singing

### About the poem and the poet

```photo id="eng10/tagore" caption="Rabindranath Tagore (1861–1941), photographed in 1909." width="60"
```

**Rabindranath Tagore** (1861–1941) was a poet, novelist, playwright, composer and painter from Bengal. He wrote *Gitanjali* ("Song Offerings"), and in **1913** he became the first non-European to win the **Nobel Prize in Literature**. He also wrote the words of the national anthems of India and Bangladesh. "Leave This Chanting and Singing" is poem number **11** in the English *Gitanjali* (1912), which Tagore translated himself from Bengali prose-poems.

### The poem

> Leave this chanting and singing and telling of beads!
> Whom dost thou worship in this lonely dark corner of a temple with doors all shut?
> Open thine eyes and see thy God is not before thee!
>
> He is there where the tiller is tilling the hard ground and where the path-maker is breaking stones.
> He is with them in sun and in shower, and his garment is covered with dust.
> Put off thy holy mantle and even like him come down on the dusty soil!
>
> Deliverance? Where is this deliverance to be found?
> Our master himself has joyfully taken upon him the bonds of creation; he is bound with us all for ever.
>
> Come out of thy meditations and leave aside thy flowers and incense!
> What harm is there if thy clothes become tattered and stained?
> Meet him and stand by him in toil and in sweat of thy brow.

### Meaning, stanza by stanza

1. **Stanza 1 — a sharp question.** The speaker tells a worshipper to stop *chanting, singing hymns and counting prayer beads* in a shut, dark corner of a temple. The question "Whom dost thou worship?" suggests that a God who is not in the world is not really being met. "Open thine eyes": God is not in front of you in that dark corner.
2. **Stanza 2 — where God is.** God is with the farmer ploughing hard ground and the road-builder breaking stones, in sun and rain, with dusty clothes. The worshipper must remove the "holy mantle" (the show of holiness) and join them on the dusty soil.
3. **Stanza 3 — deliverance.** People seek *deliverance* (release from suffering) by leaving the world. But the Master has *joyfully* taken the "bonds of creation" on himself: He is tied to us for ever. So freedom is found within the world, not outside it.
4. **Stanza 4 — the call.** Leave meditation, flowers and incense. Dirty or torn clothes do not matter. Meet God and stand with Him **in toil**, in "the sweat of thy brow".

### Central idea

**Honest labour is worship.** God is found among working people, not only in temples; true devotion means joining in the world's work. The poem does not insult prayer; it criticises empty ritual that separates a person from other people.

### Poetic devices

| Device | Example | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Imperative | "Leave…", "Open…", "Put off…", "Come out…" | The tone is urgent, like a friend shaking you awake |
| Rhetorical question | "Whom dost thou worship…?" "Where is this deliverance…?" | Makes the reader think and doubt empty ritual |
| Contrast | dark closed temple / open dusty fields | Closed religion against open life |
| Imagery | tiller, path-maker, dust, sweat | Strong pictures of manual labour |
| Archaic language | *dost, thou, thine, thy* | Gives a solemn, prayer-like tone |

### Vocabulary

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| beads | small pieces of glass or stone threaded together, used to count prayers |
| tilling | preparing and using land for growing crops |
| mantle | a loose outer garment, a cloak |
| deliverance | being rescued from danger, evil or pain; liberation |
| incense | a substance that gives off a sweet smell when burnt |
| stained | marked with dirt or colour that is hard to remove |
| toil | hard, tiring work |
| tattered | old and torn |

**Archaic words and their modern forms:** *dost* → do; *thou* → you; *thine* → your; *thy* → your; *thee* → you.

## 4.3 Grammar and writing tasks of the unit

### Grammar I: Modal verbs and their functions

**Modal verbs** (*can, could, may, might, must, should, would, will, shall*) are helping verbs that show *attitude*: ability, permission, possibility, advice or obligation. They have **no -s** in the third person and are followed by the **bare infinitive**: *She could swim* (not *She could to swim*).

| Modal | Main functions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| can | ability; permission; possibility | I can swim. Can I go out? |
| could | past ability; polite request; weaker possibility | Could you tell me the way to the airport? |
| may | permission; possibility | May I come in? It may rain. |
| might | weak possibility | He might be in his office. |
| must | strong obligation; strong certainty (deduction) | You must wear a helmet. She must be tired. |
| can't | impossibility (negative deduction) | This can't be Safal's coat; it is too small. |
| should | advice; what is proper | You should see this film. |
| would | polite offer or invitation; habit in the past | Would you like to stay with us? |
| have to / had to | external compulsion | I had to wait for an hour. |

#### Degrees of certainty (deduction)

| Certainty | Modal |
|---|---|
| 100 % sure it is true | must |
| 100 % sure it is not true | can't / couldn't |
| about 50 % sure | may / might / could |

```figure caption="Modal verbs of deduction arranged by degree of certainty."

    ax.text(x, 0.55, d, ha="center", va="top", fontsize=7.5, color=MUTED)
ax.set_xlim(0, 10); ax.set_ylim(-0.3, 2.3)
ax.axis("off")
```

::: caution Common mistakes
Do not write *He can swims*, *She musts go* or *I should to go*. *Must* has no past tense — use *had to* (*I had to wait*). *Mustn't* means *forbidden*; *don't have to* means *not necessary*.
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Fill in with a modal: "Sanam has been working in the field all day. She ____ be tired."

**Solution.** The speaker is almost certain. Choose **must**. *She must be tired.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Fill in with *must* or *can't*: "He is not a doctor; he has never studied medicine. He ____ be a doctor."

**Solution.** The speaker is sure it is not true. *He can't be a doctor.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** What does the modal show in "The poor Heron had to be content with a tiny snail"?

**Solution.** *Had to* shows compulsion — he had no choice.
:::

### Grammar II: Imperatives

An **imperative** gives a command, instruction, request, advice, warning or invitation. It uses the **base form** of the verb and usually has **no subject** (*Open the door.*). The negative uses **do not / don't** (*Don't run.*). Tagore's poem is full of imperatives: *Leave… Open… Put off… Come out…*.

| Function | Example |
|---|---|
| command / order | Stand up! Sit down. |
| request (with *please*) | Please help me with my homework. |
| instruction | Add two spoons of sugar and stir. |
| advice | Take care of your health. |
| warning | Mind your head! Don't touch the wire. |
| invitation | Come in and have some tea. |
| *let* forms | Let me help you. Let's go. |

#### Making imperatives negative

| Positive | Negative |
|---|---|
| Open the window. | Don't open the window. |
| Be quiet. | Don't be noisy. / Be not noisy (old style) |
| Let's play. | Let's not play. |
| Stand here. | Do not stand here. (more formal, for notices) |

### Writing I: A job application

A **job application** is a formal letter. Sender's address and date come at the top. The receiver's designation and address follow. The **subject** names the post. The body has three or four short paragraphs: (1) why you are writing and which advertisement you saw; (2) your qualifications and experience; (3) your skills and why you suit the post; (4) mention of enclosures, a request for an interview and thanks. End with *Yours faithfully/sincerely*, your signature and name.

::: example Model application
Bhanu-10, Tanahun
18 August

The Headteacher
Shree Janajagriti Secondary School
Bhanu-10, Tanahun

**Subject: Application for the post of School Secretary**

Dear Sir,

I am writing to apply for the post of School Secretary advertised in *The Tanahun Post*.

I am twenty years old. After passing my SLC, I worked for a year as an office secretary at Himalayan Investment Company Limited, where I managed correspondence, files and accounts. I am confident in both Nepali and English and use a computer well.

I enjoy working with students and teachers, and I am punctual, honest and hard-working. I believe I can be a valuable member of your staff.

I have enclosed copies of my certificates. I would be grateful if you would call me for an interview.

Yours faithfully,
Pabitra Nepali
:::

### Writing II: Rules and regulations

**Rules and regulations** tell people what they must, may and must not do in a place. Use a title, a numbered list, **imperatives** (*Keep silence*) and **modals** (*Visitors must…*, *Visitors should not…*). Make each rule short and clear.

::: example Model — Library rules
**Rules and Regulations of the School Library**

1. Keep silence in the library.
2. Do not bring food or drink inside.
3. Handle books carefully; do not write on or tear pages.
4. Students may borrow two books at a time for a maximum of seven days.
5. Return books on time; a fine of Rs 2 per day will be charged for late books.
6. Switch off mobile phones.
7. Put the books back on the shelves or on the reading table after use.
:::

::: example Model — Hospital rules for visitors
**Rules and Regulations for Visitors**

1. Visit only during the visiting hours (4 pm to 6 pm).
2. Only two visitors may see a patient at a time.
3. Do not bring children under ten into the wards.
4. Keep silence and switch phones to silent mode.
5. Do not touch medical equipment or patients' files.
6. Do not smoke or spit anywhere in the hospital.
7. Follow the instructions of doctors and nurses.
:::

## Chapter summary

- *Cabbage White* (adapted from Jake Allsop): Sarah and Jamie get farm work picking caterpillars off cabbages, then destroy the field while trying to kill the butterflies, and slip away.
- Themes: shortcuts and rash plans backfire; exaggeration makes humour; children want to help their parents.
- *Leave This Chanting and Singing* is poem 11 of Rabindranath Tagore's *Gitanjali* (Nobel Prize 1913): God is with the tiller and the path-maker, so honest toil is worship.
- Archaic forms: dost, thou, thine, thy, thee.
- Modals: *can, could, may, might* (ability, permission, possibility), *must / can't* (certainty), *should* (advice), *would* (offers), *have to / had to* (compulsion).
- Imperatives give commands, requests, advice, warnings and invitations; negative with *don't*; *let's not*.
- A job application: heading, subject naming the post, paragraphs on qualification, skills and enclosures, polite close. Rules use a numbered list, imperatives and modals.
