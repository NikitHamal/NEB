Ethics is the study of right and wrong; morality is how we behave in daily life because of it. This unit gives you two very different texts on the theme. "Wisdom of Little Girls" is a short, gentle story in which two children teach the adults of a village how to forgive. "World Record" is a comic drama in which a television show turns the idea of "record-breaking" into a joke. In grammar you will learn how to make **negative and affirmative sentences** and how to make a **verb agree with its subject**. In writing you will practise a **story with a moral**, a **message of condolence** and a **letter of sympathy**.

::: key What the examiner wants from this unit
Retell a story in your own words with a clear moral. Answer questions about characters in full sentences. In grammar, change a sentence from positive to negative (and back) and choose the correct verb form in tricky subjects such as *a number of*, *the number of*, *either … or* and *none*. In writing, use the right layout and a warm, polite tone for condolence and sympathy letters.
:::

## 11.1 Wisdom of Little Girls

### About the text and the author

This story is a version of a famous folk-style tale by the Russian writer **Leo Tolstoy** (1828–1910), whose own title in English is usually "Little Girls Wiser than Men" (written in the 1880s). Tolstoy is best known for the huge novels *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina*, but in his later life he also wrote very short, simple stories for peasant readers. These stories have plain language, very little description and a clear moral. Tolstoy believed that goodness, forgiveness and love of neighbour were the real basis of a good life. *(The textbook edition may adapt the original wording; the main events below follow the textbook.)*

### Summary

**Two little friends.** On a festival day, two village girls, Akoulya and Malasha, have been dressed in brand-new clothes. One wears a blue dress, the other a yellow printed one, and both have red scarves round their heads. They are on their way to church, and the clothes are the pride of the day, but the children cannot resist playing on the way. Their game begins in a narrow lane between two houses, where muddy water from the farmyards has formed a large puddle.

**The puddle.** Malasha, delighted by the water, is about to splash into it in her shoes and stockings. Akoulya warns her to take them off first, and they walk through the puddle toward each other. Malasha splashes Akoulya's dress by accident. Akoulya is furious and, forgetting her own advice, scolds her friend. Malasha begins to cry. Akoulya's mother and Malasha's mother hear the noise and come out. Each takes her own child's side, and the two women begin to abuse each other.

**From children's quarrel to street fight.** The quarrel grows. Neighbours come out; the husbands join in; soon the men are shouting and fighting in the street while a crowd gathers. Meanwhile the little girls are forgotten.

**The grandmother.** Akoulya's grandmother pushes into the crowd and talks to the men. She reminds them that it is a holiday, a time for joy and not for fighting, and asks them to think about what they are doing. Her calm and reasonable words slowly bring the crowd to its senses.

**The girls' wisdom.** While the grown-ups were quarrelling, the two girls had already forgotten the splash. They had gone off together and were playing a game, laughing as though nothing had happened. The men and women, seeing this, felt ashamed. They saw that the children were wiser than the adults, who had made a big quarrel out of a trivial matter.

### Characters

| Character | Qualities |
|---|---|
| Akoulya | A little girl, quick to anger but quicker to forgive; first to give advice, then to scold |
| Malasha | Playful and innocent; cries easily, forgives easily |
| Akoulya's grandmother | Wise, calm, respected; uses reason, not shouting |
| The mothers | Over-protective, quick to take sides; they turn a small thing into a large one |
| The men and neighbours | Hot-tempered; they copy the quarrel; in the end ashamed of themselves |

### Themes

**Forgiveness and innocence.** Children quarrel and make up within minutes. Adults hold grudges. The title calls the girls "wise" because they have the one skill adults often lack: letting go of anger.

**How quarrels grow.** The fight began with a few drops of mud. Each person who joins adds his own feelings and none of them remembers what it was about. The story is a small model of how families, villages and even nations drift into conflict.

**Peace-making through reason.** The grandmother does not shout or take sides. She appeals to the common feeling of the festival. In a moral story, the wise person is the one who calms others.

## 11.2 World Record

### About the text

"World Record" is a **drama** — a short play meant to be spoken or acted. It is a **comedy** set in a television studio, with a presenter, studio audience and contestants. In the story the contest is silly, and the humour comes from **irony**: people take a pointless contest very seriously. The text introduces a series of comic characters: Mabel Phillips, who tries to break a record for leaning on a brush; Albert Hargreaves, who tries to break the record for standing on one leg in a bucket of boiling soup; Albert's wife, who keeps the soup hot by topping it up; and a man who loses his trousers while taking a bath. The textbook does not name the playwright in the passages I could check; treat it as an unattributed comic sketch.

### Summary

The programme is a comedy show in which contestants try to break world records, however odd. Two contestants are performing today. **Mabel Phillips** is trying to set a record for leaning on a brush. **Albert Hargreaves** is standing on one leg in a bucket of scalding soup. His wife, Mrs Hargreaves, comes at intervals and pours in fresh boiling soup to keep it hot. Albert has now been standing for seven hours and two minutes; the world record is seven hours and three minutes, so only one minute remains. At this very moment, Mabel pushes him. The comic effect depends on timing: a minute from success, Albert's chance is gone. Other small stories in the show, including that of the man who took off his trousers for a bath and found them gone when he came out, add to the nonsense. The play makes fun of our hunger for fame and our habit of calling silly things "achievements".

| Verb | Noun | Adjective | Adverb |
|---|---|---|---|
| charm | charm | charming | charmingly |
| scorch | scorcher | scorching | scorchingly |

*sizzling* (making a hissing sound as it cooks; very hot) and *scorching* (burning hot) are close in meaning. **Irony** means saying or showing the opposite of what is expected, often for a funny or sad effect.

### Themes and ideas

**Humour and satire.** The play laughs at a society that rewards silly stunts with fame. A serious voice describing a foolish act is the main joke.

**The value of effort and the role of luck.** Albert endured seven hours of pain and loses by one minute. The audience laughs, but the moral side is real: record-breaking can make people take needless risks, and success can be ruined by one small thing.

**Ethics of entertainment.** Is it right to risk a person's health for a laugh? The drama invites us to think about what is truly worth celebrating.

## 11.3 Grammar and writing tasks

### Grammar I — Positive and negative sentences

An **affirmative** (positive) sentence says something is true. A **negative** sentence says something is not true. Changing between them is a common SEE grammar task.

| Tense / form | Affirmative | Negative |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present (be) | She is at home. | She is **not** at home. |
| Simple present (other verbs) | Ram **knows** the answer. | Ram **does not know** the answer. |
| Simple past | He **ate** many mangoes. | He **did not eat** many mangoes. |
| Present perfect | I have seen it. | I **have not** seen it. |
| Modal | They can help her. | They **cannot** help her. |
| Imperative | Pour much water. | **Do not** pour much water. |

**Rules**

1. With *do/does/did*, the main verb goes back to its base form: *did not eat*, not *did not ate*.
2. **Some → any**, **somebody → anybody**, **something → anything**, **always → never**, **already → yet**, **too → either** in negative sentences. *He has something in his bag* → *He does not have anything in his bag.*
3. **Everybody / all** → **nobody / not all**; **both** → **neither**; **either … or** → **neither … nor**.
4. Words such as *never, nobody, nothing, none, hardly, rarely* are already negative. Do not add *not*: *He never drinks* (not *he doesn't never drink*).
5. *All the students did not have to sit* is the negative of *All the students had to sit* (in the textbook sense *none had to sit*). *Didn't have to* means "was not required to".

::: example Worked example 11.1 — to negative and back
**Problem.** (a) Change to negative: "Ram visits me often." (b) Change to affirmative: "There is not anyone in the locality living in luxury."

**Solution.** (a) *Visits* needs *does*. *Often* becomes *rarely* or *not often*. Answer: *Ram does not visit me often* (or *Ram rarely visits me*). (b) *Not any one* ↔ *someone*. Answer: *There is someone in the locality living in luxury.*
:::

::: caution Double negatives
*I don't know nothing* means "I know something" in strict grammar. Write *I don't know anything* or *I know nothing*.
:::

### Grammar II — Subject–verb agreement

The verb must agree with its **subject** in number: a singular subject takes a singular verb; a plural subject takes a plural verb. Most mistakes occur with unusual subjects.

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| **A number of** + plural noun → plural verb | A number of soldiers **were** injured. |
| **The number of** + plural noun → singular verb | The number of deceased soldiers **is** not recorded. |
| Collective noun as one unit → singular | A pack of lions **is** approaching. The team **is** ready. |
| Words with two parts (pants, scissors, trousers, glasses) → plural | His pants **were** torn. |
| **The + adjective** meaning a group → plural | The truthful **are** trustworthy. |
| Infinitive or gerund as subject → singular | To cry **is** no solution. Walking **is** good exercise. |
| *Here / there* + verb: the verb agrees with the noun that follows | Here **lies** the tomb. There **are** two books. |
| **Either … or / neither … nor**: verb agrees with the nearer subject | Either she or her friends **are** responsible. Neither she nor I **am** getting lunch. |
| *Each, every, everyone, everything, nobody* → singular | Each of the dancers **twirls**. Everything **is** fine. |
| *None* → plural (or singular if "not one") | None **are** under the sun. |
| Subjects joined by *and* → plural | Ram and Hari **are** friends. |
| *Subject + with / together with / as well as* → verb agrees with the first subject | The teacher, with her students, **is** on the trip. |
| Quantity or amount as a whole → singular | Ten kilometres **is** a long walk. |

::: tip The "a number / the number" trick
*A number* behaves like *several* (plural). *The number* behaves like *the total* (singular). Replace the phrase mentally and the right verb is obvious.
:::

::: example Worked example 11.2 — choose the verb

**Solution.** With *either … or*, the verb agrees with the nearer subject, *paintings* (plural). Answer: *were*.
:::

::: caution Intervening words
In "The box of chocolates **is** on the table", the subject is *box*, not *chocolates*. Find the real subject and ignore the phrase between subject and verb.

### Writing I — A story with a moral

A **story** has a beginning (characters and setting), a middle (a problem and how it grows), an end (the result) and a **moral** — the lesson. Use the past tense and time-linkers: *once upon a time, one day, suddenly, after that, at last*. Keep it to one clear problem and give the characters names.

| Part | What to write |
|---|---|
| Opening | who, where, when |
| Problem | what goes wrong |
| Climax | the most exciting moment |
| Ending | how it is resolved |
| Moral | one-sentence lesson |

**Model story — The sparrow and the farmer.**

Once upon a time in a village in the hills of Gorkha lived an old farmer named Dhan Bahadur, who was hard-working and kind. One day, while working in the field, he found a small sparrow with a broken wing. He carried it home, bound its wing carefully and gave it food and water. In a few weeks the sparrow was strong again, and it stayed near his house, singing every morning.

One morning a flock of crows attacked the fresh seeds in his field. Dhan Bahadur shouted and waved his arms, but the crows would not go. Then the sparrow rose into the sky, chirping loudly. Other sparrows from the trees joined it, and together they drove the crows away. The farmer saved his crop.

From that day on he never forgot that a small kindness can come back in a great way.

**Moral:** No act of kindness, however small, is ever wasted.

### Writing II — Message of condolence

A **condolence message** expresses sorrow after a death. It is short, sincere and respectful. Do not describe the death in detail. Include: the sad news, praise of the person, sympathy with the family and a wish for peace.

**Task.** Write a message of condolence on the death of a senior folk singer and actor, using these clues: *passed away at his residence — incredible loss for the Nepali art sector — super-hit folk songs — acted in over a hundred movies — heartfelt condolences.*

**Model.**

*Heartfelt Condolences*

We are deeply saddened to learn that the senior folk singer and actor passed away at his residence yesterday. His death is an incredible loss for the Nepali art sector.

Through his super-hit folk songs he carried the voice of our villages into every home. He also acted in more than a hundred movies, and his warm presence on the screen will not be forgotten.

In this hour of grief we extend our heartfelt condolences to his family, friends and admirers. May his soul rest in peace, and may his art continue to inspire us.

*Principal and students, Sunrise Secondary School*

### Writing III — Letter of sympathy

A **sympathy letter** is written to a friend or relative who is ill, injured or has suffered a loss. Use the layout of an informal letter: address and date at top right, salutation (*Dear Ram,*), body, subscription (*Yours sincerely, / Your friend,*) and name.

**Task.** You and your friend Ram work in a factory. He broke his hand at work on 20 October 2022. Write a letter of sympathy.

**Model.**

> Birgunj
> 25 October 2022
>
> Dear Ram,
>
> I was very sorry to hear that a machine failure broke your hand on 20 October. I felt shocked when I heard the news.
>
> We have worked together for three years, and I have always known you as a regular and dutiful colleague. Accidents can happen to anyone, but it is hard to see it happen to a friend. I hope you are being well looked after in hospital and that the pain is easing.

> Please do not worry about your work. I shall try to cover your duties until you return. If you need anything from home or the market, tell me and I shall bring it.
>
> I wish you a speedy recovery. Take good rest, follow the doctor's advice and come back stronger.
>
> Your friend,
> Hari

::: tip Tone
Be gentle and practical. Avoid long philosophy. Offer one clear help. End with a wish for recovery.
:::

## Chapter summary

- "Wisdom of Little Girls" (a version of Tolstoy's tale): a splash in a puddle leads to a street fight; the girls forgive, the adults are ashamed; the grandmother calms the crowd. Moral: forgive and forget; avoid pride.
- "World Record" is a comic drama; Albert stands one-legged in hot soup for 7 h 2 min against a record of 7 h 3 min; Mabel pushes him. It satirises the craze for records.
- Negative: *do/does/did + base verb*, *not* after *be/have/modal*; some→any, always→never, either→neither.
- Agreement: *a number of* → plural; *the number of* → singular; *either/neither … or/nor* → nearer subject; *each, everyone* → singular; the + adjective → plural; gerund/infinitive subject → singular.
- A story has opening, problem, climax, ending and moral.
- A condolence message expresses sorrow, praises the person and wishes peace; a sympathy letter uses informal letter layout.

## Practice questions

**Reading comprehension**

> Once a poor boy found a purse with five hundred rupees on the road. He looked round, but nobody was near. He was hungry, and the money could have bought food for a month. Still, he took the purse to the police station. The owner, a shopkeeper, came the next day and, with tears in his eyes, thanked the boy and offered a reward. The boy refused at first but accepted a school bag, because he had none.

1. What did the boy find? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
2. Where did he take the purse? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
3. Write **True** or **False**: The boy kept the money. <span class="marks">[1]</span>
4. Why did the boy hesitate to take a reward? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. What did he accept in the end, and why? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. What does the story teach us? Write in about 50 words. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

::: note Answers
**1.** A purse with five hundred rupees. **2.** To the police station. **3.** False. **4.** He had only done his duty and thought honesty should not be paid for. **5.** A school bag, because he did not have one. **6.** Honesty is more valuable than money. The boy was poor and hungry yet did not take what belonged to someone else. His honesty was rewarded, and the story shows that doing right brings respect and peace of mind.
:::
