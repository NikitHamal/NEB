Every place has a story and every story has people in it. This unit moves from a deeply personal text about a Nepali writer's struggle to a lively factual text about one of the world's most famous mountains. "Me at the Beginning of Life" is an extract from the autobiography of **Jhamak Kumari Ghimire**, one of Nepal's best-loved writers. "Sugarloaf Mountain: 5 Interesting Facts" introduces a landmark in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil. In grammar you will learn **relative clauses** (*who, whom, whose, which, where, when*) and **prepositions**. In writing you will compose a **biography** and an **email or letter** inviting a foreign friend to a place in Nepal.

::: key What the examiner wants from this unit
From the first text: the writer's feelings, the people around her, and how she answers the unkindness of others. From the second: five clear facts about the mountain with correct figures. In grammar: relative pronouns that fit the noun they follow, and the right preposition. In writing: a biography built from notes (birth, education, works, contribution, awards), and a friendly invitation with interesting facts.
:::

## 15.1 Me at the Beginning of Life

### About the author and the text

**Jhamak Kumari Ghimire** was born on 4 July 1980 in Dhankuta in eastern Nepal. She was born with **cerebral palsy**, a condition that affects movement and speech, and she cannot speak clearly or use her hands. She writes with her **left foot**. She never went to school: she learned the alphabet by watching her younger sister study and by scratching the first letter, **ka**, in the dust with a stick. Though she was scolded and even mocked for trying to write, she kept practising until she could hold a pen between her toes. She became a poet, short-story writer, essayist and columnist. Her autobiography, *Jiwan Kada Ki Phool* ("Is Life a Thorn or a Flower?"), won the **Madan Puraskar**, Nepal's most famous literary prize, in 2010. Her poetry includes *Sankalpa* ("Vow").

"Me at the Beginning of Life" is an extract from the first part of that autobiography, written in the **first person** in Nepali and translated into English for your textbook. It is a short, honest, poetic account of her own birth and childhood.


### Summary

**Fate and the sixth day.** Jhamak begins with a Nepali belief: on the **sixth day after a baby's birth**, the goddess of fate draws the line of a person's destiny on the forehead. She feels that her own line was drawn cruelly. She was born unable to do anything by herself, and she describes her life as a **burden**, for herself and for her family.

**What people said.** Villagers blamed her. They said that her condition was "the fruit of the sins of her former life". She was hurt by this, but there was no one to answer for her. Her family was poor and sometimes had too little to eat, so she sees her childhood as full of hunger and difficulty rather than as a happy time.

**Her inner world.** As she grew, her mind awoke. She understood what was happening around her, but she had no voice to say it and no legs to walk. For her, awareness became a **curse**, because she could think and feel like anyone else but could not act. She compares herself with two famous people who overcame great disabilities: the Russian writer **Nikolai Ostrovsky** (1904–1936), who became blind and paralysed but dictated a famous novel, and the American **Helen Keller**, who was deaf and blind. They were different from her, she says, because before they died they had already written history, while she is only at the beginning of her life. The Greek poet **Homer**, traditionally said to have been blind, shows that physical weakness does not make a person incomplete.

**The grandmother.** Her grandmother cared for her, but the old woman has since passed away, and the writer cannot even please her with her voice. The thought of this loss adds to her sorrow.

**The message.** The extract ends with the writer determined to live and write. She does not define her life as a "beautiful flower of creation". She sees it as something to be shaped by effort, not by fortune.

### Themes

**Courage against disability.** Jhamak's account is not self-pity; it is a declaration that she will not be defined by her body. The passage shows how society's prejudice, such as "the fruit of former sins", does more harm than the disability itself.

**The power of words.** She has no voice, yet she becomes a writer. Writing is her way of speaking to the world.

**Family, poverty and love.** The grandmother's care and the hunger of the household show the family's love in a harsh setting.

**Society's attitude.** The text asks readers to treat disabled people with respect and give them education and opportunity.

## 15.2 Sugarloaf Mountain: 5 Interesting Facts

### About the text

This is a **tourist-style fact article**. It presents five short topics about a famous mountain in Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, each with its own heading. The words are simple, with lots of adjectives such as *spectacular, tranquil* and *bustling* to attract tourists. It is a good example of descriptive and informative writing, and you can use it as a model for a short report or a brochure.

### Summary: the five facts

**1. Awesome cable car ride.** Sugarloaf Mountain (*Pão de Açúcar* in Portuguese) is a granite peak about **396 metres** high at the entrance to **Guanabara Bay** in south-eastern Brazil. Two cable cars take visitors to the summit: the first goes to **Morro da Urca** (about 220 m), a smaller mountain, and the second goes on to the top of Sugarloaf. The cable car, opened in 1912, was one of the first in the world. From the summit one can see the whole city.

**2. Guanabara Bay.** The bay in front of the mountain is dotted with more than 130 islands and surrounded by beautiful natural scenery. It is historic because the colonial government built several **fortifications** (forts) there in the sixteenth century to defend the harbour.

**3. Rock climbing.** The mountain is a favourite for climbers: there are about **270 different routes**, so many people can climb at the same time. Beginners and experts can both find routes.

**4. Neighbouring mountains.** Next to Sugarloaf are other hills and peaks, such as Morro da Urca, which also has a play area that children enjoy, and, nearby, the Corcovado peak with the statue of Christ the Redeemer.

**5. Hollywood star.** The mountain has appeared in many films, or **blockbusters**. The first film to feature it was *Now, Voyager* (1942), and it was later seen in the James Bond film *Moonraker* (1979).

**Why "Sugarloaf"?** The name comes from the mountain's shape, which looks like the traditional cone-shaped loaf of refined sugar used in the sixteenth to nineteenth centuries.

## 15.3 Grammar and writing tasks

### Grammar I — Relative clauses

A **relative clause** gives more information about a noun. It begins with a **relative pronoun** or adverb.

| Word | Refers to | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **who** | people (subject) | I admired the player **who** scored the goal. |
| **whom** | people (object) | He is a liar **whom** you should not trust. |
| **whose** | possession (people or things) | I saw a soldier **whose** bravery impressed everyone. |
| **which** | things / animals | We had juice **which** was in the fridge. |
| **that** | people or things (defining clauses only) | The book **that** I bought is excellent. |
| **where** | place | This is the road **where** the accident took place. |
| **when** | time | It was in 2015 **when** the earthquake hit Nepal. |
| **with whom / in which …** | preposition + relative | You are the friend **with whom** I want to sit. |

```figure caption="Choosing the relative word."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
```

**Defining and non-defining clauses**

| | Defining | Non-defining |
|---|---|---|
| Job | tells **which** person or thing | adds **extra** information |
| Commas | none | commas on both sides |
| *that* allowed? | yes | **no** |
| Example | The girl **who won the race** is my sister. | My mother, **who is a farmer**, grows vegetables. |

**Rules to remember**

1. The relative clause comes **right after** the noun it describes: *The letter which my friend sent from abroad reached me this morning.*
2. **Whom** is used when the pronoun is the object; in modern use *who* is often accepted, but SEE keys use *whom* after a preposition (*with whom, to whom*).
3. In a defining clause, **who/which/that** can be dropped when it is the object: *The book (that) I bought.* It cannot be dropped as a subject.
4. Do not repeat the object: *The man whom I met him* is wrong. Write *The man whom I met.*
5. Joining two sentences: find the repeated noun, change it to the relative pronoun in the second sentence, and put the clause next to the noun.

::: example Worked example 15.1 — joining sentences
**Problem.** Join: "The boy committed a cybercrime. The judge sent him to jail."

**Solution.** The repeated noun is *the boy* (a person; in the second sentence it is the object *him*). Write: *The judge sent the boy who committed a cybercrime to jail.* (Here *who* is the subject of *committed*, so it is **who**, not **whom**.)
:::

::: example Worked example 15.2 — choose the pronoun
**Problem.** "I want to help those children ______ economic condition is very poor."

**Solution.** The condition *belongs to* the children, so we need **whose**: *… children whose economic condition is very poor.*
:::

::: caution Who / whom / whose
*Whose* shows ownership, *who* acts, *whom* receives the action. Test: if you can put *he/she* in the clause, use *who*; if *him/her*, use *whom*.
:::

### Grammar II — Prepositions

A **preposition** shows the relationship of a noun or pronoun to another word: time, place, direction, means, cause.

| Use | Preposition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Time: clock times, meals | **at** | at 9:15 pm, at noon, at night |
| Time: days and dates | **on** | on Friday, on 2 November |
| Time: months, years, seasons, parts of the day | **in** | in March, in 2002, in the morning |
| Time: duration | **for / during / since** | for two years, since 2070 |
| Place: point | **at** | at the bus stop, at a university |
| Place: enclosed area, town, country | **in** | in a city, in Nepal |
| Place: surface | **on** | on the table |
| Position | **across, beside, between, beneath, behind, opposite** | across the street |
| Movement | **to, from, into, out of, through, along, up, down** | walk up to the edge |
| Means | **by** (vehicle, agent), **with** (tool) | by plane, with a knife |
| Cause | **from** | died from injury |

**Dependent prepositions** (fixed combinations)

| Word | Preposition |
|---|---|
| afraid / scared / terrified | of |
| pleased / satisfied | with |
| good / bad / clever | at |
| interested | in |
| depend / rely | on |
| angry | with (a person), at/about (a thing) |
| die | of (disease), from (injury) |
| similar / different | to / from |
| responsible / famous | for |
| married / engaged | to |
| listen / belong | to |

::: example Worked example 15.3 — choose the preposition
**Problem.** "The bus leaves ______ 9:15 pm ______ Friday."

**Solution.** A clock time takes **at**; a day takes **on**. Answer: *at 9:15 pm on Friday.*
:::

::: caution In / on / at
*in the morning* but **at night**; *on Monday morning* (day + part of day takes **on**). *At the university* names the institution; *in the university* names the building.
:::

### Writing I — A biography

A **biography** tells the life story of a real person, written by someone else. It is written in the **third person** and mostly in the **past tense**. Use **notes** (birth, family, education, works, contribution, awards) and arrange them in order.

| Part | What to write |
|---|---|
| Title | the person's name or a phrase such as *A Life of Courage* |
| Introduction | who the person is; why he or she is famous |
| Early life | birth, family, education |
| Career | works, struggles, achievements |
| Contribution / awards | what he or she gave to the society; honours |
| Conclusion | the lesson or inspiration |

**Model biography — Jhamak Kumari Ghimire.**

Jhamak Kumari Ghimire is one of the most admired writers of Nepal. She was born on 4 July 1980 in Dhankuta, in a poor farming family. From birth she had cerebral palsy, which left her unable to speak clearly or use her hands. She never went to school. Instead she learned the alphabet by watching her younger sister study and by scratching the letter *ka* in the dust with a stick. Despite scolding and mockery from people who could not understand her, she taught herself to write with a pen held between the toes of her left foot.

Jhamak became a poet, short-story writer and essayist, and she also writes columns for a national newspaper. Her poetry collections include *Sankalpa*, and her autobiography *Jiwan Kada Ki Phool* ("Is Life a Thorn or a Flower?") won the Madan Puraskar, Nepal's most prestigious literary prize, in 2010. Her work has made readers rethink how society treats people with disabilities.

Jhamak's life shows that determination can overcome even the hardest circumstances, and she remains a source of inspiration to young Nepalis.

### Writing II — An email or letter inviting a foreign friend

An **email** (or **informal letter**) to a friend uses a **friendly, conversational** tone. An email has a **To, From, Subject** and a short greeting. A letter has an address, date, greeting, body and closing.

**Task.** Write an email to a foreign friend with interesting facts about a tourist destination in Nepal. Invite him or her to visit.

**Model email.**

**To:** emily.carter@example.com
**From:** anita.sharma@example.com
**Subject:** Come and see Pokhara with me!

Dear Emily,

I hope you are well. I am writing to invite you to visit Pokhara, the city I told you about, during your winter holiday.

Pokhara is one of the largest cities of Nepal and lies about 200 km west of Kathmandu by road, at roughly 820 metres above sea level. It sits on the shore of Phewa Lake, and on a clear morning you can see the snow-capped Annapurna range and the fish-tail peak of Machhapuchhre reflected in the water. You can go boating, try paragliding from Sarangkot or walk to the World Peace Pagoda. There are caves, waterfalls and a museum of mountaineering. The local food, especially momos and fresh lake fish, is delicious.

You can stay with my family, and my parents would love to cook *dal bhat* for you. October and November are the best months, with clear skies and pleasant weather. Please tell me the dates, and I shall arrange everything.

I can't wait to show you around!

With love,
Anita

::: tip Email checklist
Subject line, warm greeting, a reason for writing, 3–4 interesting facts, a clear invitation (when, where, who), warm closing.
:::

## Chapter summary

- "Me at the Beginning of Life": an extract from Jhamak Ghimire's autobiography *Jiwan Kada Ki Phool* (Madan Puraskar 2010). She writes with her left foot, was blamed by society, felt awareness a curse, and compares herself with Ostrovsky and Helen Keller. Themes: courage, the power of writing, social prejudice, family poverty.
- Sugarloaf Mountain: about 396 m high at the entrance to Guanabara Bay, Rio de Janeiro; two cable cars (via Morro da Urca); about 270 climbing routes; bay with 130+ islands and sixteenth-century forts; first film *Now, Voyager*.
- Relative pronouns: who (people), whom (object, after prepositions), whose (possession), which (things), that (defining), where (place), when (time). Defining clauses have no commas; non-defining have commas and cannot use *that*.
- Prepositions: at (clock, point), on (days, surfaces), in (months, years, enclosed areas); dependent prepositions (afraid of, pleased with, good at).
- A biography: third person, past tense, in order: introduction, early life, career, contribution and awards, conclusion.
- An email invitation: subject, friendly tone, facts, clear invitation.

## Practice questions

**Reading comprehension**

> Mahendra Gurung was born in a poor family in a village in Lamjung. His father died when he was ten, and he had to leave school to work as a porter. At night he studied by the light of an oil lamp. Years later, he passed the SLC examination and became a teacher in the same village school. He now runs a free evening class for children who cannot afford tuition. Many of his former students are doctors, engineers and teachers.

1. Where was Mahendra born? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
2. Why did he leave school? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
3. Write **True** or **False**: He now charges high fees. <span class="marks">[1]</span>
4. How did he study as a porter? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. What does he do for poor children now? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. What qualities of Mahendra do you admire? Write in about 50 words. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

::: note Answers
**1.** In a village in Lamjung. **2.** His father died and he had to work as a porter. **3.** False. **4.** He studied at night by the light of an oil lamp. **5.** He runs a free evening class for children who cannot pay for tuition. **6.** I admire his determination and generosity. Though he was poor and had to leave school, he did not give up but studied at night and became a teacher. Instead of forgetting his past, he now helps poor children to study free of charge, and many of his students have become successful.
:::
