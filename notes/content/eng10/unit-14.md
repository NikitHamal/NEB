To travel is to learn. A journey shows us places, people and customs that no book can fully describe, and it often surprises us. This unit has two readings about discovering things on the road. "Expect the Unexpected" is a lively travel account in which a visitor describes what amazed her in Nepal — Kathmandu, Chitwan and Pokhara. "Weathers" is a short poem by Thomas Hardy that sets two kinds of weather, a bright spring day and a wet winter day, side by side. In grammar you will study **question tags**, and in writing you will produce a **travelogue** and a **tourist brochure**.

::: key What the examiner wants from this unit
Facts about places and activities from the travel text, answered in full sentences. For the poem: the meaning of the two stanzas, the contrast between spring and winter, and what the weather stands for. In grammar: the correct question tag for any sentence, including the tricky ones. In writing: a travelogue with a personal voice and a brochure with every required heading.
:::

## 14.1 Expect the Unexpected

### About the text

"Expect the Unexpected" is a **travelogue** — a piece of writing in which a traveller tells what he or she saw, did and felt. It is written in the first person, in an easy, friendly style. The traveller is a visitor to Nepal and his surprise is the main theme: he came expecting mountains and found murals, smiles and ceremonies as well. The text is organised **place by place**, and each place gets its own paragraph or paragraphs: Kathmandu, Chitwan and Pokhara. The textbook does not name the author in the pages I could check. Read it as a model for your own travel writing.

### Summary

**Kathmandu: murals, smiles and the cycle of life.** The writer first notices the colourful **murals** painted on the walls of Kathmandu's streets, a sign that street art is on the rise. He is amazed by the smiling faces of the people, whatever their circumstances. He visits the **Durbar squares** and the **Pashupatinath temple**, both World Heritage Sites. At Pashupatinath he meets **sadhus** (holy men) who pose for photographs, and sees an **outdoor cremation** on the bank of the Bagmati river. This astonishes him, but he learns that Nepalis see death as an everyday part of life and not as something to hide. He enjoys cycling around the villages and walking about the town even at night, chatting with local people.

**Chitwan: wildlife and adventure.** Chitwan National Park, in the southern plains, offers wildlife, jeep safaris, canoeing and cycling. The writer recommends it to **physically active travellers** who like adventure and fresh air. He also hangs out with local people in the evening.

**Pokhara: lakes and mountains.** Pokhara gives the most beautiful scenery of his trip. Visitors can go **paragliding** and **trekking**, and can walk up to a hill such as **Sarangkot** to watch the sunrise over the Himalayan ranges. He enjoys boating, local food and the peaceful atmosphere.

**Leaving.** When the time comes to leave, the writer is so sad that he nearly cries. The message of the title is that one should expect the unexpected: a journey will give you more than you planned for.

## 14.2 Weathers

### The poet

**Thomas Hardy** (1840–1928) was an English novelist and poet born in Dorset, in the south-west of England. He wrote famous novels such as *Far from the Madding Crowd* (1874), *The Mayor of Casterbridge* (1886), *Tess of the d'Urbervilles* (1891) and *Jude the Obscure* (1895), set in an imaginary region he called **Wessex**. After the hostile reception of *Jude*, he gave up novels and turned entirely to poetry, publishing his first volume, *Wessex Poems*, in 1898. "Weathers" appeared in his collection *Late Lyrics and Earlier* (1922). Hardy's poems often look at ordinary country life, the seasons and the passing of time.

### The poem

The poem has **two stanzas of nine lines each**. Each stanza describes one kind of weather and ends with the same short refrain, "And so do I". The refrain tells us how the speaker feels: in the first stanza he enjoys the weather as the cuckoo does, and in the second he dislikes the weather as the shepherd does.

> This is the weather the cuckoo likes, / And so do I; …
> This is the weather the shepherd shuns, / And so do I; …

### Summary and explanation

**Stanza 1 — spring weather.** This is the weather that the cuckoo likes, and so does the poet: light showers falling on the pointed buds of the chestnut tree and young birds learning to fly. The little brown nightingale sings its best. People sit outdoors at the inn called *The Travellers' Rest*. Young girls come out in light summer dresses ("sprig-muslin"). Town-dwellers dream of travelling to the south and west of England. Everything is bright, active and hopeful.

**Stanza 2 — winter weather.** This is the weather the shepherd avoids, and so does the poet. The beech trees drip with rain and their leaves turn brown and dull (*duns*); the wind threshes (beats) them. Tides hidden in the hills throb with each wave. Meadow streams overflow. Drops of water hang in a row from the bars of the gate. The rooks, black birds, go home in families. Everything is dark, wet and uncomfortable.

### Theme and symbol

On the surface, the poem is a charming description of two kinds of weather. Underneath, "weathers" stands for **the bright and dark phases of human life**. Spring is happiness, youth and hope; winter is hardship and sorrow. Hardy accepts both: the refrain "And so do I" shows that he shares the likes and dislikes of ordinary creatures, the cuckoo and the shepherd. The poem celebrates the simple pleasures of an English spring and reminds us that each season, like each part of life, has its character.

## 14.3 Grammar and writing tasks

### Grammar I and II — Question tags

A **question tag** is a short question added to the end of a statement. It asks the listener to agree or confirm: *It is a lovely day, isn't it?* It is made of an **auxiliary verb + pronoun**.

**The main rule: opposite polarity.** A positive statement takes a negative tag; a negative statement takes a positive tag.

| Statement | Tag | Example |
|---|---|---|
| positive | negative | Ram is working, **isn't he**? |
| negative | positive | They don't need to come, **do they**? |


**Forming the tag, step by step**

1. Find the auxiliary verb in the statement (*is, are, was, have, can, will* …). Repeat it in the tag.
2. If there is no auxiliary, use **do / does / did** (present simple, past simple).
3. Reverse the polarity (add or remove *not*; use contractions: *isn't, don't, haven't*).
4. Use the **pronoun** that stands for the subject (*Ram → he, the girls → they, this → it, there → there*).

**Special cases**

| Sentence type | Tag | Example |
|---|---|---|
| *I am* | **aren't I?** | I am right, **aren't I**? |
| Imperative (command) | **will you? / won't you?** | Shut the door, **will you**? Don't forget, **will you**? |
| *Let's* | **shall we?** | Let's have some fun, **shall we**? |
| *Let us* (request) | **will you?** | Let us chat, **will you**? |
| Negative words (never, hardly, rarely, scarcely, seldom, nobody, nothing, none) make the statement negative | positive tag | He never came again, **did he**? Nobody knows, **do they**? |
| *Everyone, someone, nobody, everybody, none* | **they** | Everyone will be satisfied, **won't they**? |
| *Nothing, everything, something, this, that* | **it** | Nothing happened, **did it**? Everything is fine, **isn't it**? |
| *There is / there are* | **there** | There is a shop nearby, **isn't there**? |
| *Have to / has to / had to* | **do / does / did** | You have to go, **don't you**? |
| *Used to* | **didn't** | He used to smoke, **didn't he**? |
| *I think / I believe / I suppose* + clause | tag follows the *clause* | I think he's busy, **isn't he**? I don't think he'll come, **will he**? |
| *I'd rather* | **wouldn't I?** | I'd rather go, **wouldn't I**? |
| *I shan't* | **shall I?** | I shan't be there, **shall I**? |
| *Have* as a main verb (possession) | **haven't / don't** | He's got a voice, **hasn't he**? He has a bath daily, **doesn't he**? |

::: tip Hidden negatives
Words like *hardly, scarcely, barely, rarely, seldom, never, nobody, nothing* already make the sentence negative, so use a **positive** tag: *She can rarely come, can she?*
:::

::: example Worked example 14.1 — find the tag
**Problem.** Add a question tag: "Your phone didn't break down."

**Solution.** The statement is negative, so the tag is positive. The verb *break* has no auxiliary, so we use *do* in the past: *did*. The pronoun for *phone* is *it*. Answer: *Your phone didn't break down, did it?*
:::

::: example Worked example 14.2 — a tricky one
**Problem.** Add a tag: "I think he's busy."

**Solution.** The tag goes with the clause *he's busy* (*he is busy*), so it is *isn't he?* Answer: *I think he's busy, isn't he?*
:::

::: caution I am
*I am right, am not I?* and *I am right, amn't I?* are wrong. The correct tag is *aren't I?*
:::

### Writing I — A travelogue

A **travelogue** is a personal account of a journey. Write it in the **first person** and mostly in the **past tense**. Include sights, activities, people, food and your feelings. Keep a lively, descriptive style and give the piece a title.

| Part | What to write |
|---|---|
| Title | catchy, e.g. *A Weekend in the Hills of Ilam* |
| Opening | where, when, with whom, why you went |
| Journey | how you travelled; what you saw on the road |
| Main part | the main attractions and activities; people, food, culture |
| Feelings | what surprised or pleased you |
| Closing | return; what you learned; a wish to go again |

**Model travelogue — A Day at Phewa Lake.**

Last Dashain holiday, my parents, my sister and I travelled by bus from Kathmandu to Pokhara. The road ran beside the Trishuli and Marsyangdi rivers, and green hills rose on every side. We reached Lakeside in the late afternoon, and the first sight of Phewa Lake took my breath away. The still water mirrored the Machhapuchhre peak, and colourful boats lay along the shore.

Early next morning, we drove up to Sarangkot to watch the sunrise. It was cold, but when the sun rose behind the Annapurna range, the snow turned from pink to gold. I had never seen anything so beautiful. In the afternoon we rowed a boat to the island temple of Tal Barahi and later visited Devi's Fall and a cave. In the evening we ate momos and *dal bhat* in a small restaurant and talked with a friendly shopkeeper, who told us stories of the town.

I was surprised that so much beauty could be seen in a single day. We returned to Kathmandu tired, happy and full of memories. I hope to go there again, perhaps to try paragliding.

### Writing II — A brochure

A **brochure** is a short, folded leaflet that promotes a place, product or service. It uses **headings, short phrases and bullet points** instead of long paragraphs, and attractive, persuasive words (*breathtaking, peaceful, unforgettable*).

**Task.** Prepare a brochure of a tourist place in your locality, including: name, location, accessibility, major attractions, entry requirements and contact information.

**Model brochure.**

> **Welcome to Rara Lake — The Queen of Lakes**
>
> **Location:** Mugu district, Karnali Province, north-west Nepal, at about 2,990 m, inside Rara National Park.
>
> **How to get there:** Fly Kathmandu → Nepalgunj → Talcha airport (Jumla/Mugu side), then a short drive or walk. Trekking routes also run from Jumla.
>
> **Major attractions:**
> - Nepal's largest lake, with clear blue water
> - Forests of pine, spruce and juniper
> - Red panda, musk deer and many birds
> - Snow-capped peaks and the sunrise from the viewpoint
>
> **Entry requirements:** National park entry permit; Karnali trekking permit for some routes. Carry warm clothes.
>
> **Best season:** April–June and September–November.
>
> **Contact:** Rara National Park Office, Mugu. Phone: 0XX-XXXXXX. Email: info@example.com.
>
> *Come, breathe the clean air and see the lake that has no equal in Nepal!*

::: tip Brochure checklist
Catchy title, every heading asked for, short bullet points, persuasive adjectives, and a closing invitation to visit.
:::

## Chapter summary

- "Expect the Unexpected": a travelogue on Kathmandu (murals, smiles, Pashupatinath and Durbar squares as World Heritage Sites; Nepalis see death as everyday), Chitwan (wildlife, jeep safari, canoeing; for active travellers) and Pokhara (paragliding, trekking, Sarangkot sunrise).
- "Weathers" (Thomas Hardy, 1922): two nine-line stanzas with the refrain "And so do I". Spring weather (cuckoo, nightingale, chestnut showers, girls in sprig-muslin) is liked; winter weather (shepherd, brown beeches, overflowing streams, rooks going home) is shunned. **Reading comprehension**

> Lumbini, in the western Terai, is the birthplace of Lord Buddha and one of Nepal's World Heritage Sites. Pilgrims and tourists from many countries visit the Maya Devi Temple and the sacred garden every year. A pillar erected by Emperor Ashoka in 249 BCE still marks the spot. Monasteries built by different countries line the Monastery Zone, and a peaceful canal runs through the park. Visitors are asked to remove their shoes in the temple and to keep silence.

1. Where is Lumbini? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
2. Who erected the pillar? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
3. Write **True** or **False**: Visitors may wear shoes inside the temple. <span class="marks">[1]</span>
4. Why do people visit Lumbini? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. What is found in the Monastery Zone? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. In about 50 words, say why Lumbini is important. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

::: note Answers
**1.** In the western Terai of Nepal. **2.** Emperor Ashoka. **3.** False. **4.** It is the birthplace of Lord Buddha, so pilgrims come to pray and tourists to see the Maya Devi Temple and the sacred garden. **5.** Monasteries built by different countries. **6.** Lumbini is important because Lord Buddha was born there. It is a World Heritage Site, a place of pilgrimage for Buddhists of the world and a symbol of peace. It also brings tourists and income to Nepal.
:::
