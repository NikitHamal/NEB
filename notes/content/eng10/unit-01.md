Current affairs are the things happening in the world now that people are talking and worrying about — new technology, climate change, health, politics. This unit gives you two pieces of real, recent writing about two such issues. The first is a magazine feature about driverless cars. The second is an open letter in which a children's charity asks the Secretary-General of the United Nations to let young people have a say about climate change. Along the way you learn to report what other people say (reported speech: statements), to write a newspaper article and to write a formal letter to a local official.

::: key What the SEE paper asks from this unit
Expect (1) a reading passage on a current issue with true/false, multiple-choice and short-answer questions, (2) vocabulary-in-context questions, (3) a reported-speech transformation, and (4) a letter or a newspaper article in the writing section. Read the passage once for the idea, again for facts, and always answer from the text, not from your own opinion, unless the question says "Do you think…?".
:::

## 1.1 How Driverless Cars Will Change Our World

### About the text

"How Driverless Cars Will Change Our World" is a feature article by the journalist Jenny Cusack. It was published by the BBC Future website on 30 November 2021 as part of its *Change Agents* series, which looks at inventions that could make the planet "a better, healthier place to live". The textbook uses an adapted extract. The article is **expository** writing: it explains a subject, quotes experts, and gives facts and dates rather than telling a story.

```photo id="eng10/waymo" caption="A Waymo self-driving car on a public road in Mountain View, California. Roof sensors (cameras, radar and lidar) let the car 'see' its surroundings." width="80"
```

### What the text says

The article opens with a scene on a late night in Phoenix, Arizona. A car glides up to the kerb with nobody in the driver's seat. An arrival message pings on the waiting passenger's phone. The passenger gets in and a recorded voice says that the car is theirs, "with no one upfront". This is a **Waymo One robotaxi**, called with a phone app ten minutes earlier. The writer uses this scene to show that driverless technology is no longer science fiction.

Next she lists the promises of the technology. It could change commuting and long journeys, take people out of dangerous jobs, make industry more efficient and help build cleaner cities with less carbon dioxide. Above all it could make roads safer: the World Health Organization estimates that more than 1.3 million people die every year in road crashes. Camilla Fowler, head of automated transport at the UK's Transport Research Laboratory (TRL), says that automation could eventually give "safer roads and less fatalities".

But there are big hurdles. David Hynd, TRL's chief scientist for safety, points out that a driverless car should be "calm and serene", while human drivers around it may speed or break the rules, so the car must cope with them. There are also questions of law and regulation, the highway code, public trust, road design and, above all, **liability** — who pays when a driverless car has an accident? Richard Jinks of the software company Oxbotica says that insurers are working out how to deal with the change "from a person being responsible… to the vehicle doing that".

The article then looks ahead. The biggest task for the next two years is to make cars work safely in complicated, unpredictable human surroundings. At the **Mcity Test Facility** of the University of Michigan — the first purpose-built testing ground for self-driving vehicles, a small "town" with 16 acres of roads, signals, underpasses and building fronts — engineers test situations that would challenge even skilled drivers, such as children playing in the road or two cars merging at once. Researchers there also ask how cars can talk to each other while keeping data safe from hackers. Companies such as Waymo (USA) and AutoX (China, backed by Alibaba) are already running or planning robotaxi services; most existing driverless machines work in controlled places such as mines, warehouses and ports. Rio Tinto's mine in Western Australia runs the world's largest autonomous truck fleet, controlled from Perth. In the next five years, the writer says, most of this technology will stay "behind the scenes" — for example lorries travelling in close **platoons** on motorways to save fuel and reduce congestion.

### Themes and ideas

- **Technology is advancing fast but not finished.** The article balances promise (safety, clean cities) with problems (rule-breaking humans, law, trust).
- **Responsibility.** A car cannot be punished; somebody must be liable. This is a moral and legal question as much as an engineering one.
- **Safety first, step by step.** Companies roll out slowly because "safety takes time" (the Waymo executive's words). Experts prefer starting in controlled places and then scaling up.
- **Teamwork across subjects.** Mcity brings in psychologists and human–machine-interaction experts as well as engineers.

## 1.2 Open Letter to UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres

### About the text

This is an **open letter** — a letter addressed to one person but published so that the public can read it. It was written on 7 August 2020 by the children's charity **Save the Children** (with other child-rights groups) to **António Guterres**, the Secretary-General of the United Nations. The *subject line* tells you the issue: "Inclusion of children in the Youth Advisory Group on Climate Change". The letter is a model of formal, persuasive writing, so it is also a template for your own letters in the exam.

### What the text says

Paragraph 1 *congratulates* the Secretary-General. He had just named seven young climate leaders, aged 18 to 28, to a Youth Advisory Group that would advise him regularly on speeding up action against the climate crisis. The writers welcome the idea of bringing young people into decision-making and of getting "frank and fearless advice".

Paragraph 2 states the **disappointment**: the group does not include children "in all their diversity". The writers remind him that millions of children joined school strikes for climate action in 2019 and early 2020, showing "outrage and constructive solutions in equal measure". Children have acted as agents of change using schools, social media and street protest to spread global activism. Young people from the global North and South have spoken to political and business leaders, most recently at the UN Human Rights Council, claiming their right to a safe, clean, healthy and sustainable environment.

Paragraph 3 gives **evidence from children's own voices.** The letter quotes the young activist **Greta Thunberg** (2019): "We proved that it does not matter what you do and that no one is too small to make a difference." In 2020, children say the same. **Fabrizio**, a 16-year-old from Peru, told the writers in an online conversation, "The future of children is at stake now, tomorrow will be too late." Children like him fear that the rush for economic recovery after the pandemic will push climate promises, such as "net-zero" emissions, aside, and they demand a *green recovery*.

The final paragraph repeats the **request**: please make the group inclusive of children and adolescents under 18, respecting their right to be heard and to contribute their expertise directly.

### How the letter is built

| Part | Purpose | Example in the letter |
|---|---|---|
| Heading (recipient, address, date, subject) | Tells the reader who, where, when, why | "Subject: Inclusion of children in the Youth Advisory Group" |
| Salutation | Formal greeting | "Your Excellency," |
| Opening | Polite, positive start | Congratulations on the new group |
| Body | The problem, with evidence | School strikes, Greta Thunberg, Fabrizio |
| Request | The action wanted | Include children under 18 |
| Close | Polite end | "Once again, Secretary-General, we congratulate you…" |

### Vocabulary

| Word | Meaning | Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| tackle | to deal with a problem | Governments must tackle air pollution. |
| corporate | relating to a large company | The corporate office is in Dubai. |
| outrage | strong anger and shock | The decision caused public outrage. |
| catalyse | to cause or speed up change | Protests can catalyse action. |
| at stake | at risk of being lost | Our future is at stake. |
| net-zero | balancing greenhouse gases emitted with those removed | The country aims for net-zero by 2050. |
| expertise | special skill or knowledge | Her expertise in business helped the company. |
| scupper | to ruin or spoil (informal) | Heavy rain scuppered our picnic. |

### Themes

- **Children as citizens.** Young people are not only future adults; they have rights and ideas now.
- **Climate justice.** The letter speaks for children "from the global North to the global South".
- **Participation.** Being heard changes decisions; the writers ask for a seat at the table, not sympathy.
- **Formal persuasion.** Polite praise first, then a clear complaint, evidence and a precise request.

## 1.3 Grammar and writing tasks of the unit

### Grammar: Reported speech — statements

**Direct speech** gives the speaker's exact words in quotation marks. **Reported (indirect) speech** tells what was said, without the quotation marks, from the reporter's point of view.

> Direct: Khushi said, "I go to school every day by bus."
> Reported: Khushi said that she went to school every day by bus.

#### Step by step

1. Remove the quotation marks and put **that** (it may be left out) after the reporting verb.
2. Change the **pronouns** to fit the reporter's view (*I → she/he*, *we → they*, *you → me/him/them*).
3. If the reporting verb is in the **past** (*said, told*), shift the tense **one step back**.
4. Change **time and place words** if the time or place of reporting is different.
5. Use *said to + person* → *told + person*.

#### Backshift of tenses

| Direct speech | Reported speech |
|---|---|
| Simple present: *"I go."* | Simple past: *he went* |
| Present continuous: *"I am going."* | Past continuous: *he was going* |
| Present perfect: *"I have gone."* | Past perfect: *he had gone* |
| Simple past: *"I went."* | Past perfect (or stays simple past): *he had gone* |
| Will: *"I will go."* | Would: *he would go* |
| Can: *"I can go."* | Could: *he could go* |
| May: *"I may go."* | Might: *he might go* |
| Must / should / would / could / might | Usually do not change |

#### Changes in time and place words

| Direct | Reported |
|---|---|
| now | then / at that time |
| today | that day |
| tonight | that night |
| yesterday | the day before / the previous day |
| tomorrow | the next day / the following day |
| last week | the week before |
| next week | the following week |
| ago | before |
| this / these | that / those |
| here | there |

#### When the tense does not change

- The reporting verb is in the **present or future** (*says, will say*): *Susan says, "I will leave next week." → Susan says that she will leave the following week.*
- The statement is a **universal truth or habit that is still true**: *The teacher said, "The earth revolves round the sun." → The teacher said that the earth revolves round the sun.*
- The verb is already *would, could, should, might, must, ought to*.
- The statement is about something that is still true when reported: *My father said that the sun is very hot.*

::: caution Common mistakes
Do not write *She said me* (correct: *She told me* / *She said to me*). Do not keep a question mark or quotation marks in reported speech. Do not forget to change *my → his/her* and *this → that*.
:::

#### Reporting verbs

*Said* is only one choice. A good reporting verb shows the **function** of the sentence and often removes the need for *that*.

| Function | Verb | Example |
|---|---|---|
| giving information | informed, told, announced | The agent informed us that the bus left at six. |
| saying sorry | apologised (for) | Karma apologised for coming late. |
| promising | promised (to) | Prakriti promised to return my money by Saturday. |
| admitting | admitted | He admitted that he had broken the mirror. |
| advising | advised (+ person + to) | Pemba advised me to see a doctor. |
| warning | warned (+ person + not to) | The librarian warned me not to make a noise again. |
| offering | offered (to) | Raman offered to help me plant rice. |
| thanking | thanked (+ person + for) | Sonam thanked me for my help. |
| agreeing | agreed (to) | Anupam agreed to work hard. |

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Report: Tshering said, "I will surely meet you this Saturday."

**Solution.** (1) Pronouns: *I → he*, *you → me/us*. (2) *will → would*. (3) *this Saturday → that Saturday*. Result: *Tshering said that he would surely meet me that Saturday.* A shorter version using a function verb: *Tshering promised to meet me that Saturday.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Report: "I work from home these days," said Yanjal.

**Solution.** Present simple → past simple; *these* → *those*. *Yanjal said that he worked from home those days.*
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Report: Raima said, "We had to cross the river and go ahead."

**Solution.** The statement is already past simple; it may stay or move back to past perfect, and *we → they*. *Raima said that they had to cross the river and go ahead.*
:::

### Writing I: A newspaper article

A **newspaper article** gives information about an event or issue in a clear, factual style. It has a **headline**, a **by-line** (writer's name), an **introduction** that answers who/what/when/where/why, a **body** in short paragraphs and a **conclusion**. Use the present or past tense carefully, avoid *I* and use formal vocabulary.

**Task.** The use of AI is making life easier but also bringing new challenges. Write a newspaper article about the challenges brought by the advance of AI.

::: example Model article
**Artificial Intelligence: Great Power, Great Challenges**
*By a staff reporter*

Artificial intelligence (AI) is now part of daily life, from phone assistants to hospital scanners. However, experts warn that the technology brings serious challenges that societies must face.

The first challenge is **privacy and security**. AI systems learn from huge amounts of personal data, so a leak can expose the details of millions of people. Strong security rules are needed to protect this data.

The second is **fairness and ethics**. An AI system is only as good as the data it learns from. If the data is biased, its decisions about jobs, loans or schools can be unfair, yet there are few clear laws on who is responsible for such errors.

Third, many workers fear for their **jobs**. Machines can already do routine office and factory work, and people will need new skills to keep up.

Finally, AI is **expensive**. It needs powerful computers, reliable data and trained experts, which poor countries and small businesses often lack.

Specialists say the answer is not to stop AI but to guide it with careful laws, honest data and good education. If these steps are taken, AI can serve everyone safely.
:::

### Writing II: A letter to the Mayor

**Task.** Write a letter to the Mayor of your municipality requesting him/her to involve representatives of children in decisions about children's issues.

Layout of a formal letter: sender's address and date at the top right; the receiver's title and address at the left; salutation (*Dear Sir/Madam* or *Respected Sir/Madam*); a **subject line**; the body in three or four paragraphs (reason for writing, details, request); a polite close (*Yours faithfully / sincerely*); signature and name.

::: example Model letter
Tarkeshwar Municipality-5
Nuwakot
15 Baisakh 2082

The Mayor
Tarkeshwar Municipality Office
Nuwakot

**Subject: Request to include children's representatives in decision-making**

Respected Sir/Madam,

I am a student of Grade 10 at a local secondary school. I am writing on behalf of the students of our ward to request that children's representatives be included when the municipality makes decisions about children's issues.

Many decisions of the municipality directly affect us: the condition of school buildings, safe roads to school, playgrounds, libraries and drinking water. At present, no child is asked for an opinion, although we know best what problems we face every day. Children in other parts of the world have been heard in decisions on matters such as climate change, and their ideas have made the plans better.

Therefore, I request you to form a child committee with one boy and one girl representative from each ward. The committee could meet the municipal council every three months and present its suggestions. This would also teach us leadership and responsibility.

I hope you will consider our request positively. Thank you for your valuable time.

Yours faithfully,
Sunita Tamang
Grade 10, Student Representative
:::

::: tip Letter-writing marks
Examiners look for correct layout (address, date, subject, salutation, close), a clear purpose in the first paragraph, logical paragraphs and a polite, formal tone. Keep to about 150 words.
:::

## Chapter summary

- *How Driverless Cars Will Change Our World* (Jenny Cusack, BBC Future, 2021) explains that driverless cars could make roads safer and cities cleaner, but must still learn to cope with human drivers and settle legal liability.
- Key names: Camilla Fowler and David Hynd (TRL), Richard Jinks (Oxbotica), Mcity (University of Michigan), Waymo One (Phoenix, USA).
- The *Open Letter to UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres* (7 August 2020, Save the Children) asks that children under 18 be included in the UN Youth Advisory Group on climate change.
- The letter praises first, then complains, gives evidence (Greta Thunberg, Fabrizio of Peru) and ends with a clear request.
- Reported speech: remove quotation marks, change pronouns, step the tense back after a past reporting verb, change time and place words.
- Do not backshift when the reporting verb is in the present, or for universal truths.
- Function verbs (*apologised, promised, advised, warned, offered, thanked, admitted, informed*) make reports shorter and better.
- A newspaper article needs a headline, an introduction, short paragraphs and a conclusion; a formal letter needs a correct layout and a subject line.

**Short answer questions (2 marks each)**

1. Why does David Hynd say driverless cars face a challenge from human drivers? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
2. What did Fabrizio say about children's future? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
3. Change into reported speech: "I will leave for Pokhara next week," said Sanjay. <span class="marks">[2]</span>
4. Change into reported speech: The teacher said to the class, "Water boils at 100 °C." <span class="marks">[2]</span>

::: note Answers
**1.** Because humans may speed or break traffic rules, and a driverless car, which is built to be calm and rule-following, must still cope safely with them.
**2.** Fabrizio, a 16-year-old from Peru, said that the future of children is at stake now and that tomorrow would be too late.
**3.** Sanjay said that he would leave for Pokhara the following week.
**4.** The teacher told the class that water boils at 100 °C. (No backshift: a scientific fact.)
:::

**Long answer questions (4 marks each)**

1. Write a newspaper article of about 120 words on "Road Safety in Nepal". <span class="marks">[4]</span>
2. Write a letter to the editor suggesting that students should learn about climate change in school. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

::: note Answers
**1.** *Model outline:* headline (*Road Safety: Everyone's Duty*); introduction — accidents on Nepal's roads have risen; body — causes (speeding, poor roads, careless overtaking), results (deaths, injuries, cost), solutions (strict law enforcement, driver training, helmets and seat belts); conclusion — safety is a shared duty. Write in short paragraphs, factual tone.
**2.** *Model outline:* sender's address and date; the Editor, newspaper name; subject: "Teaching climate change in schools"; paragraph 1 — reason for writing; paragraph 2 — arguments (melting glaciers, floods and landslides in Nepal; students can change habits); paragraph 3 — request (a short climate lesson in every grade); polite close, signature.
:::
