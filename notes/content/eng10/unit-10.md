## 10.1 The First Olympic Games

### About the text

"The First Olympic Games" retells a Greek **myth** — a traditional story about gods, heroes and the origin of customs. It is not a history lesson. The real Games began at Olympia in southern Greece, traditionally dated to **776 BCE**, and were held in honour of Zeus, king of the gods. The myth in your book is one of the old stories that Greeks told to explain *why* the Games existed. It follows one family through three generations: Tantalus, his son Pelops, and Pelops's bride Hippodamia.

### Summary of the story

**Tantalus and his terrible crime.** Tantalus was half man and half god, a son of Zeus, and the gods let him sit at their table. But he wanted to test them. He cut up his own son Pelops, cooked him in a stew and served it at a feast, to see whether the gods could be tricked. The gods were not fooled. They were furious and punished Tantalus by sending him to the **underworld**, where he must stand in water up to his neck for ever.

**Pelops is brought back to life.** The gods felt sorry for the boy. They put the pieces of his body together and brought him back to life. He came back more handsome than before. He grew up and went to the land of Elis, hoping to marry Princess Hippodamia, the daughter of the king of Pisa.

**The dangerous race.** The king had made a cruel rule. Every man who wanted to marry his daughter had to race him in a chariot. If the suitor won, he would get the princess and the kingdom. If he lost, the king would cut off his head. The king's horses were a gift from the god of war and ran faster than lightning, so many young men had already died. When Pelops saw Hippodamia, he fell in love with her, and she was pleased with him at first sight. Pelops had a gift too: his horses, a present from the sea-god Poseidon, ran faster than the wind.

**The stable boy's trick.** Hippodamia did not want Pelops to die, so she asked the king's stable boy for help. The stable boy replaced the metal pins that held the wheels of the king's chariot with pins made of candle wax. During the race the wax melted, the wheels came off and the king fell from his chariot and died. Pelops had won.

**The feast and the Games.** Pelops became king and married Hippodamia. To honour the dead king, they held a great feast with races and contests. At this feast the new king ordered that such games be held every four years. This, says the myth, was the beginning of the Olympic Games. The text ends by saying that the Olympic torch is still lit from the rays of the sun, linking today's Games with the ancient ones.

### Themes and ideas

**Punishment for pride.** Tantalus tries to deceive the gods and is punished for ever. Myths often teach that no one, not even a child of Zeus, is above the law of heaven.

**Love and cleverness against cruelty.** The king's rule is unfair; Pelops and Hippodamia defeat it with love and a clever plan. Notice, however, that the win depends on a trick, so modern readers may ask whether it is right.

**Origin of customs.** Myths explain why things are as they are. This one explains why the Games take place every four years, and why a torch and a sacred flame are part of the ceremony.

## 10.2 Popularity of Different Sports in the World

### About the text

This is an **informative (expository) text**: it gives facts rather than telling a story. It compares several well-known sports — football, cricket, volleyball, basketball, rugby, golf, hockey, lawn tennis and table tennis — and explains why they are popular. Its tone is neutral and simple, and each paragraph is about one game. Read it as a collection of mini-profiles: *where it began, how it is played, who loves it, what is special about it.*

### Summary

People play sports for health, recreation and pride. Some games have been played for so long that nobody knows exactly where they began; others have a known inventor. The text points out that **football** and **basketball** are among the most widespread games because the equipment is cheap: a ball and a patch of ground are enough. Football is believed to have taken its modern form in England in the nineteenth century.

**Cricket** is a bat-and-ball game most popular in India, Pakistan and Australia, as well as in Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bangladesh and England. It is different from **baseball**, which uses a different shaped bat and ball and different rules for scoring runs. **Volleyball** is played by two teams of six on a court divided by a high net; each team tries to *volley* the ball over the net so that the other cannot return it. **Beach volleyball** differs mostly in the number of players. **Basketball** was invented by James Naismith, a Canadian teacher in the USA, in the late nineteenth century. **Rugby** is a rough contact sport, so injury is a risk. **Golf** is said to have got its name from the Dutch word for the club; the text names the Netherlands. **Hockey** and **volleyball** both have varieties according to where they are played (field hockey and ice hockey; indoor and beach volleyball).

## 10.3 Grammar and writing tasks

### Grammar I — Present perfect and present perfect continuous

Both tenses connect the **past with the present**. They answer the question "What is the situation now, and how did it come about?"

| | Present perfect | Present perfect continuous |
|---|---|---|
| Form | has/have + past participle (V3) | has/have + been + verb-ing |
| Focus | the **result**; the action is complete or its result matters now | the **activity**; how long it has been going on |
| Example | I **have finished** my homework. | I **have been doing** my homework for two hours. |
| Typical time words | already, yet, just, ever, never, so far, recently | for, since, all day, lately, how long |

```figure caption="Present perfect (result) and present perfect continuous (activity up to now)."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.0,2.6))
for y,lab,ex,kind in [(1.2,'Present perfect','I have lost my keys.','point'),(0.0,'Present perfect continuous','I have been looking for them since 8.','span')]:
    ax.text(0,y+0.5,lab+':  '+ex,fontsize=7.8,color=INK,ha='left')
    ax.plot([0,10],[y,y],color=INK,lw=1.2)
    ax.plot([8],[y],'o',color=ACCENT,ms=6)
    ax.text(8.2,y+0.14,'now',ha='left',fontsize=7.5,color=ACCENT)
    if kind=='point':
        ax.plot([4],[y],'s',color=INK,ms=6)
        ax.annotate('',xy=(7.8,y-0.14),xytext=(4.2,y-0.14),arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='->',color=MUTED,lw=0.9))
        ax.text(6,y-0.28,'result still true now',ha='center',va='top',fontsize=7,color=MUTED)
    else:
        ax.fill_between([3,8],[y-0.07]*2,[y+0.07]*2,color=ACCENT,alpha=0.45)
        ax.text(5.5,y-0.2,'activity continues up to now',ha='center',va='top',fontsize=7,color=MUTED)
ax.set_xlim(-0.2,10.2); ax.set_ylim(-0.7,2.0); ax.axis('off')
```

**Rules to remember**

1. Use *for* with a length of time (for two years) and *since* with a starting point (since 2020, since Monday).
2. Do not use a finished-time word (yesterday, in 2019, last week) with the present perfect. Say *I saw him yesterday*, not *I have seen him yesterday*.
3. Stative verbs (know, like, own, belong) are not used in the continuous form: *I have known him for years*, not *have been knowing*.
4. For a repeated or completed count, use the simple perfect: *She has written five letters*. For duration, use the continuous: *She has been writing letters all morning*.
5. The continuous often explains a present sign: *Your eyes are red. Have you been crying?*

::: example Worked example 10.1 — choosing the tense
**Problem.** Complete: "It ______ (rain) all night, and the roads ______ (flood)."

**Solution.** "All night" shows duration, so *has been raining*. The flooded roads are a present result, so *have flooded* (or *are flooded*). Answer: *It has been raining all night, and the roads have flooded.*
:::

::: caution Since / for confusion
*I have lived here since five years* is wrong. Five years is a length of time, so write *for five years*. *Since* needs a point: *since 2075 BS*.
:::

### Grammar II — The passive voice

In the **active voice** the subject does the action: *The farmers are ploughing the field.* In the **passive voice** the subject receives the action: *The field is being ploughed by the farmers.* We use the passive when the doer is unknown, unimportant or obvious, or when we want to stress what happened.

**Formula:** object of the active sentence → subject of the passive + **be (in the same tense)** + past participle (+ *by* + doer).

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Simple present | People speak English here. | English is spoken here. |
| Present continuous | They are building a bridge. | A bridge is being built. |
| Present perfect | The boys have eaten the biscuits. | The biscuits have been eaten by the boys. |
| Simple past | Someone stole my bicycle. | My bicycle was stolen. |
| Past continuous | They were repairing the road. | The road was being repaired. |
| Past perfect | She had drawn the picture. | The picture had been drawn. |
| Simple future | The teacher will help us. | We will be helped by the teacher. |
| Modals | You must answer all questions. | All questions must be answered. |

**Special cases**

- **Questions:** *Has he translated the book?* → *Has the book been translated by him?* Wh-questions: *Who wrote this?* → *By whom was this written?*
- **Imperatives:** *Close the door.* → *Let the door be closed.* (or *The door should be closed.*)
- **Two objects:** *She gave me a book.* → *I was given a book.* or *A book was given to me.*
- **Verb + -ing:** *I like people driving me home.* → *I like being driven home.*
- **Reporting verbs:** *People believe that he is honest.* → *It is believed that he is honest.*
- Pronouns change: I→me, we→us, he→him, she→her, they→them, and the reverse.

::: tip Four steps
1. Find the object; make it the new subject. 2. Note the tense of the active verb. 3. Write *be* in that tense + past participle. 4. Put the old subject after *by* (drop it if it is *someone, people, they*).
:::

::: example Worked example 10.2 — active to passive
**Problem.** Change into the passive: "Who might have stolen your bicycle?"

**Solution.** The tense frame is *might have + V3*. Passive: *might have been + V3*. The question word *who* becomes *by whom*. Answer: *By whom might your bicycle have been stolen?*
:::

### Writing I — The paragraph

A **paragraph** is a group of sentences about **one idea**. A good paragraph has three parts.

| Part | Job | Where |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic sentence** | states the main idea | usually first |
| **Supporting sentences** | give facts, reasons, examples, details | middle (4–6) |
| **Concluding sentence** | closes the paragraph or restates the idea in new words | last |

**Tips.** Keep to one idea. Use linking words (*first, also, moreover, as a result, finally*). Do not start a new topic halfway. Write 100–150 words unless told otherwise. The topic and concluding sentences should be related, but not identical.

**Model paragraph — My favourite sport.**

*(Topic)* My favourite sport is football, because it is simple, fast and full of teamwork. *(Supporting)* All we need is a ball and a field, so my friends and I play almost every evening after school. The game keeps us fit and teaches us to pass, to trust one another and to accept defeat. Moreover, matches on television, such as the World Cup, make us feel part of a huge world family. Some of my best friendships began on the playground, and the discipline I learn there helps me in my studies too. *(Concluding)* For these reasons, football is more than a game for me; it is a way of growing up happily.

### Writing II — The dialogue

A **dialogue** is a conversation between two or more people. Write the speakers' names (or *Me* and *Friend*) followed by a colon, and begin a new line for each turn. Use short sentences, contractions (*I'm, we'll*), question tags and reactions (*Really? That's great!*). Cover **every point** you are given, and give the conversation a natural beginning and ending.

**Task.** Write a dialogue between you and your friend about the upcoming Sports Week in your school.

**Model.**

Sunita: Hi, Anita! Have you heard? Our school Sports Week starts next Sunday.

Anita: Yes, I have! I've been waiting for it since last term. Which events are you taking part in?

Sunita: The 100 metre race and volleyball. And you?

Anita: I've put my name down for the relay and table tennis. I've been practising every morning.

Sunita: That's great. Who is the chief guest this year?

Anita: A national volleyball player, I think. She is going to give the prizes on the final day.

Sunita: Wonderful! Let's cheer each other on, whoever wins.

Anita: Of course. It isn't about winning only; it's about taking part and having fun.

Sunita: You're right. See you at the ground at seven, then!

Anita: See you!

## Chapter summary

- "The First Olympic Games" is a Greek myth: Tantalus offended the gods, was punished, Pelops was revived, won Hippodamia by a chariot race (the king's wheel pins were replaced by wax) and the games began at the feast.
- The ancient Games began at Olympia in 776 BCE; the modern Games began in Athens in 1896; the torch is lit from the sun's rays.
- "Popularity of Different Sports" is an informative text. Football and basketball need cheap equipment; cricket is popular in India, Pakistan and Australia; Naismith invented basketball; rugby is rough.
- Present perfect = have/has + V3 (result); present perfect continuous = have/has been + V-ing (duration). *For* + period, *since* + starting point.
- Passive = be (same tense) + V3; object becomes subject; *by* + doer; questions and modals keep their frame (*might have been done*).
- A paragraph has a topic sentence, supporting sentences and a concluding sentence.
- A dialogue uses speaker names, short natural turns and covers all the given points.
