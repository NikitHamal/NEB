Do you prefer the quiet of a village or the bustle of a big city? Is Paris better than London, or London better than Paris? These are questions of **comparison**, and comparison is the thread running through this unit. The first text, "London vs Paris", compares two of the world's greatest cities on size, transport, food, people and cost. The second, "The Country Mouse and the City Mouse", is a classic fable that compares town life and country life through two mice. In grammar you will learn **adjectives and adverbs** with their **comparative and superlative forms**, and **connectives of reason and contrast**. In writing you will compare two places and describe a village.

::: key What the examiner wants from this unit
From "London vs Paris": exact comparisons, each supported by a fact, and your own choice with reasons. From the fable: the sequence of events and the moral. In grammar: correct adjective/adverb forms (*quiet / quietly*, *better / best*) and the right connective (*because / because of / although / despite / however*). In writing: a comparison that is organised point by point, and a vivid description of a place.
:::

## 17.1 London vs Paris

### About the text

This is a **comparison article** of the type found in travel magazines. The writer sets two cities side by side and compares them under headings such as size, population, transport, tourist attractions, food, people and cost. It is written in plain, modern English with a few French and British slang words (*chugger, baguette, gastronome*). The aim is to help a tourist choose which city to visit.

### Summary of the comparison

**Size and population.** London is far bigger than Paris. The text says that London is almost **fifteen times larger** in area, and it has the larger population, while Paris is compact, dense and easy to cover on foot. If you want a less crowded city, the writer implies, you should choose Paris.

**Rivers and landmarks.** London stands on the **River Thames** and Paris on the **River Seine**. London is known for Big Ben, the Tower of London, Buckingham Palace and the London Eye; Paris is known for the Eiffel Tower, the Louvre, Notre-Dame and the Champs-Élysées.

**Transport.** London has a widespread transport network: the Underground (the Tube), buses and its famous black cabs. Paris has its Métro, trams and buses, and it is smaller, so many places are within walking distance. The writer says London taxis are easier to catch because of the regulated black cabs.

**Museums and culture.** London has about **240 museums**, many of them free, and is famous for theatre and its music. Paris is a showcase for art, fashion and architecture.

**Food.** Paris is a city of *gastronomes*, with more variety and better cuisine: cafés, bakeries selling *baguettes* and espresso bars where the French enjoy talking informally. London's food has improved and is now very varied, but Paris is better in the writer's view.

**People.** The French are very formal in greetings and kiss on the cheek, though the text says they enjoy talking informally in cafés. Londoners are described as polite but less easy to get along with than they seem; street fund-raisers (chuggers) are common.

**Weather and cost.** The weather in both cities is changeable and neither is a place for perfect weather all year round. The best time to visit London is spring (March to May) or early autumn (September). Paris is generally slightly cheaper than London for rooms and food, so the cost of living is not the same. In the year 2018 London was visited by more tourists than Paris.

## 17.2 The Country Mouse and the City Mouse

### About the text

This is a **fable**: a short story, usually about animals that behave like people, that ends with a **moral** (a lesson). The story is very old and is attributed to **Aesop**, the Greek storyteller said to have lived in the sixth century BCE. The Roman poet **Horace** also told it, and later writers, including children's authors, have retold it in many versions. The textbook version has simple language and humorous details such as the mice eating custard and trifle. The fable compares **simple country life** with **luxurious but dangerous town life**.

### Summary

**The country mouse.** A mouse lived happily in a snug hole in the country. She ate plain food (peas, grain and simple fare), but she was content and lived in peace.

**The visit.** One day her friend, the town mouse, came to pay a short visit. The country mouse offered him the best she had, but the town mouse looked at the plain food with a frown. He was not happy with what he was served. "How can you live like this?" he asked. "Come to town and see how I live. There you will have a grand meal every day."

**The invitation.** The country mouse was glad to go. She hoped for better food, so she went with her friend to the town.

**The feast.** The town mouse took her to a grand house and a dining-room where the remains of a splendid dinner still lay on the table: custard, trifle and many other delicious things. The country mouse had never seen such a feast, and she began to eat with delight.

**The danger.** Suddenly, cats, dogs and a maid burst in. The mice had to run and hide for their lives. When the danger had passed, they came out again, but the frightened country mouse could not enjoy her food. There was no peace for mice in the town.

**The return.** The country mouse said goodbye. She asked her friend to take her safely back to the country. "I prefer my plain food and quiet life to your grand dinners and your fear," she said.

**Moral.** *Better a crust of bread in peace than a feast in fear.* (A common version of Aesop's moral is "Better beans and bacon in peace than cakes and ale in fear.")

## 17.3 Grammar and writing tasks

### Grammar I — Adjectives and adverbs

An **adjective** describes a noun (*a quiet child*). An **adverb** describes a verb, an adjective or another adverb (*She spoke quietly; very quiet*).

| Adjective | Adverb | Example |
|---|---|---|
| quiet | quietly | The children were quiet. They sang quietly. |
| poor | poorly | A poor family. He performed poorly. |
| patient | patiently | A patient cook. She waited patiently. |
| loud | loudly (or loud) | The teacher spoke loudly. |
| soft | softly | She sang softly. |
| timid | timidly | The kitten looked timidly out. |

**Forming adverbs.** Add **-ly** to the adjective (*slow → slowly*). If the adjective ends in **-y**, change *y* to *i* (*happy → happily*). If it ends in **-le**, drop *e* (*gentle → gently*). Some adverbs are irregular: **good → well**; and some have the same form: **fast, hard, late, early** (*hard* ≠ *hardly*; *late* ≠ *lately*).

**Adjectives after linking verbs.** After *be, seem, look, feel, sound, taste, become*, use an **adjective**: *Ram is calm under pressure. The soup tastes good.*

**Comparison of adjectives and adverbs**

| Degree | Use | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Positive | equal comparison | **as … as** | A family car is not **as fast as** a sports car. |
| Comparative | two things | **-er than** / **more … than** | Metal is **heavier than** wood. |
| Superlative | three or more | **the -est** / **the most …** | Everest is **the highest** mountain. |

```figure caption="How to form the comparative: choose by the number of syllables."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.patches import FancyBboxPatch
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.0,2.4))
def b(x,y,w,t,c,fs=7.4):
    ax.add_patch(FancyBboxPatch((x,y),w,0.6,boxstyle='round,pad=0.02,rounding_size=0.08',fc=c,ec=INK,lw=0.8))
    ax.text(x+w/2,y+0.3,t,ha='center',va='center',fontsize=fs,color=INK)
b(1.9,1.8,2.3,'How many syllables?','#FFF3D6')
b(0,0.8,1.6,'one: add -er','#DCE8F8'); b(1.75,0.8,2.1,'two ending -y: -ier','#DCE8F8'); b(4.0,0.8,2.1,'long: more … than','#DCE8F8')
for x in (0.8,2.8,5.05): ax.annotate('',xy=(x,1.42),xytext=(3.05,1.78),arrowprops=dict(arrowstyle='->',color=MUTED,lw=0.9))
ax.text(0.8,0.6,'tall → taller\nbig → bigger',ha='center',va='top',fontsize=6.9,color=ACCENT,linespacing=1.3)
ax.text(2.8,0.6,'easy → easier\nbusy → busier',ha='center',va='top',fontsize=6.9,color=ACCENT,linespacing=1.3)
ax.text(5.05,0.6,'beautiful →\nmore beautiful',ha='center',va='top',fontsize=6.9,color=ACCENT,linespacing=1.3)
ax.set_xlim(-0.2,6.3); ax.set_ylim(-0.2,2.5); ax.axis('off')
```

| Rule | Positive | Comparative | Superlative |
|---|---|---|---|
| One syllable: add **-er / -est** | tall | taller | tallest |
| One syllable ending in single vowel + consonant: double the consonant | big | bigger | biggest |
| Ending in -e: add **-r / -st** | wise | wiser | wisest |
| Two syllables ending in -y: **-ier / -iest** | happy | happier | happiest |
| Most other adjectives of two or more syllables | beautiful | more beautiful | most beautiful |
| Adverbs ending in -ly | quickly | more quickly | most quickly |
| **Irregular** | good / well | better | best |
| | bad / badly | worse | worst |
| | far | farther / further | farthest / furthest |
| | much / many | more | most |
| | little | less | least |

**Rules to remember**

1. Use **than** with the comparative and **the** with the superlative.
2. Do not use a double comparative: *more better* and *most easiest* are wrong.
3. **as … as** shows equality; **not as … as** shows inequality. *Wood is not as heavy as iron* = *Iron is heavier than wood.*
4. Use **much / far / a lot** to strengthen a comparative: *much more complicated*. Use **very** with the positive: *very quick*.
5. The same-person comparison uses the right case: *He ran more quickly than I (did).*

::: example Worked example 17.1 — adjective or adverb?
**Problem.** "The cook ______ (patient) removed the boiling pot."

**Solution.** *Removed* is a verb, so it needs an adverb: *patiently*.
:::

::: example Worked example 17.2 — comparison
**Problem.** "Sanskrit is ______ (complicated) to deal with than Nepali."

**Solution.** *Complicated* has three syllables, so use *more*, and *than* follows: *much more complicated.*
:::

::: caution Hard / hardly, late / lately
*He worked hard* (with effort). *He hardly worked* (almost not at all). *She arrived late* (not on time). *I have not seen her lately* (recently).
:::

### Grammar II — Connectives of reason and contrast

Unit 13 covered the connectives of contrast. This unit adds **reason** and compares the two groups.

| Connective | Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **because** | + full clause | I took a taxi **because** it was raining. |
| **because of** | + noun / noun phrase | All flights were cancelled **because of** the fog. |
| **since / as** | + clause (reason, formal) | **Since** it was late, we left. |
| **although / though** | + clause (contrast) | **Although** it was summer, the days were cold. |
| **despite / in spite of** | + noun / -ing | **Despite** feeling unwell, she went to school. |
| **however** | separate sentence + comma | A stone struck the man's head. **However**, he was not hurt. |
| **but** | joins two clauses | I like popcorn **but** I don't like pizza. |

::: tip Reason or contrast?
Ask: does the second part **explain** the first (reason: *because*) or **surprise** us (contrast: *although*)? *She stayed at home because she was ill* (reason). *She went out although she was ill* (surprise).
:::

::: example Worked example 17.3 — because or because of?
**Problem.** "All flights were cancelled ______ the fog."

**Solution.** *Fog* is a noun, so *because of*. If a clause followed (*because it was foggy*), we would use *because*.
:::

### Writing I — Comparing two places

A **comparison essay** shows the similarities and differences between two things. There are two ways to organise it.

| Method | How it works |
|---|---|
| **Block** | Describe place A fully, then place B fully, then conclude |
| **Point-by-point** | Compare size, then climate, then facilities, and so on |

Useful words: *similarly, likewise, both, whereas, while, on the other hand, in contrast, unlike, more … than, as … as*.

**Task.** Write a couple of paragraphs comparing Kathmandu with another city of Nepal (Pokhara).

**Model.**

Kathmandu and Pokhara are two of the most popular cities of Nepal, but they offer very different experiences. Kathmandu, the capital, is bigger, busier and older. It is the centre of government and business, and its Durbar Squares and temples attract visitors from all over the world. Pokhara, in contrast, is smaller, calmer and greener. It lies beside Phewa Lake with the Annapurna range behind it, and most people come there to relax, boat or trek.

In terms of facilities, Kathmandu has more hospitals, colleges and shopping centres, whereas Pokhara's strength is its natural beauty and adventure sports such as paragliding. Kathmandu is more crowded and polluted; Pokhara has cleaner air and wider streets. On the other hand, living costs are higher in Kathmandu. Both cities are rich in culture and food, and both are worth visiting. A traveller who likes history and bustle should choose Kathmandu, while one who prefers peace and views will love Pokhara.

### Writing II — Describing a village

A **description** paints a picture in words. Cover **location, scenery, people, buildings, occupation, culture and your feelings**. Use the present tense for a place as it is now, or past tense if it is a memory. Use vivid adjectives and comparisons.

**Task.** Describe a village using: *located in a district — gateway to a sacred temple — gorgeous landscape — farmland — traditional houses — mountain views — humble villagers.*

**Model description.**

**Khokana: A Village of Mustard and Memory**

Khokana is a small village in Lalitpur district, a few kilometres south of Kathmandu. It is a gateway to the sacred temple of Rudrayani, and every year people from all over the valley come here for its festivals. The village is surrounded by a gorgeous landscape of golden mustard fields and green terraces, and on clear days snow peaks shine in the north.

The houses are built of red brick with carved wooden windows and have stood for hundreds of years. Narrow lanes run between them and people sit in the sun to peel garlic or spin wool. The villagers are humble and hospitable, and they still press mustard oil in the traditional way. The village has a quiet beauty that no big city can match, and visitors always leave with warm memories.

## Chapter summary

- "London vs Paris": London is almost fifteen times larger, with a widespread transport network and about 240 museums; Paris is compact, better in food, slightly cheaper; rivers: Thames and Seine; best time for London is spring and early autumn.
- "The Country Mouse and the City Mouse" is a fable of contentment: plain food in peace is better than a feast in fear. Moral: peace is worth more than luxury.
- Adverbs: add -ly (poor → poorly); adjectives follow linking verbs; irregular: good → well.
- Comparison: -er/-est, more/most, irregular better/best, worse/worst; *as … as*; *than*.
- Connectives: because + clause, because of + noun, although + clause, despite + noun/-ing, however + comma, but.
- A comparison essay: block or point-by-point with linking words. A description of a village covers location, scenery, people, buildings and feelings.

1. Which is the capital of Nepal? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
2. Where does Pokhara lie? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
3. Write **True** or **False**: Rooms are cheaper in Kathmandu. <span class="marks">[1]</span>
4. Why should a visitor who loves history go to Kathmandu? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. What is the difference between the two cities? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. Which would you choose and why? Write in about 50 words. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

::: note Answers
**1.** Kathmandu. **2.** By a lake, with snow mountains behind. **3.** False. **4.** Because Kathmandu has many old temples and palaces. **5.** Kathmandu is bigger, noisier and more historical; Pokhara is calmer and more scenic. **6.** I would choose Pokhara because I enjoy quiet places and natural beauty. I would like to boat on the lake and look at the snow mountains. Rooms are also cheaper there, so I could stay longer.
:::
