Billions of people now shop, study, talk and play online. This unit looks at the risks. A comic short story shows what can happen when a teenager hides her identity from a worried father, and an information text gives practical advice for staying safe on the internet. In grammar you will study **advice and obligation** (*should, ought to, must*) and **articles**; in writing you will compose a **reply email** and a **newspaper article**.

::: key What the SEE paper asks from this unit
Reading: vocabulary from definitions, sequencing events, short answers on character and misunderstanding; matching paragraph headings, true/false, short answers on safety measures. Grammar: *should / ought to / must* for advice, moral obligation and compulsion; *a, an, the* and zero article. Writing: email, newspaper article, informal letter.
:::

## 7.1 Parents

### About the text

"Parents" is a short, humorous story about the generation gap and the dangers of online friendship. It is told in the first person by a father to his friend Dhruva, and it ends with a misunderstanding that is funny for the listener and a little embarrassing for the father. The textbook uses it to raise a serious question: what happens when young people pretend to be someone else online?

### What the story says

Anuja, the narrator's teenage daughter, has made a new friend on the internet — an "e-friend" — and one day tells her father that she wants to meet her. The father is worried. He insists on going with her to be sure the friend is genuine and that his daughter will be safe. Anuja protests: she calls her father *obsessed* and says he sees danger lurking in every corner. Underneath, she is embarrassed — he is middle-aged, bald, with a big belly, and she does not want her friend to see him.

At home, however, a shock awaits the father. Anuja confesses a secret: she has been chatting with her friend **under the identity of a boy**. She never told the other girl she was a girl. That explains why she is dressed like a boy for the meeting.

They reach the meeting place and wait, but no one comes. A car with tinted windows stands on the opposite side of the road for some minutes, then drives off quickly. After ten minutes the father decides that the girl was perhaps fake or had not been serious, and they go home without meeting her.

Later he tells the whole incident to his friend Dhruva. Dhruva bursts out laughing and explains what happened. The e-friend and her mother **had** come, and they were sitting in the car. The mother, seeing a middle-aged, bald, big-bellied man hanging around the meeting place — Anuja's father, looking at the cars and the people — took him for a suspicious man with bad intentions and quickly drove her daughter away. Neither side could talk to the other because each had the wrong idea of the other.

### Order of events

1. Anuja tells her father that she wants to meet her new friend.
2. She tells him about her e-friend.
3. Her father goes with her to the meeting.
4. He notices that Anuja looks like a boy in her new outfit.
5. She reveals her secret e-identity.
6. They see a car across the road.
7. They wait for ten minutes.
8. The father tells Dhruva about the incident.

### Themes

- **Generation gap.** Parents worry; teenagers want independence.
- **Online identity and safety.** Faking identity makes trust hard — Anuja hid that she was a girl, and the other family also acted from fear.
- **Misunderstanding and irony.** The father fears the stranger, and the stranger's mother fears the father. The humour is that both are protective parents.
- **Parental love.** Behind his embarrassing behaviour is real concern.

## 7.2 Cyber Security: Basic Internet Safety Tips

### About the text

This is an **informative / instructional text** of the kind found on technology websites. It begins by pointing out that we protect our physical property with locks and safes, but that our digital belongings — photos, messages, bank details — need protection too. Then it gives eight headed pieces of advice, each in its own paragraph. The headings let you scan the text quickly, a skill often tested in exams.

### The eight pieces of advice

| Heading | Main advice |
|---|---|
| Protect your personal information with strong passwords | Use long passwords that mix letters, numbers and symbols; use a different password for every account; never share them; do not let the computer remember them on shared machines; store them securely |
| Keep your personal data safe | Be careful where you enter financial details; read the terms and conditions; use a chip debit card (safer than a magnetic-strip card); do not enter financial data on unsafe sites |
| Make sure your devices are secure | Lock screens, use up-to-date security software and be careful with unknown downloads |
| Pay attention to software updates | Update promptly or enable automatic updates because updates fix security holes |
| Be careful about Wi-Fi | Public Wi-Fi may be watched; avoid banking on open networks; use a secured network |
| Set up two-factor authentication | A second proof (a code sent to your phone) protects the account even if your password is stolen |
| Keep personal information private | Do not share your address, school, phone number or location carelessly on social media |
| Back up your personal data | Keep copies of important files so that you can recover them if a device is lost or attacked |

### Common threats explained

| Threat | What it is | Defence |
|---|---|---|
| Phishing | Fake emails or messages that trick you into giving a password or bank details | Never click links from strangers; check the sender |
| Malware | Harmful software (viruses, spyware, ransomware) | Security software; avoid pirated programs |
| Identity theft | Someone uses your personal information to pretend to be you | Strong passwords; keep information private |
| Cyberbullying | Harassing or humiliating someone online | Block, report to the platform, tell an adult |
| Online fraud | Cheating people out of money through fake offers | If it sounds too good to be true, it is |

In Nepal, cybercrime is covered by the **Electronic Transactions Act, 2063 (2008)** and the Nepal Police has a **Cyber Bureau** that investigates online crimes.

## 7.3 Grammar and writing tasks of the unit

### Grammar I: *should*, *ought to* and *must*

| Modal | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **should** | advice; what is a good idea | You should see a doctor. |
| **ought to** | advice or moral duty (like *should*, a little more formal) | Children ought to respect their parents. |
| **must** | compulsion (a strong rule) or strong moral duty | You must obey the traffic rules. |
| **should not / ought not to** | advice against | You shouldn't eat so much sugar. |
| **must not** | prohibition | You must not use a phone while driving. |
| **have to** | compulsion from outside | I have to wear a uniform. |

- **Advice:** *You should raise your voice against discrimination.*
- **Moral obligation:** *Every child should take good care of ageing parents.*
- **Compulsion:** *You must abide by traffic rules while driving.*

::: caution Common mistakes
*Mustn't* means "it is forbidden", but *don't have to* means "it is not necessary". Do not write *should to*, *musts*, *ought go* (correct: *ought to go*).
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Fill in with *should*, *ought to* or *must*: "Someone picked my pocket." — "You ____ immediately inform the police."

**Solution.** A strong, urgent duty → **must**.
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Say whether the modal shows advice, moral obligation or compulsion: "You must abide by traffic rules while driving."

**Solution.** It is a rule with a penalty → **compulsion**.
:::

### Grammar II: Articles — *a*, *an*, *the* and no article

| Article | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **a** | before a singular countable noun that *begins with a consonant sound*, mentioned for the first time or in general | a doctor, a university, a one-rupee coin |
| **an** | before a singular countable noun that *begins with a vowel sound* | an actor, an hour, an MBBS doctor |
| **the** | for a specific thing known to both speaker and listener; things that are unique; superlatives; rivers, oceans, mountain ranges, nations in plural form; the second time you mention something | the sun, the Himalayas, the Bagmati, the best student |
| no article (zero) | plural and uncountable nouns in general; names of people, most countries and cities, languages; meals; subjects | Water is life. Nepal is beautiful. She studies English. |

**Remember: the sound, not the letter.** *an hour* (silent h); *a university* (starts with "yoo"); *an honest man*; *a European*.

::: caution Common mistakes
Do not write *a information* or *an advices* (uncountable: *some information*). Do not use *the* before the names of most countries (*the Nepal* ✘) — except *the USA, the UK, the Netherlands*. Do not drop the article before singular countable nouns: *He is doctor* ✘ → *He is a doctor*.
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** Fill in a/an/the: "Would you like to be ____ actor in the future?"

**Solution.** *Actor* starts with a vowel sound → **an**.
:::

::: example Worked example
**Problem.** "I saw a boy in the street. ____ boy was crying." Which article?

**Solution.** Second mention, specific → **The**.
:::

### Writing I: Emails

An email follows the layout: **To / Cc / Subject**, salutation, body, closing, name. *Informal* emails to friends use contractions and a warm tone; *formal* emails use full forms and polite expressions. A **reply email** answers the earlier message directly.

**Task.** Imagine you are Niti. You could not attend your friend Sambridhi's brother's wedding because of urgent family business. Write a reply email apologising.

::: example Model email
**To:** sambridhi@example.com
**Subject:** Sorry — I can't come to the wedding

Dear Sambridhi,

Thank you very much for the invitation to your brother Naman's wedding. I was really looking forward to celebrating with you and your family.

Unfortunately, an urgent family matter has come up, and I have to travel to Pokhara on the day of the wedding. I am very sorry that I cannot be there. I know how much this day means to you.

Please give Naman and his wife my warmest congratulations and best wishes for a happy married life. I will call you as soon as I return, and I would love to see the wedding photographs.

With love,
Niti
:::

### Writing II: A newspaper article

**Task.** Write a newspaper article on "Internet and Cyber Security" using these guidelines: introduction to the internet and technology; use of the internet in daily life; abuse of the internet; the need and ways of data privacy; conclusion.

::: example Model article
**Internet and Cyber Security: Protecting Our Digital Lives**

The internet has become as important as electricity. From online classes to digital wallets, technology has made life faster and easier for millions of Nepalis.

Students search for study materials, shopkeepers receive payments by QR codes, and families in Nepal talk to relatives abroad on video calls. Government services such as passport and licence applications are also moving online.

However, the same internet can be abused. Criminals send fake messages to steal bank details, hackers break into accounts, and some people spread rumours or bully others on social media. Many young users share personal information without thinking of the risks.

Therefore, data privacy is essential. Users should create strong passwords, use two-factor authentication, update their software, avoid unknown links and keep personal details private. Parents and teachers should teach children safe online habits, and the government should enforce cybercrime laws.

In short, the internet is a powerful tool, but we must use it carefully. A safe digital world needs careful users as well as strong laws.
:::

## Chapter summary

- *Parents*: a father's anxiety, a daughter's secret online identity and a comic misunderstanding in which a mother thinks the father is a suspicious stranger. Themes: generation gap, online safety, irony.
- Words: lurk, obsessed, outfit, swerve, munch, leer, stammer, window shopping.
- *Cyber Security: Basic Internet Safety Tips*: eight pieces of advice — strong passwords, safe personal data, secure devices, updates, careful Wi-Fi, two-factor authentication, private information, backups.
- *Should / ought to* = advice and moral duty; *must* = compulsion or strong duty; *must not* = prohibition.
- *A* before consonant sounds, *an* before vowel sounds, *the* for specific/unique things, no article for general plurals and uncountables.
- A reply email thanks, explains and apologises; a newspaper article has a headline, introduction, body and conclusion.
