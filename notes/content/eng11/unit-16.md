This is the most uncomfortable text in your course, and it is meant to be. It is
a definition essay whose writer refuses to define from the outside. Instead of
telling you what poverty *is*, she makes you smell it, hear it and stand in it —
and then, in her last line, she turns the whole essay round and asks a question
about you rather than about her.

::: key What the exam wants from this essay
Two things. First, the **content**: poverty as Parker defines it is not a lack of
money but a permanent condition of dirt, illness, tiredness, shame and
hopelessness, and she answers, one by one, the three solutions comfortable people
offer — welfare, schools, health clinics. Second, the **technique**: the
anaphora ("Poverty is…"), the direct second-person address, the piling-up of
concrete sensory detail, the two metaphors of acid and chisel, and the closing
question. Almost every question on this text is about one or the other.
:::

## 16.1 About the author

Almost nothing is known about **Jo Goodwin Parker**, and this chapter will not
invent anything.

What is known is this. In 1971 George Henderson, a professor at the University of
Oklahoma, was preparing a book called *America's Other Children: Public Schools
Outside Suburbia*. He received this essay in the post from West Virginia, in the
southern United States. It was signed "Jo Goodwin Parker" and it came with a
request that no byline — no author's biography — be printed with it. No further
information about the essay or its sender was ever discovered.

So the honest position is the one your textbook takes. The author may have been a
woman describing her own life exactly as she had lived it; she may have been a
writer or social worker who had adopted the voice of such a woman in order to be
heard. Nobody knows. Jo Goodwin Parker remains a mystery, and the essay is
printed with no biographical data at all.

::: caution Do not invent a biography
Some study guides on the internet supply Parker with a birth date, a husband's
name and a life story. All of it is fabricated. If an examination question asks
about the author, the correct and complete answer is: the writer is anonymous,
the essay reached its editor from West Virginia in 1971 with a request for no
byline, and nothing further is known. You can then add the more interesting point
— that the anonymity is part of the essay's meaning.
:::

**Why the anonymity matters.** A named author invites the reader to judge the
person: is she reliable, is she deserving, did she bring it on herself? An unnamed
one cannot be judged, only heard. The voice in this essay is therefore not one
woman's case history but the voice of a whole class of people who, as the last
paragraph says, are "all around you". It is also a demonstration of the essay's
own final claim: the poor are silent, and this is what it sounds like when one of
them speaks.

## 16.2 The context

The essay belongs to the United States of the 1960s and early 1970s. President
Johnson had declared a "War on Poverty" in 1964, and the whole country was
arguing about welfare payments, food programmes and whether the poor deserved
help. Parker is writing into that argument from the inside, from a house eight
miles outside a town in the rural South, on seventy-eight dollars a month for a
family of four.

Two features of that world must be understood before the essay makes sense. There
was no free national health service; a clinic meant a journey and a bill. And
welfare was not a right quietly transferred to a bank account but a favour
applied for in person, in an office, in front of strangers — which is why the
paragraph on asking for help is the emotional centre of the essay.

## 16.3 The essay, paragraph by paragraph

### The opening: "Listen to me"

Parker does not begin with a definition. She begins with an instruction. She
places herself physically in front of the reader — dirty, smelling, with rotting
teeth — and demands two things: **listen**, and **listen without pity**. Pity, she
says, she cannot use. She asks the reader to put on her worn-out shoes instead.
The whole strategy of the essay is announced here: the reader is not an audience
but a person being addressed, accused and enlisted.

### Poverty is a smell

The second paragraph builds the world through one sense. It is the smell of a
mattress stained with dirt and illness, of urine, of milk gone sour because the
refrigerator has not worked for a long time, of spoiling food, of long-cooked
onions (onions are cheap), of the outdoor privy, of rotting garbage. And each
detail is closed with the same small, devastating explanation: the refrigerator
costs money to fix; she could bury the garbage, but a shovel costs money.

### Poverty is being tired

Now the body. She has always been tired. At the hospital, after her last baby,
she was told she had chronic anaemia from a poor diet, a bad case of worms, and
needed an operation. She listened politely — "the poor are always polite" — and
said nothing about there being no money for iron pills, better food or worm
medicine. Then the arithmetic that traps her: she once earned twenty-two dollars
a week, and decent care for three children cost twenty. When she left them with
"Granny", she came home to a baby whose unchanged nappy took the skin with it,
one child playing with broken glass and another alone at the edge of a lake. She
quit her job. Working made her poorer and endangered her children; not working
keeps her poor. This paragraph is the essay's proof that poverty is a trap, not a
choice.

### Poverty is dirt

Here she answers the comfortable reader directly: *anybody can be clean*. She
explains housekeeping without money. Breakfast is grits without margarine, or
cornbread without eggs — which has the advantage of using few dishes. The dishes
are washed in cold water without soap, because even the cheapest soap must be
saved for the baby's nappies. Hot water is a luxury, because fuel costs money
whether it is wood or electricity. She once saved for two months to buy a jar of
Vaseline for her cracked hands and the baby's rash; by the time she had the
money, the price had gone up two cents, and she went without. She looks far older
than she is.

### Poverty is danger

Cold nights are spent awake watching the fire, because one spark on the newspaper
that covers the walls would burn a sleeping child. In summer, gnats and flies
settle on the baby's tears; the window screens are torn and the rent is so low
she knows they will never be mended. Insects are in the food, the nose, the eyes.
Rain is a disaster because nappies will not dry. The paragraph ends in one of the
essay's compressed paradoxes: **"Poverty is cooking without food and cleaning
without soap."**

### Poverty is asking for help

The tone changes from description to procedure, and Parker uses the second person
throughout, so that it is the reader who walks round the block four or five times
before going in. You find the office. You are told the person you have reached is
not the person you need. You tell your story — spilling "the whole shame of your
poverty all over the desk" — and discover it is the wrong office, and must start
again. You are told to wait, and you do not really hear the reason through the
shame and despair. Nothing here is about money at all; it is entirely about
humiliation.

### Poverty is remembering

Only now does she tell her life. She left school in junior high because "nice"
children were cruel about her clothes and her smell. Her mother told the
attendance officer she was pregnant — she was not — so that she could work. She
had jobs off and on, never long enough to learn anything. She married young, and
for a time had a small house with hot water and everything the reader has. Her
husband lost his job; the unemployment insurance ran out; their things were
repossessed; they moved back to this house, which gets worse every week because
nothing is ever fixed. Three years, three babies. After the last one she ended
the marriage herself — it had been a good one, but she asks the reader whether
they could go on bringing children into this dirt, and whether they have ever
considered what birth control costs. There were no goodbyes; she hopes he
escaped, because he could never rise while they dragged him down. It is a
devastating paragraph precisely because there is no self-pity in it.

### The figures

Then she gives the number: seventy-eight dollars a month for four people, and
that is the most she can ever get. Twenty dollars goes on rent and most of the
rest on food — grits, cornmeal, rice, milk, beans. The electricity is rationed,
because every unit used is food not bought. That is why there is no soap, no
needles and thread, no hot water, no aspirin, no worm medicine, no shampoo —
"None of these things forever and ever and ever."

### Poverty is looking into a black future

Her children have no future she can see. Respectable children will not play with
her boys, so they will find friends who steal; she can already see them behind
prison bars instead of the bars of her poverty, or escaping into alcohol and
drugs. For her daughter, the best she can imagine is a life like her own.

### The three official answers

Parker now takes the three things a comfortable reader is about to say, and
answers each one.

*There are schools.* Yes — but her children have no extra books, magazines,
pencils, crayons or paper, and, most important, no health. They have worms,
infections, pink-eye all summer; they sleep on the floor or in her one bed; they
are not starving but they are malnourished. There is no surplus commodities
programme where she lives — the county said it cost too much — and although
there is a school lunch programme, two of her children will already be damaged
before they ever reach school.

*There are health clinics.* Yes — and they are in the towns, eight miles away.
She could walk sixteen miles there and back; her small children cannot. A
neighbour will drive her, but expects payment "one way or another" — and Parker
lets the reader work out what that means. Then she turns the knife: you know this
neighbour. He is the large man at the petrol station, the barbershop and the
corner store, complaining about the government wasting money on immoral mothers
of illegitimate children.

*There is welfare.* She has already shown what applying for it costs in shame,
and what it pays.

### The verdict, the dream and the demand

Two metaphors deliver the judgement: poverty is an **acid** that drips on pride
until all pride is gone, and a **chisel** that chips at honour until honour is
gone. Then a challenge — some readers say they would do something in her
situation, and perhaps they would, for the first week or the first month, "but
for year after year after year?"

The dream that follows is almost unbearably modest: money for the right food,
worm medicine, iron pills, toothbrushes, hand cream, a hammer and nails and a bit
of screening, a shovel, paint, sheeting, needles and thread; money to pay a fare
into town; and "oh, money for hot water and money for soap." She also dreams of a
welfare office as decent as other government offices, with enough staff, where
you tell your story once and are not made to prove your poverty over and over
again.

The final paragraph is the turn. She has come out of her despair to speak. She
did not come from another place or another time: others like her are all around
you. And she asks not for pity but for **anger** — anger that will make you help
her, anger that will make you tell other people about her. Then the last two
sentences: **"The poor are always silent. Can you be silent too?"**

## 16.4 The definition Parker arrives at

The essay is formally a **definition essay**, and it does produce a definition —
but not the one a dictionary would give. Poverty, for Parker, is not a shortage
of money; money is only the mechanism. Poverty is:

- **a physical condition** — dirt, smell, insects, cold water, rotting teeth,
  worms, anaemia, permanent tiredness;
- **a trap** — every escape route costs more than she has, so that working makes
  her poorer than not working;
- **a social humiliation** — being pitied, being suspected, being made to prove
  her need again and again;
- **the slow destruction of pride and honour** — the acid and the chisel;
- **and a sentence passed on her children**, who will inherit it.

Notice how the definition is built. She never writes "poverty means…". She writes
"Poverty is being tired", "Poverty is dirt", "Poverty is asking for help", and
lets forty concrete details do the defining. It is definition by accumulation.

## 16.5 Themes

**Poverty as a total condition, not a lack of cash.** Every detail in the essay
converts money into something else: a shovel, a jar of Vaseline, two cents, hot
water, a bus fare, a night's sleep. The essay's real subject is what the absence
of these does to a body and a mind over years.

**Shame and the cost of asking.** Parker devotes more space to the welfare office
than to hunger. The message is that a system which helps people while humiliating
them has not finished the job — a point she makes positively in her dream, where
the office is decent and you tell your story once.

**Prejudice as the real obstacle.** The large man at the petrol station is the
essay's only fully drawn character apart from the narrator, and that is
deliberate. He is comfortable, respectable, idle enough to spend his days
talking, and he is the source of the opinion that keeps her poor. Her greatest
obstacle is not the weather or the price of soap but what other people believe
about her.

**Inheritance.** The children carry the theme forward. They have no books and no
health; they will be damaged before they reach school; the boys will drift to
theft or drink; the daughter's best prospect is her mother's life. Poverty in
this essay reproduces itself.

**Dignity and the refusal of pity.** The first paragraph rejects pity and the
last demands anger. Between them the narrator never once asks to be felt sorry
for. She asks to be believed, and then to be acted on.

**Silence and voice.** "The poor are always polite. The poor always listen." Both
sentences describe the same silence, and the essay exists to break it. The final
question transfers the choice to the reader.

## 16.6 Style and technique

**Anaphora.** Most paragraphs open with the same two words: *Poverty is*. The
repetition does three jobs at once. It holds a very disorderly experience in a
clear shape; it makes the essay a definition by insisting each time that this,
too, is what the word means; and it builds a rhythm like hammer blows, so that
the accumulation itself becomes the argument.

**Direct address.** The essay is written *to* someone: "You ask me what is
poverty?", "You can say in your clean clothes coming from your clean house", "I
bet you know my neighbour." The reader is given a clean house, clean clothes and
comfortable opinions, and then made to occupy the narrator's shoes. In the
welfare paragraph the pronoun switches entirely to "you", so that it is the
reader who circles the block and spills the shame on the desk.

**Concrete sensory detail.** Parker almost never uses an abstract noun where an
object will do. Sour milk, fly specks, a dried nappy, broken glass, a two-cent
rise in the price of Vaseline, newspaper on the walls. Each detail is small
enough to be believed and specific enough to be unforgettable, and several
carry their own arithmetic — twenty-two dollars against twenty, seventy-eight
dollars against a month.

**Paragraph development.** The pattern is remarkably consistent: a short
declarative topic sentence ("Poverty is dirt"), then a challenge or a question,
then a run of concrete examples, then a flat closing line that gives the reason —
almost always that the thing costs money. That final turn is what converts
description into argument.

**Metaphor, used sparingly.** For most of its length the essay avoids figurative
language, which is why the two metaphors near the end land so hard: poverty as
**acid** dripping on pride, and as a **chisel** chipping at honour. Both are
images of slow, mechanical, unstoppable wearing away — exactly the point she has
been making about time.

| Device | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Anaphora | "Poverty is being tired… Poverty is dirt… Poverty is asking for help" | Turns an accumulation of misery into an ordered definition |
| Second-person address | "Put yourself in my dirty, worn out, ill-fitting shoes" | Removes the reader's safe distance |
| Rhetorical question | "but for year after year after year?" | Exposes the emptiness of easy sympathy |
| Metaphor | "Poverty is an acid that drips on pride" | Shows the damage as gradual and chemical, not sudden |
| Parallelism | acid on pride / chisel on honour | Doubles the blow and makes the pair memorable |
| Paradox | "cooking without food and cleaning without soap" | Compresses an impossible daily situation into six words |
| Irony | the neighbour who complains about "immoral mothers" while demanding payment "one way or another" | Convicts the respectable of the vice they denounce |
| Repetition | "over and over and over again"; "forever and ever and ever" | Makes the reader feel duration, which is her central point |
| Understatement | "I listened politely — the poor are always polite" | Hides fury under good manners, which is more damning than anger |
| Closing question | "The poor are always silent. Can you be silent too?" | Transfers responsibility from the writer to the reader |

::: tip How to quote this essay in an answer
You do not need long quotations. Three short ones will carry almost any answer:
**"Listen without pity"** for her refusal of charity, **"Poverty is an acid that
drips on pride"** for the moral damage, and **"The poor are always silent. Can
you be silent too?"** for the ending. Name the device each time — refusal of
pity, metaphor, rhetorical question — and you have technique as well as content.

## Chapter summary

- "What is Poverty?" (1971) is an anonymous American **definition essay** sent
  from West Virginia to the editor George Henderson with a request that no
  author's biography be printed. Nothing is known about "Jo Goodwin Parker".
- The narrator is a young woman, made old by hardship, living eight miles from
  town with three children on seventy-eight dollars a month.
- She defines poverty not as a lack of money but as a total condition: a
  permanent smell, permanent tiredness, dirt that cannot be washed away without
  soap or hot water, insects, illness, and danger.
- Working made her poorer — twenty-two dollars a week against twenty dollars for
  childcare — and left her children injured, so she had to stop. Poverty is a
  **trap**, not a choice.
- Asking for help costs shame: the wrong office, the repeated story, the waiting,
  "the red cloud of shame."
- She answers the three usual solutions. Schools cannot help children who have no
  books and no health; clinics are eight miles away and the lift must be paid for
  "one way or another"; welfare is seventy-eight dollars and a humiliation.
- Her greatest obstacle is **prejudice**, embodied in the respectable neighbour
  who complains about immoral mothers while demanding a price for a car ride.
- Poverty is "an acid that drips on pride" and "a chisel that chips on honour".
  Her dream is soap, hot water, worm medicine and a welfare office where you tell
  your story once.
- Technique: **anaphora** ("Poverty is…"), **second-person address**, relentless
  **concrete detail**, sparing **metaphor**, and a closing **rhetorical
  question** — "The poor are always silent. Can you be silent too?" — which
  demands anger rather than pity.

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** For Parker, poverty is not simply a shortage of money but a whole
condition of life. It is a smell that never leaves the house; it is being always
tired, with anaemia, worms and no money for medicine; it is dirt, because soap,
hot water and fuel all cost money; it is insects, torn screens and a fire that
must be watched all night; it is the shame of asking for help; and it is an acid
that wears away pride and a chisel that chips away honour. Above all it is a trap
from which every exit costs money she does not have.

**2.** Her three children suffer in body, in schooling and in prospects. They
have worms, infections and pink-eye all summer; they are not starving but they
are malnourished; they sleep on the floor or in her single bed; the baby's skin
came away with a dried nappy, another child played with broken glass and the
eldest was left alone at the edge of a lake. At school they have no extra books,
magazines, pencils, crayons or paper — and two of them, she says, will already be
damaged before they ever get there. Looking ahead she expects her sons to drift
into theft, alcohol or drugs, and for her daughter "at best, there is a life like
mine."

**3.** She goes to the welfare office. She circles the block four or five times
before she can make herself go in, and goes only because she thinks of her
children. Inside, everyone is busy; the person who eventually comes out is never
the person she needs; and after telling her whole story — "spilling the whole
shame of your poverty all over the desk" — she is told this is the wrong office
and must begin the process again somewhere else, where it is no easier. Finally
she is told to wait, and cannot take in the reason because of the shame and
despair. The problems are therefore not only bureaucratic delay but repeated
humiliation, and the payment at the end is seventy-eight dollars a month for four
people.

**4.** Because pity is useless to her — "I cannot use your pity." Pity lets the
listener feel kind while changing nothing, and it places the poor person below
the listener as an object of charity rather than beside them as a person with a
case. What Parker wants instead is first understanding ("Listen with
understanding… put yourself in my shoes") and finally anger — anger that will
make the reader act and speak on her behalf. The refusal of pity at the start and
the demand for anger at the end frame the whole essay.

**5.** It means that Parker can see her children's future and it is as hopeless as
her present. Respectable families will not let their children play with her sons,
so the boys will attach themselves to boys who steal, and she can already picture
them "behind the bars of their prison instead of behind the bars of my poverty";
or they will escape into alcohol or drugs and be enslaved by them. For her
daughter the best she can imagine is a repetition of her own life. The sentence
states the essay's bleakest theme — that poverty is inherited — and the word
"black" carries no hope of dawn.

**6.** She means that poor people do not complain, do not protest and are never
heard. Earlier she has explained why: "the poor are always polite. The poor always
listen" — they accept a doctor's advice they cannot afford without saying so,
because objecting is dangerous and useless, and because shame keeps them quiet.
Their silence is what allows comfortable people to believe whatever they like
about them. This is why the essay exists: it is one of the silent speaking. And
because the next sentence is "Can you be silent too?", the phrase also becomes a
challenge — she has broken her silence, and the reader no longer has an excuse
for keeping his.

**7.** Because working cost more than it earned and endangered her children. She
was making twenty-two dollars a week, while a good nursery school for three
children cost twenty dollars a week, so almost nothing remained. When she left the
children with "Granny" instead, she came home to find the baby covered with fly
specks in a nappy that had not been changed — so hardened that when it came off,
bits of the baby's flesh came with it — one child playing with a sharp piece of
broken glass and the eldest alone at the edge of a lake. The episode is her
central proof that poverty is a trap rather than laziness.

   welfare, education and health clinics? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. What writing strategy does Parker use at the beginning of most of her
   paragraphs? How does she develop each paragraph, and how does the final
   paragraph use questions to involve the reader? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. Using adjectives that highlight the futility of the situation, write a short
   definition essay on "Growing up in Poverty", imitating Parker's style. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** Money, food and medicine are all things Parker could be given. Opinions
cannot be, and it is opinions that decide whether she is given anything at all.
That is why she calls them, in effect, her greatest obstacle.

The prejudices are named one by one in the essay. The first is that cleanliness
is a matter of will: "Anybody can be clean," says the reader in clean clothes,
coming from a clean house — and Parker has to spend a whole paragraph explaining
that soap, hot water and fuel are purchases, not decisions. The second is that
the poor are lazy. Her answer is arithmetic: she worked for twenty-two dollars a
week when childcare cost twenty, and the children were injured and neglected while
she did it. The third is that the poor are immoral and deserve what they get, and
this is the one she attacks hardest, through the only other character she draws in
the whole essay — the large man who spends his days at the gas station, the
barbershop and the corner store "complaining about the government spending money
on the immoral mothers of illegitimate children," and who is also the neighbour
who will drive her to the clinic but "expects to get paid, one way or another."
In two sentences she convicts the respectable of exactly the vice they denounce.

These opinions are obstacles in the most practical sense. They decide how much
welfare is voted and how the office treats her; they are why she must prove her
poverty over and over again, and why asking for help costs so much shame; they are
why her county decided a surplus commodities programme cost too much; they are why
respectable children will not play with her sons, which in turn decides the
company the boys will keep. The other hardships wear down her body; prejudice
wears down her pride and closes the exits. This is also why the essay's final
demand is for **anger** rather than money — anger is what changes an opinion, and
opinion is what has to change first.

**2.** Parker deals with the three standard answers in turn, and in each case her
method is the same: she agrees that the thing exists, and then shows that it
cannot reach her.

**Welfare.** She does not deny that help is available — she is receiving it. She
attacks it on two grounds. The first is the amount: seventy-eight dollars a month
for four people, of which twenty goes on rent and almost all the rest on grits,
cornmeal, rice, milk and beans, with electricity rationed because every unit used
is food not bought. That is why there is no soap, no aspirin, no worm medicine,
no shampoo, "none of these things forever and ever and ever." The second, and to
her the worse, is the cost in dignity: circling the block before going in, the
wrong person, the wrong office, the whole shame spilt across a desk, the waiting,
the proving of her poverty again and again. Her dream is not for a larger cheque
alone but for an office as decent as other government offices, with enough staff,
where you tell your story once.

**Education.** "But you say to me, there are schools. Yes, there are schools." The
concession is followed by the destruction. Her children have no extra books, no
magazines, no pencils, crayons or paper — and, most important of all, no health.
They have worms and infections and pink-eye; they sleep on the floor; they suffer
from malnutrition even though they are not hungry. A school lunch programme exists,
but two of her children will already be damaged by the time they reach school, and
there is no surplus commodities programme in her county because it was judged too
expensive. Her point is that schooling can only work on a child who arrives fed,
rested and well; poverty has already disabled the remedy before it is applied.
She adds, drily, that she does remember what she was taught about health at
school, and that it does not do much good.

**Health clinics.** Again the concession: yes, there are clinics — "and they are
in the towns." She lives eight miles out. She could walk sixteen miles there and
back; her small children cannot. The neighbour with the car will take her, but
expects payment "one way or another," and that neighbour is the respectable man
who denounces immoral mothers. So the clinic is real, free at the point of entry
perhaps, and completely inaccessible.

Taken together, the three refutations make one argument. Society's solutions are
designed for people who already have the minimum — transport, health, decency of
treatment, time. Poverty removes precisely those things, so that the poorer a
person is, the less able she is to use the help that exists. It is not that the
remedies are absent; it is that they are out of reach.

**3.** **The opening strategy.** Parker begins most of her paragraphs with the
same two words — "Poverty is". "Poverty is being tired." "Poverty is dirt."
"Poverty is asking for help." "Poverty is remembering." "Poverty is looking into a
black future." "Poverty is an acid…" This is **anaphora**, and there is a clear
recurring pattern: each paragraph is a fresh, complete definition of the same
word. The effect is threefold. It imposes order on an experience that is
otherwise shapeless; it keeps reminding the reader that the essay is answering the
question in its title; and the hammering repetition makes the accumulation itself
feel like an argument, as though the word had to be defined again and again
because no single definition could hold it. A second, connected strategy is the
frequent opening concession — "But you say to me, there are schools" — which
allows her to voice the reader's objection before demolishing it.

**Paragraph development.** Each paragraph is then built the same way: a short flat
topic sentence; a challenge or a question to the reader; a run of small, exact,
physical details; and a closing sentence that supplies the reason, which is
almost always that the thing costs money. "I could bury it, but where is the
shovel? Shovels cost money." What makes each paragraph memorable is the quality of
those details. They are specific enough to be checked — the refrigerator that has
not worked, the onions that are cheap, the two-cent rise in the price of
Vaseline after two months of saving, the dried nappy that took the baby's skin
with it, the twenty-two dollars against the twenty. They also work through the
senses, especially smell and touch, which are the senses a comfortable reader can
least easily ignore. And the tone is level throughout: she never raises her voice,
which makes the facts do all the work.

**The final paragraph.** The questions at the end change the reader's position
from spectator to participant. Throughout the essay Parker has used the second
person to place the reader in her shoes; now she uses it to place a demand on
them. She tells them she did not come from another place or another time, and that
"others like me are all around you" — so the problem is local and present, not
distant. She asks them to look at her "with an angry heart", defining the anger
precisely: "anger that will help you help me. Anger that will let you tell of me."
Then the closing pair: "The poor are always silent. Can you be silent too?" This
is a **rhetorical question**, and it is unanswerable in the reader's favour. To
say yes is to admit indifference; to say no is to accept a duty. Because the essay
has spent its whole length establishing that she has no voice and the reader has
one, the question also transfers the work: she has broken her silence at the cost
of describing her own degradation, and the only decent response is for the reader
to break his.

**4.** *(A model imitation. Notice the repeated opening, the second-person
address, the concrete detail and the flat closing reason.)*

**Growing up in Poverty**

You ask me what it is to grow up poor. I will tell you. Do not be sorry for me.
Look instead, and remember.

Growing up in poverty is a cold, dark, endless morning. It is waking on a thin
mat in a room shared with four others, in clothes you also slept in, because
there is only one set and it must last. It is walking an hour to school on an
empty stomach because the tea is finished and the flour is for the evening.

Growing up in poverty is being hungry in a room full of books. The teacher speaks,
and you hear him, but your head is light and the words slide off. You are not
stupid. You are hungry. Nobody in that room knows the difference.

Growing up in poverty is shame. It is the torn shoe you keep behind the other
foot. It is the fee you cannot pay, read out in front of the class. It is the
friend's house you never invite anyone back from. Shame is cheap; it is the one
thing poverty gives you in plenty.

Growing up in poverty is waiting. Waiting for the remittance that is late.
Waiting for the health post to have the medicine. Waiting for a father who went
to the Gulf when you were six and writes that he will come next year, and next
year.

Growing up in poverty is a narrow, fixed, inherited future. Your father dug; you
will dig. Your mother left school at twelve; your sister will leave at twelve. The
land is too small to divide again, so somebody must go, and it will be you.

Some of you say you would study anyway, and work anyway, and rise anyway. Perhaps
you would — for a term, or a year. But for year after year after year, on an
empty stomach, in a torn shoe, with nobody at home who can read your homework?

I am not asking for your sympathy. I am asking you to look at the boy at the back
of your own classroom, the one who is always tired, and to stop calling him lazy.

**Reference to the context**

1. "Poverty is an acid that drips on pride until all pride is worn away. Poverty
   is a chisel that chips on honour until honour is worn away."
   (a) Identify the figure of speech and explain both images. (b) Why are the two
   sentences built in exactly the same way? (c) What challenge does Parker put to
   the reader immediately afterwards? (d) Why does she place these lines near the
   end of the essay? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. "I have come out of my despair to tell you this… The poor are always silent.
   Can you be silent too?"
   (a) Where does this come, and what has just preceded it? (b) What does she
   mean by saying she did not come from another place or another time? (c) What
   does she ask of the reader, and how does it differ from what she rejected at
   the start? (d) What is the effect of ending on a question? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — reference to the context
**1.** (a) Both sentences are **metaphors**. In the first, poverty is an **acid**
dripping on pride: acid does not strike, it falls drop by drop and eats away the
surface it lands on, silently and without a sound, until nothing is left. In the
second, poverty is a **chisel** chipping at honour: a chisel works by many small
blows, each of which removes a little, until the shape of the thing is gone. Both
images say the same thing in different materials — that poverty destroys a person
not by one great disaster but by continuous small erosion over years. Neither
image allows for resistance: you cannot argue with acid.
(b) They are built identically — the same grammar, the same rhythm, the same
closing phrase "until all/until … is worn away" — which is **parallelism**. The
repetition doubles the force, makes the pair easy to remember, and, most
importantly, enacts the very thing being described: the second sentence wears at
the reader exactly as the first one did, so the form imitates the content. The
pairing of *pride* with *honour* also widens the claim from how a person feels
about herself to how she stands in the eyes of others.
(c) She immediately turns on the reader: some of you say that you would do
something in my situation, and maybe you would — "for the first week or the first
month, but for year after year after year?" The challenge is aimed precisely at
the comfortable listener's confidence that character would save him. Her answer is
that character is exactly what is being dissolved, and that no one's survives
indefinitely.
(d) Because by this point she has earned them. For most of the essay she has
refused figurative language and given nothing but hard physical fact — sour milk,
fly specks, two cents, seventy-eight dollars. The reader therefore trusts her as a
witness, and the two metaphors arrive as a verdict on evidence already heard,
rather than as decoration. They also lift the argument from the physical to the
moral at exactly the moment she needs to: she is about to ask for anger rather
than charity, and anger is a moral response.

**2.** (a) These lines are the last paragraph of the essay. They follow the
modest dream — money for soap and hot water, for worm medicine and a shovel and
needles and thread, and for a welfare office where you tell your story once and
are not made to prove your poverty over and over again. The essay therefore ends
by moving out of description altogether and speaking directly to the reader.
(b) She means that she is not a historical example or a foreign case study, and
that the reader cannot file her away as somebody else's problem. "Others like me
are all around you": the poverty she has described is happening now, in the
reader's own country, within walking distance of the reader's own clean house.
The sentence blocks the last available escape route, which is distance.
(c) She asks for **anger** — but a very precisely defined anger: "anger that will
help you help me. Anger that will let you tell of me." It is not rage for its own
sake but the emotion that produces action and speech. This is the exact opposite
of what she forbade in her first paragraph, where she said "Listen without pity. I
cannot use your pity." Pity looks down and does nothing; anger stands level and
acts. The essay therefore begins by refusing one emotion and ends by demanding
another, and the whole body of the text is the evidence that justifies the swap.
(d) Ending on a **rhetorical question** does three things. It reverses the
direction of the essay: for eleven paragraphs the reader has been examining her
life, and in seven words she is examining his. It makes evasion impossible,
because both answers convict — to say yes is to confess indifference, to say no is
to accept a duty. And it exploits the parallel she has just drawn: the poor are
silent because they have no choice, while the reader would be silent by choice,
which is a far worse thing. Because the essay stops there, with no reassurance and
no conclusion, the question is left with the reader rather than with the writer —
which is precisely where she wants it.
