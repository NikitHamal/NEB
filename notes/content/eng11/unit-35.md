Question 8 is worth five marks and takes five minutes, which makes it the best-paid
question on the paper. It tests vocabulary: the meaning of a word, the word that fits
a gap, the opposite of a word, the meaning of an idiom, or a word built from a given
root. None of it can be bluffed, but almost all of it can be worked out — English
words are made of parts, and the parts have fixed meanings. This chapter is about
those parts, and about the sounds and idioms that go with them.

::: key Learn families, not words
Learning *nation* alone gives you one word. Learning *nation, national, nationality,
nationalise, international, nationalism* gives you six, and they cost almost no extra
effort because they share a root. Every vocabulary hour should end with a family.
:::

## 35.1 What Question 8 asks

| Item type | Example |
|---|---|
| Meaning in context | *What does "insistent" mean in the passage?* |
| Word formation | *Fill the gap with the correct form of* **able**: *his ____ to pay.* |
| Opposites | *Give the antonym of "scarce".* |
| Idiom or phrasal verb | *What does "call off" mean?* |
| Prefix or suffix | *Add a prefix to "regular" to give the opposite.* |

Five items, one mark each, no part marks. A near-miss — *ability* written as *able*,
*irregular* as *unregular* — scores nothing, so word class and spelling matter as much
as meaning.

## 35.2 Sounds and spelling

English spelling records how words were said several centuries ago, which is why
letters sit in words without being pronounced. The commonest **silent letters**:

| Silent | Words |
|---|---|
| k before n | *know, knee, knife, knock, knowledge* |
| w before r, and in some others | *write, wrong, wrist, answer, sword, two* |
| b after m, and before t | *comb, climb, lamb, thumb; doubt, debt, subtle* |
| l before f, k, m | *half, calf, walk, talk, calm, palm, should, would* |
| t between s and l, or in -ten | *castle, whistle, listen, fasten* |
| h at the start of a few words | *hour, honest, honour, heir* |
| g before n | *sign, design, foreign, gnaw, campaign* |
| p before s, n, or in a few others | *psychology, pneumonia, receipt, cupboard* |
| n after m at the end | *autumn, column, hymn, condemn* |

A silent letter often reappears when a suffix is added — the *g* of *sign* is heard in
*signature*, the *n* of *column* in *columnist* — which is a useful clue that two words
belong to the same family.

::: caution Pairs that sound alike and are not
- *their* (belonging to them) · *there* (in that place) · *they're* (they are)
- *its* (belonging to it) · *it's* (it is, or it has)
- *your* (belonging to you) · *you're* (you are)
- *to* · *too* (also, or excessively) · *two* (2)
- *principal* (head of a college; main) · *principle* (a rule or belief)
- *stationary* (not moving) · *stationery* (paper, pens and envelopes)
- *complement* (something that completes) · *compliment* (praise)
- *weather* (rain and sun) · *whether* (if)
- *peace* (no war) · *piece* (a part)

And three pairs that are *not* homophones but are confused anyway: *advice* (noun,
with an /s/) and *advise* (verb, with a /z/); *loose* (not tight) and *lose* (to
misplace); *accept* (to receive) and *except* (apart from).
:::

## 35.3 Word stress

Every English word of more than one syllable has one syllable said louder, longer and
higher than the rest. Putting the stress in the wrong place can make a word
unrecognisable even when every sound in it is correct.

Two patterns are worth memorising because they are almost exceptionless.

**Pattern 1 — the noun/verb shift.** In many two-syllable words spelt the same, the
noun is stressed on the first syllable and the verb on the second.

```figure caption="The noun/verb stress shift. The shaded syllable is the loud one. Identical spelling, two different words."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import matplotlib.patches as mpatches
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4.9, 3.1))
ax.axis("off")
pairs = [("re", "cord"), ("pre", "sent"), ("ob", "ject"), ("in", "crease")]

def chip(x, y, txt, hot):
    ax.add_patch(mpatches.FancyBboxPatch((x, y), 1.65, 0.80,
                 boxstyle="round,pad=0.0,rounding_size=0.10", linewidth=1.1,
                 edgecolor=ACCENT if hot else GRID,
                 facecolor=ACCENT if hot else "#ffffff"))
    ax.text(x + 0.82, y + 0.40, txt, ha="center", va="center", fontsize=7.6,
            color="white" if hot else MUTED)

ax.text(2.42, 5.30, "NOUN  —  stress on the first", ha="center", va="center",
        fontsize=6.7, color=INK)
ax.text(7.12, 5.30, "VERB  —  stress on the second", ha="center", va="center",
        fontsize=6.7, color=INK)
y = 4.15
for a, b in pairs:
    chip(0.75, y, a, True); chip(2.55, y, b, False)
    chip(5.45, y, a, False); chip(7.25, y, b, True)
    y -= 1.05
ax.plot([4.70, 4.70], [0.95, 4.98], color=GRID, linewidth=1.0)
ax.set_xlim(0.45, 9.25)
ax.set_ylim(0.75, 5.75)
```

So *keep a **re**cord* but *please re**cord** it*; *a sharp **in**crease* but *prices
will in**crease***. The same holds for *conduct, conflict, contrast, export, import,
object, permit, present, rebel, suspect* and *progress*.

**Pattern 2 — the suffix that pulls the stress.** Certain endings force the stress onto
the syllable immediately before them: **-ion, -ity, -ic, -ical, -ial, -graphy, -logy**.

| Base | Derived word | Stress |
|---|---|---|
| **in**form | infor**ma**tion | before *-ion* |
| **pos**sible | possi**bi**lity | before *-ity* |
| **e**conomy | eco**no**mic | before *-ic* |
| **po**litics | po**li**tical | before *-ical* |
| **pho**tograph | pho**to**graphy | before *-graphy* |

A few endings take the stress **themselves**: *-ee, -eer, -ese, -ique, -esque* — hence
employ**ee**, engin**eer**, Japan**ese**, un**ique**, picturesque. And the ordinary
endings *-ly, -ness, -ful, -less, -ment, -er, -ing, -ed* never move the stress at all:
*care, careful, carefully, carelessness* all keep it on *care*.

::: example Worked example 35.1 — hearing the difference
Read each pair aloud, putting the loudness where the bold mark shows.

1. *The rebels planned to re**bel** against the king.* — noun first, verb second.
2. *The government will not **per**mit … you need a per**mit**.* Careful: here the
   **noun** is *permit* (a document) and the **verb** is *permit* (to allow), so the
   order in the sentence above is verb, then noun — *per**mit*** then ***per**mit*.
3. *Their **con**duct was poor, though the teacher con**duc**ted the class well.*

The test: if the word is doing something in the sentence, stress the second syllable;
if it is a thing you can put *the* or *a* in front of, stress the first.
:::

## 35.4 Prefixes

A **prefix** goes in front of a root and changes its **meaning**. It almost never
changes the word class: *happy* and *unhappy* are both adjectives.

| Prefix | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| un- | not, the reverse of | *unhappy, unable, undo, unlock* |
| in-, im-, il-, ir- | not | *incorrect, impossible, illegal, irregular* |
| dis- | not, the opposite of | *dishonest, disagree, disappear* |
| non- | not (neutral) | *non-violent, non-stop, non-fiction* |
| mis- | wrongly, badly | *misunderstand, misspell, misuse* |
| re- | again, back | *rewrite, rebuild, return* |
| pre- / post- | before / after | *preview, prepaid / postgraduate, postpone* |
| over- / under- | too much / too little | *overwork, overcrowded / underpaid, underfed* |
| sub- / super- | under / above, beyond | *submarine, subway / supervise, superhuman* |
| inter- | between | *international, interval, interview* |
| trans- | across | *transport, transfer, translate* |
| anti- / pro- | against / in favour of | *antiseptic, anti-clockwise / pro-democracy* |
| auto- | self | *autobiography, automatic* |
| mono-, uni- / bi- / tri- / multi- / semi- | one / two / three / many / half | *monologue, uniform, bilingual, tricycle, multipurpose, semicircle* |
| micro- / macro- / tele- | small / large / far | *microscope, macroeconomics, telephone* |

The four negative prefixes *in-, im-, il-, ir-* are one prefix with four spellings, and
the spelling is decided by the first letter of the root:

- **im-** before *b, m, p* — *imbalance, immoral, impossible, impatient*
- **il-** before *l* — *illegal, illiterate, illogical*
- **ir-** before *r* — *irregular, irrelevant, irresponsible*
- **in-** before everything else — *incorrect, invisible, inactive*

No rule tells you whether a word takes *un-*, *in-* or *dis-*; that is learnt word by
word. *Unhappy*, not *inhappy*; *incorrect*, not *uncorrect*; *dishonest*, not
*unhonest*.

## 35.5 Suffixes, and the spelling rules for adding them

A **suffix** goes at the end of a root and usually changes the **word class**.

| Makes a | Suffixes | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Noun (thing, quality) | -ment, -ness, -tion/-sion, -ity, -ance/-ence, -ship, -hood, -dom, -age, -ism | *agreement, kindness, decision, ability, importance, friendship, childhood, freedom, marriage, tourism* |
| Noun (person) | -er, -or, -ist, -ian, -ee | *teacher, actor, artist, musician, employee* |
| Adjective | -ful, -less, -able/-ible, -ous, -ive, -al, -ic, -y, -ish, -en | *careful, careless, comfortable, visible, dangerous, active, national, historic, rainy, childish, wooden* |
| Verb | -ise/-ize, -en, -ify, -ate | *modernise, widen, simplify, activate* |
| Adverb | -ly, -wards, -wise | *quickly, backwards, clockwise* |

Beware of *-ly*. On an adjective it makes an adverb (*quick → quickly*), but on a
**noun** it makes an adjective: *friend → friendly*, *love → lovely*, *cost → costly*.
*She smiled friendly* is wrong; *She gave a friendly smile* is right.

**Four spelling rules cover almost every case.**

1. **Drop silent *e*** before a vowel suffix, keep it before a consonant suffix:
   *hoping* but *hopeful*, *caring* but *careful*. Keep it after *c* or *g* before *a*
   or *o*, to hold the soft sound: *noticeable, courageous*. Exceptions to learn:
   *truly, argument, ninth*.
2. ***y* becomes *i*** after a consonant: *happiness, carried, beautiful*. But keep *y*
   before *-ing* (*carrying*) and after a vowel (*played, enjoyment*). Exceptions:
   *daily, paid, said*.
3. **Double the final consonant** before a vowel suffix when a one-syllable word ends
   consonant–vowel–consonant (*stopping, bigger, runner*), and in a longer word only
   when the **stress falls on the last syllable**: be**gin** → *beginning*, pre**fer** →
   *preferred*, but **of**fer → *offered*, **vi**sit → *visited*. British English
   doubles a final *-l* whatever the stress: *travelled, cancelled*.
4. ***-ful* has one *l***: *beautiful, careful, useful* — though the adverb has two,
   *beautifully*.

## 35.6 Building words: prefix + root + suffix

```figure caption="The anatomy of an English word. Change the prefix and the meaning turns over; change the suffix and the part of speech changes."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import matplotlib.patches as mpatches
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(4.9, 2.7))
ax.axis("off")
words = [
    (7.05, [("un", 1.17, "prefix\nnot"), ("comfort", 4.09, "root\ncarries the meaning"),
            ("able", 2.34, "suffix\nmakes an adjective")]),
    (3.60, [("inter", 2.92, "prefix\nbetween"), ("nation", 3.51, "root\ncarries the meaning"),
            ("al", 1.17, "suffix\nadjective")]),
]
for ytop, parts in words:
    x = 1.20
    for i, (txt, w, lab) in enumerate(parts):
        face = "#e8f1f9" if i == 1 else "#ffffff"
        ax.add_patch(mpatches.FancyBboxPatch((x, ytop), w, 1.10,
                     boxstyle="round,pad=0.0,rounding_size=0.10", linewidth=1.1,
                     edgecolor=ACCENT if i == 1 else GRID, facecolor=face))
        ax.text(x + w / 2, ytop + 0.55, txt, ha="center", va="center",
                fontsize=8.4, color=INK)
        ax.text(x + w / 2, ytop - 0.30, lab, ha="center", va="top",
                fontsize=6.0, color=MUTED, linespacing=1.5)
        x += w
ax.set_xlim(0.80, 9.40)
ax.set_ylim(1.55, 8.55)
```

Ask two questions of any new word: *what does the prefix do to the meaning?* and *what
class does the suffix make it?*

::: example Worked example 35.2 — one root, eight words
Root: **act** — "to do".

| Word | Class | Built from | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| act | verb / noun | root | to do; a deed |
| action | noun | act + -ion | the doing of something |
| active | adjective | act + -ive | doing things, busy |
| activity | noun | active + -ity | something done |
| inactive | adjective | in- + active | not doing anything |
| react | verb | re- + act | to act in response |

Now the exam item *"Fill the gap: after the operation he remained ______ for a month
(act)"* answers itself. The gap follows *remained*, which needs an adjective; the sense
is negative; so the answer is **inactive** — not *inaction*, which is a noun.
:::

## 35.7 Synonyms, antonyms and words that are nearly the same

A **synonym** has a similar meaning (*begin / start / commence*); an **antonym** has the
opposite (*scarce / plentiful*). Two warnings.

**Synonyms are rarely exact.** *Slim*, *thin* and *skinny* all describe a narrow body,
but *slim* is a compliment, *thin* is neutral and *skinny* is unkind. *Commence* is not
better than *begin*, only more formal — and in a story it is the wrong choice.

**Some antonyms are made by prefix, others are separate words.** *Regular → irregular*
(prefix), but *hot → cold* (separate word). If the question says *add a prefix*, it
wants the first kind.

| Word | Antonym by prefix | Word | Antonym is a different word |
|---|---|---|---|
| legal | illegal | ancient | modern |
| possible | impossible | scarce | plentiful |
| relevant | irrelevant | shallow | deep |
| obey | disobey | permanent | temporary |
| employment | unemployment | artificial | natural |

Finally, six pairs whose members are not interchangeable, however similar they look:

- **affect** (verb: to influence) · **effect** (noun: the result). *The rain affected
  the crop; the effect was severe.*
- **lie** (to rest; *lay, lain*; no object) · **lay** (to put down; *laid, laid*; takes
  an object). *Lie down. Lay the book here.*
- **sensible** (having good judgement) · **sensitive** (easily hurt or affected).
- **among** (more than two) · **between** (two).

## 35.8 Idioms and phrasal verbs

An **idiom** is a fixed group of words whose meaning cannot be worked out from the
words themselves. You cannot guess it and you cannot alter it: it is *beat about the
bush*, never *beat around the shrub*.

| Idiom | Meaning |
|---|---|
| a piece of cake | very easy |
| once in a blue moon | very rarely |
| beat about the bush | avoid coming to the point |
| let the cat out of the bag | reveal a secret by accident |
| burn the midnight oil | work or study late into the night |
| hit the nail on the head | say exactly the right thing |
| turn a blind eye | ignore something on purpose |
| under the weather | slightly unwell |
| cost an arm and a leg | be extremely expensive |
| break the ice | make a first meeting less awkward |
| pull someone's leg | tease someone playfully |

A **phrasal verb** is a verb plus a small word (*up, off, after, into*) whose meaning
is often nothing like the verb alone.

| Phrasal verb | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| put off | postpone | *The meeting was put off till Sunday.* |
| call off | cancel | *They called off the match.* |
| give up | stop doing; surrender | *He gave up smoking.* |
| look after | take care of | *She looks after her grandmother.* |
| look up | find in a book or list | *Look the word up.* |
| get over | recover from | *He got over the fever quickly.* |
| carry out | perform | *They carried out the experiment.* |
| run out of | have no more of | *We ran out of petrol.* |
| take after | resemble (a relative) | *He takes after his mother.* |
| put up with | tolerate | *I cannot put up with the noise.* |

::: caution Where phrasal verbs go wrong
Some phrasal verbs can be split by their object and some cannot.
*Call off the match* and *call the match off* are both correct, and with a pronoun the
split is **compulsory**: *call it off*, never *~~call off it~~*.
But *look after* cannot be split: *look after the baby*, never *~~look the baby after~~*,
and *look after her*, never *~~look her after~~*.
When in doubt, keep the phrasal verb together — except with a pronoun object, where you
must test whether the split sounds right.
:::

## 35.9 Working out a word you do not know

Unknown words turn up in Questions 1 and 8, and the paper expects reasoning rather than
guessing. Four checks, in this order:

1. **The parts.** *Irreplaceable* = *ir-* (not) + *replace* + *-able* (can be) =
   "cannot be replaced".
2. **The class.** A word after *the* is a noun; a word after *very* is an adjective or
   adverb. That alone halves the possibilities.
3. **The signal.** *That is, in other words, such as, or*, a dash or a comma pair —
   writers often define a hard word immediately after using it.
4. **The direction.** Even without the exact sense, the sentence tells you whether the
   word is positive or negative. Half a mark's worth of sense beats a blank.

::: example Worked example 35.3 — reasoning to a meaning
> *The road was **impassable** after the landslide, and lorries queued for three days
> at Krishnabhir.*

**Parts:** *im-* (not, before *p*) + *pass* + *-able* (able to be) — "not able to be
passed".
**Class:** it follows *was*, so it is an adjective describing *the road*.
**Direction:** lorries queued for three days, so the sense must be negative.
All three agree. The answer is **not able to be travelled along; blocked**.

Now the same reasoning on a harder word:

> *Her account of the accident was **corroborated** by two witnesses who had been
> standing at the junction.*

**Parts:** nothing familiar. **Class:** *was ____ by* — a passive verb. **Signal:** the
agents are *two witnesses*. **Direction:** witnesses standing at the junction make her
account more believable, not less. So *corroborated* must mean something like
**confirmed, supported by evidence** — which is exactly right. You reached a correct
answer from a word you had never seen.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Question 8 is five one-mark items on vocabulary. Word class and spelling must both be
  right; there are no part marks.
- **Silent letters** often reappear when a suffix is added (*sign → signature*), which
  is a clue to word families.
- **Stress** matters: two-syllable noun/verb pairs stress the first syllable as a noun
  and the second as a verb; the endings *-ion, -ity, -ic, -ical, -graphy* pull the
  stress onto the syllable before them.
- A **prefix** changes meaning; a **suffix** changes word class. The negative prefix is
  *im-* before *b, m, p*; *il-* before *l*; *ir-* before *r*; *in-* elsewhere.
- Four spelling rules cover adding suffixes: drop silent *e* before a vowel suffix,
  *y → i* after a consonant, double the final consonant when the stress is at the end,
  and *-ful* has one *l*.
- **Synonyms are rarely exact** — they differ in formality and in feeling. Some antonyms
  are made with a prefix, others are separate words.
- **Idioms and phrasal verbs** are fixed. A pronoun object must split a separable
  phrasal verb (*call it off*) but never an inseparable one (*look after her*).
- An unknown word can be reasoned out from its **parts, class, signal words and
  direction**.
