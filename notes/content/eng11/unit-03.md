Leo Tolstoy's "God Sees the Truth, but Waits" (1872) tells of a merchant who is
convicted of a murder he did not commit and spends twenty-six years in a Siberian
prison. It is a story about injustice, but far more about what a man does with
injustice once he has stopped expecting help from the world. The title is an old
Russian proverb, and the story is Tolstoy's argument that truth and forgiveness may
arrive very late — and still be worth waiting for.

::: key The central idea
Aksionov cannot change his sentence; he can only change himself. The story's climax
is not the confession of the real murderer but the moment Aksionov chooses to
forgive him, after which "his heart grew light" and he stops wanting to go home at
all.
:::

## 3.1 About the author

Count Lev Nikolayevich Tolstoy (1828–1910), known in English as Leo Tolstoy, was a
Russian novelist and moral thinker, and is often called the greatest novelist of
the nineteenth century. He was born on the family estate of Yasnaya Polyana, south
of Moscow, into the old Russian nobility, and was orphaned as a child. As a young
man he served as an artillery officer in the Crimean War, and the *Sevastopol
Sketches* he wrote there began his reputation.

His two enormous novels are *War and Peace* (1869), about Russian society during
Napoleon's invasion, and *Anna Karenina* (1877). Both belong to **Realism** — the
nineteenth-century movement that tried to show ordinary life exactly as it is,
without romantic decoration, and to explain characters by their circumstances.

::: definition Realism
A style of writing that presents life as it actually is: ordinary people, everyday
speech, accurate social detail, and no supernatural or sentimental solutions.
:::

In his fifties Tolstoy passed through a severe spiritual crisis, described in *A
Confession* (1882). He came to reject the Church, private property, the state and
violence of every kind, and adopted a simple Christian ethic built on the Sermon on
the Mount: love your enemies, judge no one, and do not resist evil by force. This
doctrine of **non-resistance** deeply influenced Mahatma Gandhi, who corresponded
with him. After the crisis his writing changed. He produced short moral tales
written in the plainest possible Russian — many of them, including this one, for a
school reader he compiled — along with *The Death of Ivan Ilyich* (1886), *What Is
Art?* (1897) and the novel *Resurrection* (1899). He died in 1910 at a small railway
station at Astapovo, having left home at the age of eighty-two.

The voice of the late tales is what you meet here: very short sentences, almost no
description, no comment from the author, and a plot arranged so that a moral
question is put to the reader rather than answered for them. Tolstoy strips away
everything a modern reader might call "style", and the bareness is the point.

## 3.2 The story

**The warning.** Ivan Dmitrich Aksionov is a young merchant in the town of
Vladimir. He owns two shops and a house, and he is handsome, fair-haired,
curly-headed, fond of fun and of singing. He drank heavily as a young man but gave
it up after his marriage. One summer he sets out for the fair at Nizhny. As he says
goodbye, his wife begs him not to go that day: she has dreamt that he came back
from the town and when he took off his cap his hair was grey. Aksionov laughs, calls
it a lucky sign, promises presents, and drives away.

**The night at the inn.** Half-way there he meets a merchant he knows, and the two
put up at the same inn, drink tea together and sleep in adjoining rooms. Aksionov is
not a late sleeper and wants to travel while it is cool, so he wakes his driver
before dawn, pays the landlord and goes on. About twenty-five miles further he stops
to feed the horses, orders a samovar and sits in the porch playing his guitar.

**The arrest.** A troika drives up with an official and two soldiers. The official
cross-questions him: where did he spend the night, was he alone, did he see the
other merchant this morning, why did he leave before dawn? Aksionov answers
everything and finally asks why he is being questioned like a thief. The official
replies that the merchant he slept beside has been found with his throat cut, and
orders a search. In Aksionov's bag they find a blood-stained knife. Aksionov, who
cannot explain it, stammers, turns pale and trembles — and his fear is taken for
guilt. He is bound, thrown into a cart and imprisoned. He is charged with murdering
a merchant from Ryazan and stealing twenty thousand rubles.

**The wife's question.** His wife comes to the prison with their small children.
Seeing him in prison dress and chains among criminals she faints. When she recovers
she tells him her petition to the Czar has been refused, reminds him of her dream —
"It was not for nothing I dreamt your hair had turned grey" — and then, stroking his
hair, asks the question that destroys him: "Vanya dearest, tell your wife the truth;
was it not you who did it?" Aksionov hides his face and weeps. When they have gone
he says to himself that only God can know the truth, and that it is to God alone he
must appeal. He writes no more petitions, gives up hope and only prays.

**Twenty-six years.** Aksionov is flogged with the knot and sent to the mines in
Siberia. He lives there twenty-six years. His hair turns white as snow, his beard
grows long and thin, he stoops, speaks little and never laughs. He learns to make
boots, earns a little money and buys *The Lives of the Saints*; he reads the lessons
in the prison church and sings in the choir. The authorities like him for his
meekness and the prisoners respect him: they call him "Grandfather" and "The Saint",
make him their spokesman, and bring their quarrels to him to judge. No news ever
reaches him from home.

**Makar Semyonich.** A new gang of convicts arrives. One of them, a strong grey
man of sixty, says he is from Vladimir and that his name is Makar Semyonich; he
claims he was sent to Siberia merely for untying a horse. When Aksionov's own story
is told to the newcomers, Makar slaps his knee and exclaims how wonderful it is that
they should meet, but refuses to explain why. Questioned, he jokes that the knife
must belong to the man in whose bag it was found — and that nobody could have hidden
a knife under a sleeping man's head without waking him. That remark tells Aksionov
everything. He goes away and lies awake all night, remembering his wife as she was,
his little children, himself young and careless with his guitar, the flogging, the
chains, the wasted twenty-six years — and his anger against Makar is so great that
he longs for revenge even if he perishes for it.

**The tunnel.** For a fortnight Aksionov cannot sleep or bear to look at Makar. One
night he notices earth rolling out from under the sleeping-shelves. Makar crawls out
and, seeing he is discovered, admits he has dug a hole under the wall, carrying the
earth out in his boots. He threatens to kill Aksionov if he tells. Aksionov answers
that he has no wish to escape and no need to be killed, "you killed me long ago",
and that whether he informs or not will be as God directs. Next day the tunnel is
found. The Governor questions everybody; nobody will betray Makar, who would be
flogged to death. Finally the Governor turns to Aksionov, whom he knows to be a
truthful man, and asks him directly. Aksionov's lips and hands tremble. He thinks:
why should I screen the man who ruined my life? Then he answers, "I cannot say, your
honour. It is not God's will that I should tell. Do what you like with me; I am in
your hands." No pressure moves him further, and the matter is dropped.

**Forgiveness.** That night Makar comes and sits on his bed, and whispers, "Ivan
Dmitrich, forgive me!" He confesses that he killed the merchant and hid the knife in
Aksionov's bag, and that he had meant to kill Aksionov too but heard a noise and
escaped through the window. He kneels on the ground, beats his head on the floor and
begs to be forgiven, promising to confess so that Aksionov can go home. Aksionov
answers bitterly that after twenty-six years he has nowhere to go: his wife is dead
and his children have forgotten him. But when he hears Makar sobbing he begins to
weep himself and says, "God will forgive you! Maybe I am a hundred times worse than
you." At those words his heart grows light, the longing for home leaves him, and he
wishes only for his last hour to come. Makar confesses to the authorities in spite
of what Aksionov has said; but when the order for Aksionov's release arrives, he is
already dead.

## 3.3 Characters

| Character | Who they are | Role in the story |
|---|---|---|
| Ivan Dmitrich Aksionov | A young merchant of Vladimir; later an old convict called "Grandfather" and "The Saint" | Protagonist; innocent victim who becomes a moral hero |
| Makar Semyonich | A convict of about sixty, also from Vladimir; the real murderer | Antagonist, then penitent; the man Aksionov must forgive |
| Aksionov's wife | Unnamed; dreams of his grey hair; mother of several small children | Her single question — "was it not you?" — cuts him off from human help |
| The murdered merchant | A trader from Ryazan who shares the inn | His death sets the plot moving; he never speaks in the story |
| The police-officer | The official who arrives by troika and finds the knife | Represents human justice, which is confident and wrong |
| The Governor | The head of the Siberian prison | Offers Aksionov revenge in a form that would look like duty |
| Fellow prisoners | Convicts who make Aksionov their spokesman and judge | Show what Aksionov has become in twenty-six years |

**Aksionov.** Tolstoy gives us the young man in one paragraph — handsome,
curly-headed, "full of fun", a singer, once fond of drink — so that we can measure
what is destroyed. His transformation happens in two stages. The first is despair:
after his wife's question he gives up petitions and turns to God, not out of
saintliness but because every human door has shut. The second, twenty-six years
later, is a genuine choice. When the Governor asks who dug the tunnel, Aksionov
holds in his hands the revenge he had longed for, and refuses it. His words —
"It is not God's will that I should tell" — cost him nothing less than his last
chance of being the man who was right. Notice that his final act is not to claim
his innocence but to say, "Maybe I am a hundred times worse than you." That line,
not the confession, is the summit of the story.

**Makar Semyonich.** He is a coward and a liar before he is a penitent. He lets an
innocent man serve his sentence for twenty-six years, jokes about the knife in front
of the man whose life it ruined, and threatens murder a second time when his tunnel
is discovered. What breaks him is not punishment but mercy: he says himself that
being flogged with the knot was easier to bear than seeing the old man who could
have destroyed him and did not. Tolstoy's point is that Makar is changed by
precisely the thing the law cannot supply.

**The wife.** She appears in only two scenes and is given no name, yet she turns
the plot twice. Her dream is a warning the story's hero laughs at, and her question
in the prison — asked gently, while she strokes his hair — is the most damaging blow
he receives, worse than the flogging. Tolstoy is showing how loneliness is made: a
man can survive the courts, but not being doubted by the person who knows him best.

## 3.4 Themes

**Injustice, and what a man does with it.** The trial is over in two sentences;
Tolstoy is not interested in courtroom drama. He is interested in the twenty-six
years afterwards. Circumstantial evidence — a knife, an early departure, a frightened
manner — convicts an innocent man, and no appeal works, because his wife's petition
to the Czar is refused. From that point the only question the story asks is what
Aksionov will become.

**Forgiveness as release.** Aksionov forgives Makar not when he is calm but when he
hears him sob. Tolstoy marks the effect in one plain sentence: "his heart grew
light, and the longing for home left him". Forgiveness in this story is not a favour
done to the offender; it is the moment the victim stops being a prisoner inside
himself. That is why he no longer wants release when release finally comes.

**Faith and patience — God sees the truth, but waits.** The proverb of the title is
the story's structure. The truth is known from the first night; it is simply not
spoken for twenty-six years. Tolstoy neither promises that justice will come in time
nor denies that it comes at all: the confession is made, and Aksionov is dead. The
comfort offered is not rescue but meaning.

**Suspicion and the destruction of trust.** Two suspicions run the plot. The
police-officer suspects Aksionov because he stammers and turns pale; his wife
suspects him because of a dream. Neither is malicious and both are catastrophic.
Tolstoy quietly shows how easily evidence is read to fit a story people have already
decided on.

**Revenge refused.** Aksionov is offered revenge in a socially respectable form: the
Governor, a lawful authority, asks him a direct question, and telling the truth would
have been his duty. Tolstoy makes the temptation as strong as possible — one honest
sentence would have had Makar flogged nearly to death — precisely so that refusing it
means something.

## 3.5 Setting, structure and point of view

**Setting.** Nineteenth-century Russia, described with the minimum of detail: the
merchant town of Vladimir, the great fair at Nizhny, an inn on the road, and then a
convict prison in Siberia. Siberia is not described at all — no cold, no landscape,
no work in the mines. Tolstoy deliberately refuses atmosphere, because the story is
happening inside a man and scenery would only distract.

**Structure.** The plot is built as a chain of five decisions, four of them taken by
Aksionov: to travel on the day of the dream; to stop petitioning and pray; to keep
silent when Makar threatens him; to keep silent when the Governor questions him; and
Makar's own decision to confess. The chronology is strictly linear except that
twenty-six years pass in about a hundred words. This compression is the story's most
striking technical feature: it tells you that the years themselves are not the
subject.

**Point of view.** Third-person limited. The narrator stays close to Aksionov,
reporting his memories on the sleepless night and his exact thoughts before he answers
the Governor, but never enters Makar's mind — which is why Makar's arrival at the
bedside is a surprise. Tolstoy makes no moral comment in his own voice; the proverb in
the title does all the commentary the story allows itself.

## 3.6 Literary devices

| Device | Example from the text | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Foreshadowing | The wife's dream that his hair had turned grey | Plants the ending in the first page; his hair does turn white, but in Siberia |
| Dramatic irony | Aksionov alone knows who dug the tunnel; the Governor does not | Makes the reader share the weight of a decision the other characters cannot see |
| Situational irony | The order for release arrives after Aksionov's death; his innocence is proved when it can help nobody | Shows that justice and timeliness are different things |
| Symbolism | The blood-stained knife; the grey hair; *The Lives of the Saints* | Compresses guilt, lost time and spiritual change into single objects |
| Proverb as title | "God sees the truth, but waits" | States the theme without the author having to preach in the text |
| Simple diction and short sentences | "He was flogged with a knot"; "She was dead" | The bareness makes the events feel like facts rather than fiction |
| Contrast | The curly-headed young singer against the stooping old man who never laughs | Measures the cost of the injustice without a word of protest |
| Understatement | "Maybe I am a hundred times worse than you" | Gives the emotional climax to a quiet sentence instead of a dramatic one |

::: caution The commonest misreading
Many students write that the story ends happily because the truth comes out. It does
not. Aksionov's wife is dead, his children have forgotten him, and he himself dies
before the release order arrives. Tolstoy's consolation is inward: the change in
Aksionov's heart, not the change in his sentence. Say so, and say it in the answer's
last line.
:::

::: tip Exam technique
A five-mark question on this story almost always wants the *inner* plot, not the
outer one. Structure your answer as: the wrong done to him — how he responds to it —
the moment of temptation — the act of forgiveness — the result of that act. Quote the
two short sentences that mark the turning points: "It is not God's will that I should
tell" and "God will forgive you!"

## Chapter summary

- Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910), Russian realist and moral thinker; "God Sees the Truth,
  but Waits" (1872) takes its title from a Russian proverb.
- Aksionov, a merchant of Vladimir, ignores his wife's warning dream and travels to
  the Nizhny fair; a fellow merchant is murdered at the inn.
- A blood-stained knife is found in Aksionov's bag; he is flogged and sent to Siberia
  for twenty-six years, charged with a murder he did not commit.
- His wife's question, "was it not you who did it?", ends his hope of human help; he
  stops petitioning and turns to prayer.
- In prison he becomes "Grandfather" and "The Saint"; Makar Semyonich, the real
  murderer, arrives and gives himself away.
- Aksionov refuses to betray Makar's escape tunnel to the Governor, and later forgives
  him — "God will forgive you! Maybe I am a hundred times worse than you."
- Makar confesses, but Aksionov dies before the release order arrives.
- Themes: injustice; forgiveness as release; faith and patience; suspicion; revenge
  refused. Devices: foreshadowing, dramatic and situational irony, symbolism,
  understatement, extreme plainness of style.

## Practice questions

**Short answer questions (2 marks each)**

1. What dream did Aksionov's wife have, and how did he react to it? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
2. What evidence convinced the police-officer that Aksionov was the murderer? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
3. Why did Aksionov stop sending petitions to the Czar? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
4. How did Makar Semyonich betray, without meaning to, that he knew about the murder? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. Why did Aksionov refuse to tell the Governor who had dug the tunnel? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. What happened to Aksionov after Makar confessed to the authorities? <span class="marks">[2]</span>

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** She dreamt that he returned from the town and that when he took off his cap
his hair had turned completely grey, and she begged him not to start that day.
Aksionov laughed at her fear, joked that she was afraid he would go on a spree at
the fair, called the dream a lucky sign and drove away.

**2.** The police-officer found a blood-stained knife in Aksionov's bag. Added to
this were the facts that Aksionov had slept in the room next to the murdered
merchant, that the house was locked from inside, and that he had left the inn before
dawn. His pale face and broken voice were taken as further proof.

**3.** Because his own wife asked him whether he had really committed the murder.
Once the person closest to him doubted him, he decided that only God could know the
truth and that it was to God alone he should appeal. He gave up all hope of help
from men and simply prayed.

**4.** He laughed at the idea that anyone could have hidden a knife in the bag under
a sleeping man's head without waking him — a detail only the murderer could know. He
also exclaimed how wonderful it was that they should meet, and asked how Aksionov
had grown so old, but refused to explain his surprise.

**5.** Telling the truth would have had Makar flogged almost to death, and Aksionov
would not take that revenge. He replied that it was not God's will that he should
tell, and that the Governor could do as he liked with him. He had also decided,
after Makar's threat, to leave the matter "as God shall direct".

**6.** Nothing that could help him. Makar confessed his guilt in spite of Aksionov's
words, but by the time the official order for Aksionov's release arrived, Aksionov
was already dead. His innocence was proved only after his death.
:::

**Long answer questions (5 marks each)**

1. Discuss the theme of forgiveness in "God Sees the Truth, but Waits". <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Trace the character development of Ivan Dmitrich Aksionov through the story. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Justify the title of the story with reference to its events. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** Forgiveness in this story is not a polite word but a costly act, and Tolstoy
builds the whole plot so that we can measure its cost. Aksionov has lost twenty-six
years, his business, his wife and his children to a crime he did not commit, and he
knows exactly who is responsible. When the escape tunnel is discovered, he is given
a perfect revenge: a lawful authority asks him a direct question, and one honest
sentence would have had Makar flogged nearly to death, with nobody able to blame the
informer. He refuses, saying only, "It is not God's will that I should tell." The
second and greater act comes that night. Makar kneels, beats his head on the floor
and begs pardon, and Aksionov at first answers bitterly that he has nowhere left to
go. Then he hears the man sobbing, weeps himself, and says, "God will forgive you!
Maybe I am a hundred times worse than you." Tolstoy records the result in one plain
sentence: his heart grew light and the longing for home left him. Forgiveness is
therefore shown as something that frees the victim, not the offender. It changes
Makar too — he confesses although he has been forgiven — but its first effect is on
Aksionov, who no longer needs the freedom he had wanted for twenty-six years.

**2.** Aksionov begins as a prosperous young merchant of Vladimir: handsome,
fair-haired, full of fun, fond of singing, once given to drink but settled since his
marriage. He is confident to the point of carelessness, laughing at his wife's dream
and promising her presents from the fair. The arrest destroys this man. Frightened
and unable to explain the knife, he stammers and trembles, and his very innocence
makes him look guilty. The second stage is despair. His wife's question — "was it
not you who did it?" — hurts him more than the flogging, and after it he stops
writing petitions, gives up hope of human help and turns entirely to prayer. Twenty-
six years then pass in a few lines: his hair goes white as snow, he stoops, speaks
little and never laughs, learns to make boots, reads *The Lives of the Saints*, and
is called "Grandfather" and "The Saint" by prisoners who bring their quarrels to him
to judge. But Tolstoy does not let this saintliness go untested. When Makar arrives,
Aksionov's old rage returns so violently that he longs for vengeance even if he
should perish for it. His greatness lies in what he does with that rage: he keeps
silent before the Governor and then forgives the sobbing man at his bedside. The
final Aksionov wants neither revenge nor release, only his last hour.

**3.** The title is a Russian proverb, and the story is arranged to prove both halves
of it. "God sees the truth": from the first night, the truth about the murder exists
and is known — by Makar Semyonich, and, in the story's religious sense, by God. No
human being sees it. The police-officer is honest and completely wrong; the court
convicts on a knife and a frightened face; even Aksionov's wife suspects him. The
second half of the proverb is the harder one: "but waits". The truth is not spoken
for twenty-six years. During that time an innocent man is flogged, sent to the mines,
loses his wife and is forgotten by his children. Tolstoy refuses to soften this. When
the truth is finally told, it arrives too late for any earthly repair: Makar
confesses, the release order is written, and Aksionov is already dead. The title
therefore promises exactly what the story delivers — not rescue, but certainty. What
Aksionov gains in the meantime is not justice but peace, and the story suggests that
this, and not the pardon, is the real answer to his suffering.
:::

**Reference to the context**

1. "It seems that only God can know the truth; it is to Him alone we must appeal,
   and from Him alone expect mercy."

   (a) Who says this, and when? (b) What has just happened to make him say it?
   (c) What decision follows from this thought? (d) How does the rest of the story
   test this belief? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

2. "God will forgive you! Maybe I am a hundred times worse than you."

   (a) Who is the speaker and who is being addressed? (b) What confession has just
   been made? (c) What effect do these words have on the speaker himself?
   (d) Why is this moment considered the climax of the story? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — reference to the context
**1.** (a) Ivan Dmitrich Aksionov says it to himself in prison, before his trial,
just after his wife and children have been sent away at the end of their only visit.
(b) His wife had told him her petition to the Czar had been refused, reminded him of
her dream, and then asked him gently whether it was not he who had committed the
murder. Being suspected by his own wife convinced him that no human being would ever
believe him. (c) He decides to write no more petitions. He gives up all hope of help
from men and from then on only prays. (d) The rest of the story tests the belief
twice. In Siberia he must decide whether to hand Makar to the Governor, and he
refuses, leaving the matter to God's will; and at the end the truth does come out,
but so late that it cannot restore his wife, his children or his life. The story
neither rewards his faith with rescue nor mocks it — it gives him inward peace
instead.

**2.** (a) Aksionov speaks the words to Makar Semyonich, the convict who has come at
night and knelt beside his bed in the Siberian prison. (b) Makar has confessed that
it was he who killed the merchant from Ryazan and hid the blood-stained knife in
Aksionov's bag, and that he had intended to kill Aksionov too but heard a noise and
escaped through the window. He begs forgiveness and offers to confess publicly so
that Aksionov can go home. (c) Aksionov, who has just told Makar bitterly that he has
nowhere to go, hears him sobbing and begins to weep himself. After speaking these
words his heart grows light, the longing for home leaves him, and he desires nothing
except his last hour. (d) It is the climax because the story's real conflict is
inside Aksionov, not in the courts. He had longed for vengeance so fiercely that he
was ready to perish for it; here he surrenders the last of it, and places himself
lower than the man who ruined him. Everything afterwards — Makar's confession, the
release order, Aksionov's death — is consequence rather than struggle.
