R. K. Narayan's "An Astrologer's Day" takes one evening in the working life of a
street fortune-teller who knows nothing whatever about the stars. A single customer
arrives, and by the end of the story we discover why this particular fraud is able
to tell this particular man the exact truth about his past. The story is on the
syllabus as a model of compact plotting: every detail in the opening page turns out,
by the last page, to have been necessary.

::: key The central idea
A cheat gives an honest reading, and a man pays his own would-be murderer for the
news that his murderer is dead. Narayan builds the whole story on one perfectly
placed piece of irony.
:::

## 7.1 About the author

R. K. Narayan (1906–2001) — Rasipuram Krishnaswami Narayanaswami — was one of the
first Indian writers in English to be read all over the world. He was born in
Madras, now Chennai, brought up largely by his grandmother, and spent most of his
life in Mysore. He began as a schoolteacher and a newspaper reporter before turning
entirely to fiction.

His first novel, *Swami and Friends* (1935), was accepted only because the English
novelist Graham Greene read the manuscript and found a publisher for it; the two
remained friends for life. In that first book Narayan invented Malgudi, an imaginary
small town in south India in which almost all his later work is set. The Malgudi
novels include *The Bachelor of Arts* (1937), *The English Teacher* (1945), *The
Financial Expert* (1952), *The Guide* (1958), which won the Sahitya Akademi Award,
*The Man-Eater of Malgudi* (1961) and *The Vendor of Sweets* (1967). He also wrote
many short stories, collected in *An Astrologer's Day and Other Stories* (1947) and
*Malgudi Days*, and prose retellings of the *Ramayana* and the *Mahabharata*. He died
in Chennai in 2001.

::: definition Malgudi
The fictional south Indian town Narayan invented and used for sixty years. It has
its own streets, river, school and Town Hall Road, and it allowed him to write about
ordinary Indian life without tying his stories to any real place.
:::

What is distinctive about Narayan's voice is its gentle irony. He writes very plain
English, describes small people doing small things — a sign painter, a sweet seller,
a guide, a street astrologer — and lets their self-deceptions show without ever
mocking them. There is no anger in his satire and no preaching in his morality. He
is also a master of the quiet, exact detail, and in a story as short as this one,
every detail is doing work.

## 7.2 The story

**The astrologer at work.** The astrologer opens his business punctually at midday
and spreads out his equipment: cowrie shells, a square of cloth covered with
mysterious charts and symbols, a notebook, and a bundle of palmyra writing. His
forehead shines with sacred ash and vermilion, his eyes gleam, and a saffron turban
is wound round his head. Narayan makes it clear that all of this is stagecraft:
these things attract customers "as a bee is attracted to a lotus" — they are the
tools of a trade, not of a science.

**Where he sits.** His place is under a tamarind tree beside a path through the Town
Hall Road park, in the middle of a noisy row of sellers: a man selling medicine, a
man selling stolen hardware, an auctioneer of cheap cloth, a magician, and a vendor
of fried groundnuts. There is no municipal lighting here, so the sellers work by
flares, hissing gaslights and smoky lanterns, and the astrologer's own face is lit
only by the shifting glare from the groundnut vendor's flare. This half-light, as
the story will show, is part of his business.

**A fraud with a method.** Narayan then tells us plainly that the astrologer knows
no more about the stars than any of his customers. What he has instead is a "working
analysis of mankind's troubles: marriage, money and the tangles of human ties", a
quick eye, a willingness to listen, and the habit of letting the client talk for ten
minutes before answering. His success is built on shrewd guessing, flattery and
common sense, and his customers go away satisfied. We are also told, in one short
paragraph that we do not yet understand, that he had never intended to be an
astrologer at all: he had left his village suddenly, without telling anyone, and but
for that he would have lived and died a farmer among his fields and cattle.

**The last customer.** The evening's business has been poor. The astrologer is
packing up, about to go home with very little, when a man stops in front of his mat.
The astrologer, unwilling to lose a customer, begins his usual patter; the man is
aggressive and scornful, and challenges him to say something worth hearing. They
argue over the fee and end by making a wager: the stranger will pay a rupee if he is
satisfied, and the astrologer must pay back double if he is proved wrong. The
astrologer feels uneasy about the bargain and tries to get out of it, but the
stranger grips his wrist and holds him to it.

**The face in the matchlight.** The stranger strikes a match to light a cheroot, and
for a moment the flame lights his face. The astrologer sees it, and everything
changes. He becomes anxious to escape, offers to call the wager off, and is refused.

**The reading.** Then he begins to speak, and what he says is not guesswork. He
tells the man that he was once left for dead, that a knife had gone through him, and
that he was pushed into a well and abandoned there. The stranger is astonished: it
is all exactly true, and he pulls up his shirt to show the scar. Finally the
astrologer names him — Guru Nayak — and the man is convinced that he is in the
presence of real power.

**The lie that saves a life.** Guru Nayak explains what he is looking for: he has
searched for years for the man who attacked him, and he intends to kill him with his
own hands. The astrologer tells him he is too late. The man he is hunting, he says,
died four months ago in a far-off town, crushed under a lorry. Then he adds a piece
of advice: Guru Nayak must go straight home tonight and never travel southward
again, or his own life will be in danger — "Never travel southward again, and you
will live to be a hundred." Disappointed but satisfied, Guru Nayak throws down a
handful of coins, grumbles that he had wanted to kill the man himself, and goes
away.

**At home.** The astrologer reaches home very late. His wife is waiting anxiously at
the door with their child. He hands over the money, and she is delighted — she
counts it and says she will buy jaggery and coconut for the child, who has been
asking for sweets for days. But the astrologer is irritated: the man had promised a
rupee and given only twelve and a half annas, cheating him even in this.

**The confession.** Then he tells her something he has never told her. A great load
has gone off him today, he says, because he had always believed he had "the blood of
a man on my hands all these years". Years ago, in his village, in a drunken quarrel
over gambling, he had stabbed a man, pushed him into a well and run away, certain he
had killed him. That, we now realise, was why he left his village without a word,
why he came to this town, and why he married her and took up a false trade. Tonight
he has discovered that the man is alive — and the man has just paid him to be told
that his enemy is dead. Having said all this, the astrologer yawns, remarks that he
is sleepy, and goes to bed.

## 7.3 Characters

| Character | Who they are | Role in the story |
|---|---|---|
| The astrologer | An unnamed street fortune-teller in his village disguise; a farmer's son, a fugitive, a husband and father | The protagonist: a fraud whose one honest reading saves two lives |
| Guru Nayak | A hard, aggressive man who has searched for years for the person who tried to kill him | The customer, the victim and the hunter — and the only person in the story who is deceived |
| The astrologer's wife | A practical, affectionate woman who waits at the door with their child | Represents the ordinary life he built on top of the secret |
| The other sellers | The medicine man, the magician, the auctioneer, the groundnut vendor | Create the noisy, half-lit market world in which the astrologer can hide |

**The astrologer.** Narayan builds him in three layers. On the surface he is a
performer: the ash and vermilion, the saffron turban, the cowrie shells and charts
are all "professional equipment", carefully chosen to produce belief. Underneath
that he is a shrewd, observant man with no supernatural gift at all, who has
studied human beings instead of the stars and knows that most people's troubles come
down to marriage, money and family quarrels. Underneath *that* is a frightened man
who has spent years believing himself a murderer. All three layers are visible in
the scene with Guru Nayak: he takes the wager out of greed, recognises the face and
tries to escape out of fear, and then uses his professional skill with complete
coolness to send his enemy away in the wrong direction for the rest of his life. He
is not a good man, but he is not a monster either — he is an ordinary person
managing the consequences of one terrible night, which is exactly how Narayan sees
most people.

**Guru Nayak.** He is loud, suspicious and violent. He challenges the astrologer
rather than consulting him, grips his wrist to hold him to the bargain, and states
openly that he means to kill the man he is hunting with his own hands. This
aggression is what makes the ending credible: the astrologer cannot confess, because
confession would mean death. Guru Nayak is also, unknowingly, the story's biggest
loser. He gets a scar, years of searching, a false answer and a warning that will
keep him away from the south for the rest of his life, and he pays for all of it.

**The astrologer's wife.** She appears for only a page, but she gives the story its
warmth and its scale. Her pleasure at the money is completely practical — jaggery
and coconut for a child who has been asking for sweets — and her horror when she
hears what her husband did ("you tried to kill!") is the only moral judgement anyone
makes. Through her we see what the astrologer has been protecting: a small, poor,
ordinary happiness.

## 7.4 Themes

**Appearance and reality.** Almost nothing in the story is what it looks like. A man
who claims to read the stars cannot read them; a "reading" that seems supernatural
is simple memory; a satisfied customer has been comprehensively cheated; and a
fortune-teller who deals in others' futures is running from his own past. Even the
setting is double, since the astrologer works in a light that reveals and conceals
at the same time.

**Irony and chance.** The central irony is perfect and needs no comment from the
narrator: the only client the astrologer can read truthfully is the one man he
should never have met. Around it Narayan arranges smaller ironies — the victim pays
the criminal; the criminal is set free from guilt by the man he wronged; and the
astrologer complains about being cheated of a few annas by a man he once tried to
kill. Chance rules all of it. Had business been better, the astrologer would have
packed up before Guru Nayak arrived.

**Guilt, and the wish to be free of it.** The most human moment in the story is at
home, when the astrologer tells his wife that a great load has gone off him today.
For years he has believed he had a man's blood on his hands; that belief drove him
out of his village, changed his trade and shaped his whole life. Narayan is
interested less in the crime than in what carrying it has cost, and in how easily
the load lifts once the fact changes.

**Survival by wits.** The astrologer's profession is a dishonest one, but Narayan
shows it as work. The man sits out every day next to other struggling sellers, he
studies his customers, and he earns a few annas at a time to feed a wife and child.
When his life is in danger, the same skills save him. Narayan neither approves nor
condemns; he simply shows how a poor man survives.

**Superstition and the people who live off it.** The crowd who buy the astrologer's
advice want certainty, and they are willing to pay for it. Narayan's satire is
gentle: the customers get, in fact, sensible common-sense counsel and go away
comforted, and the story's best piece of advice — never travel southward again — is
both a complete fiction and the thing that will keep Guru Nayak alive.

## 7.5 Setting, structure and point of view

**Setting.** A busy footpath through the Town Hall Road park, under a tamarind tree,
in an unnamed south Indian town that readers of Narayan recognise as Malgudi. The
time is important: the story runs from midday to night, and almost all of it takes
place after dark. Because there is no municipal lighting in that part of the park,
the only light comes from the sellers' flares and lanterns, so faces are visible
only in flashes. That half-darkness is not decoration. It is what allows the
astrologer to work unrecognised for years, and it is a single match that finally
betrays his customer's face to him.

**Structure.** The story is very tightly built, and it divides into three parts: the
description of the astrologer and his trade; the encounter with Guru Nayak; and the
short domestic scene at home. Narayan plants his information early and casually —
the man's ignorance of astrology, his skill at reading people, and the fact that he
left his village suddenly and never meant to do this work at all — and none of it
looks important at the time. The twist at the end makes every one of those details
snap into place. Notice too that the story's earliest event in time, the stabbing in
the village, is the last thing we are told.

**Point of view.** Third person, following the astrologer, but with the narrator
deliberately holding something back. We are allowed to see his uneasiness when the
match flares, and his hurry to end the wager, without being told why. This creates
suspense honestly: the narrator never lies, he simply postpones. The information is
released at last through dialogue with the wife, so that the reader learns the truth
in the same breath as she does.

::: definition Twist ending
An ending that suddenly reveals information which changes the meaning of everything
before it. A fair twist, like Narayan's, is one the reader could have worked out
from clues already given.
:::

## 7.6 Literary devices

| Device | Example from the text | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Situational irony | The fraud gives a completely accurate reading | The story's central device: he can only be accurate because he is guilty |
| Situational irony | Guru Nayak pays his own attacker to hear that his attacker is dead | The victim finances his own deception |
| Dramatic irony | In the last page the reader knows what Guru Nayak will never know | Makes us complicit with the astrologer |
| Light and darkness | Flares, lanterns, the absence of municipal lighting, the lit match | Concealment and revelation are built into the setting |
| Symbolism | The "professional equipment" — ash, turban, cowrie shells, charts | Appearance manufactured to create belief |
| Foreshadowing | He left his village suddenly; he never meant to be an astrologer | Plants the ending in the opening pages without exposing it |
| Suspense through withholding | His panic when the match is struck, unexplained for two pages | The reader knows something is wrong before knowing what |
| Simile | Customers are attracted "as a bee is attracted to a lotus" | Shows his trade as an entirely natural, mechanical process |
| Understatement | He yawns, says he is sleepy, and goes to bed | The calm closing line measures how far the burden has lifted |
| Gentle satire | The whole business of street astrology | Narayan laughs at the trade without despising the people in it |

::: caution Two mistakes to avoid
(i) Do not say that the astrologer has real powers. Narayan states in the first page
that he knows nothing about the stars; the accuracy of the reading has an entirely
natural explanation, and missing that is missing the story.
(ii) Do not call the ending a coincidence and stop there. It *is* a coincidence, but
the exam answer must go on to show how carefully Narayan prepares for it — the
darkness, the match, the unexplained flight from the village — so that it reads as
inevitable rather than accidental.
:::

## Chapter summary

- R. K. Narayan (1906–2001), Indian novelist and story writer, creator of the
  imaginary town of Malgudi; "An Astrologer's Day" is the title story of his 1947
  collection.
- The astrologer works under a tamarind tree on Town Hall Road, among other street
  sellers, lit only by their flares; his ash, vermilion, turban, cowrie shells and
  charts are pure stagecraft.
- He knows nothing about astrology. He succeeds through observation, flattery and a
  "working analysis of mankind's troubles: marriage, money and the tangles of human
  ties".
- A last customer, Guru Nayak, challenges him and they make a wager; a struck match
  shows the astrologer the man's face and he tries to call the bet off.
- He then tells Guru Nayak, accurately, that he was stabbed, pushed into a well and
  left for dead, and names him.
- He lies that the enemy died four months ago under a lorry, and warns him never to
  travel southward again. Guru Nayak pays and leaves.
- At home he gives the money to his wife, complains of being cheated of a few annas,
  and finally tells her that he had believed for years that he had "the blood of a
  man" on his hands — he was the attacker.
- Themes: appearance and reality, irony and chance, guilt and relief, survival by
  wits, and superstition; devices include situational and dramatic irony,
  light-and-dark imagery, foreshadowing, withheld information and a twist ending.

## Practice questions

**Short answer questions (2 marks each)**

1. What does the astrologer's "professional equipment" consist of, and why does he need it? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
2. How does the astrologer manage to satisfy his customers without knowing astrology? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
3. What is the wager between the astrologer and Guru Nayak? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
4. Why does the astrologer try to back out of the bargain after the match is struck? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. What advice does the astrologer give Guru Nayak at the end of the reading, and why? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. Why does the astrologer say that a great load has gone off him? <span class="marks">[2]</span>

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** It consists of a dozen cowrie shells, a square piece of cloth covered with
mystic charts, a notebook and palmyra writing, together with the sacred ash and
vermilion on his forehead and a saffron turban. None of it has any use except
display: it makes him look like a holy man with occult knowledge, and so it draws
customers to his mat.

**2.** He relies on shrewd guesswork rather than the stars. He lets the customer talk
for about ten minutes first, watches him closely, and then gives advice based on what
Narayan calls a working analysis of mankind's troubles — marriage, money and the
tangles of human ties. Since his answers are flattering and sensible, the customers
go away satisfied.

**3.** Guru Nayak challenges him to say something worth hearing. They agree that if
the astrologer answers his questions correctly he will be paid a rupee, and that if
he is proved wrong he must pay back double. Guru Nayak grips his wrist to hold him
to the terms.

**4.** Because the flame of the match lights the stranger's face, and the astrologer
recognises the man he stabbed and threw into a well years before in his own village.
He realises he is sitting in front of someone who has come to kill him, and he wants
to get away before he is recognised in turn.

**5.** He tells him that the man he has been hunting was crushed under a lorry four
months ago in a far-off town, and warns him to go home at once and never travel
southward again, promising that he will then live to be a hundred. The advice is a
lie, and its purpose is to keep Guru Nayak permanently away from the town where his
attacker actually lives.

**6.** Because he has discovered that Guru Nayak is alive. For years he had believed
he had killed a man in his village and had been living with the fear and guilt of
it — indeed it was why he ran away, changed his life and became an astrologer.
Meeting his victim alive removes that burden at a stroke, and he goes to bed calm
enough to yawn.
:::

**Long answer questions (5 marks each)**

1. Discuss the irony in "An Astrologer's Day". <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Sketch the character of the astrologer. What kind of man is he? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. How does Narayan prepare the reader for the twist at the end of the story? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** Irony is not one feature of this story; it is the whole design. The largest
example is situational. The astrologer is an admitted fraud who, as Narayan tells us
on the first page, knows nothing whatever about the stars — and yet the reading he
gives Guru Nayak is entirely accurate, down to the knife, the well and the man's own
name. He can be accurate for one reason only: he is the criminal. A second irony
follows immediately. Guru Nayak has spent years searching for the man who tried to
kill him, and he pays that very man twelve and a half annas to be told that his
enemy is dead; the victim finances his own deception, and goes away pleased. A third
is quieter but sharper: the astrologer, who once tried to murder this man, is
genuinely annoyed at being cheated of a few small coins by him. There is dramatic
irony too, since in the closing scene the reader knows the truth while Guru Nayak
never will, and will keep away from the south for the rest of his life on the
strength of a lie. Finally there is the irony that the lie does real good: it saves
the astrologer's life, prevents a murder, and buys jaggery and coconut for a child.
Narayan never points any of this out. He lets the facts sit side by side, which is
what makes the ending so satisfying.

**2.** The astrologer is a poor man living by his wits, and Narayan draws him without
either admiration or contempt. Professionally he is a fake, and he knows it. The
sacred ash, the vermilion, the saffron turban, the cowrie shells and the charts are
described frankly as equipment, chosen because they attract customers as a lotus
attracts bees. Yet he is not stupid or lazy: he is genuinely skilled at reading
people, he listens before he speaks, and his understanding that most human unhappiness
comes down to marriage, money and family quarrels is not a bad working philosophy.
Morally he is a mixture. He tried to kill a man in a drunken quarrel over gambling
and ran away instead of facing the consequences; he lies to Guru Nayak for his own
safety; he is sharp enough, at the end of a terrifying evening, to count his money
and complain. But he is also a husband and father supporting a family on a few annas
a day, and his relief at learning that he is not a murderer is completely sincere.
What finally defines him is competence under pressure. In the space of a few minutes
he recognises his enemy, sees that confession means death, and constructs a story
that disposes of the danger for ever. He is an ordinary man with an extraordinary
secret, which is Narayan's usual subject.

**3.** Narayan plays fair with the reader. Every fact needed for the ending is
supplied early, but none of it is emphasised. In the opening pages we are told
plainly that the astrologer has no knowledge of the stars, which is what makes the
later accuracy of his reading significant rather than magical. We are also told,
almost in passing, that he had never intended to take up this work, that he would
otherwise have lived and died a farmer, and that he left his village abruptly
without telling anybody — three sentences that mean nothing on a first reading and
everything on a second. The setting is arranged with equal care: the park has no
municipal lighting, the sellers work by flares and lanterns, and faces are visible
only in flashes, so that a man may sit there for years unrecognised. Narayan then
uses one struck match, lit for a cheroot, to break that protection at precisely the
right moment. The astrologer's reaction is shown but not explained — he suddenly
wants the wager cancelled, he is uneasy, he tries to leave — which creates suspense
without cheating. Even the wager itself is necessary, since Guru Nayak's grip on his
wrist traps him into speaking. When the truth finally comes out in the conversation
with his wife, the reader does not feel tricked but enlightened, because the clues
were all in plain sight.

1. "a working analysis of mankind's troubles: marriage, money and the tangles of human ties"

   (a) Who is being described here? (b) What does the phrase mean? (c) How is this
   knowledge used in his trade? (d) Why is this description important for the story's

2. "Never travel southward again, and you will live to be a hundred."

   (a) Who says this, and to whom? (b) What has the speaker just told the listener?
   (c) Why does the speaker give this particular advice? (d) Bring out the irony of
   the words. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — reference to the context
**1.** (a) The astrologer, who sits under a tamarind tree on Town Hall Road and
earns his living telling fortunes, although he has no knowledge at all of astrology.
(b) It means that instead of studying the stars he has studied people, and has
learned that nearly every trouble a customer brings him concerns a marriage, a
question of money, or a quarrel within a family. (c) He uses it as a system. He lets
the client speak for about ten minutes, observes him closely, and then produces
answers that fit one of those three categories, delivered with confidence and a
little flattery, so that the customer leaves satisfied and pays. (d) It is essential
preparation for the ending. Because Narayan has established that the man is a fraud
with no occult powers whatever, his astonishingly exact reading of Guru Nayak cannot
be explained as magic. The reader is therefore forced to look for a natural
explanation, and finds it in the confession at home.

**2.** (a) The astrologer says it to his last customer of the evening, Guru Nayak,
just before the man pays him and leaves. (b) He has told him, correctly, that he was
once stabbed, thrown into a well and left for dead, and has named him; then he has
told him a lie — that the man he has been hunting for years died four months ago,
crushed under a lorry in a distant town. (c) Because Guru Nayak has said openly that
he means to kill his attacker with his own hands, and the attacker is the astrologer
himself. Sending the man home for good, with a solemn warning never to come south
again, is the only way to make sure they never meet a second time. (d) The irony is
double. The advice is entirely false as prophecy and yet entirely true as
prediction: if Guru Nayak stays away he really will live longer, because the man he
wants to kill is the man giving him the advice. And the warning is paid for by the
person it is designed to deceive, who walks away grateful to his own would-be
murderer.
