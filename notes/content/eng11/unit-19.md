Two old people quarrel over a park bench. Within twenty minutes they have
discovered that fifty years ago they were lovers — and each decides, separately
and silently, not to say so. *A Sunny Morning* is a comedy that turns, without a
single raised voice, into one of the gentlest love scenes in modern drama.

::: key What the exam wants from this play
The **plot** in order (the birds, the quarrel, the snuff, the poem, Maricela, the
two lies, the violets); **when and how** each recognises the other; **why**
neither confesses; the **two invented deaths** and what they reveal; the
**humour and irony**; the **symbols** — the sunny morning, autumn, the birds, the
violets, the Campoamor verses; and the justification of the **title**.
:::

## 19.1 About the authors

**Serafín Álvarez Quintero (1871–1938)** and **Joaquín Álvarez Quintero
(1873–1944)** were Spanish dramatists — brothers who wrote everything together and
never published a line separately. They grew up in Utrera, a small town near
Seville in the southern Spanish region of **Andalusia**, and began writing for the
theatre while still very young. In Madrid they became so successful so early that
they were known as **"the Golden Boys of the Madrid theatre."**

Between them they wrote almost **200 plays**, nearly all of them depicting the
life, manners and speech of Andalusia. Their first stage piece, *Gilito*, was
written in 1889. Their best-known works are the comedies *The Flowers* (1901), *A
Sunny Morning* (1905) and *The Merry Heart* (1906), together with the
uncharacteristically serious *Malvaloca* (1912). Several of their plays were
translated into English by Helen and Harley Granville-Barker (1927–32), and their
complete works appeared in seven volumes as *Obras Completas* in the early 1950s.

*A Sunny Morning* (in Spanish, *Mañana de sol*) was first staged in 1905. The
version in your textbook is the English translation by **Lucretia Xavier Floyd**.
It is a *paso de comedia* — a short comic sketch built on one situation, two
characters and a single change of feeling — and it is the brothers' most famous
piece outside Spain.

::: caution Spelling of the names
Your textbook prints the names without Spanish accents: *Dona Laura*, *Don
Gonzalo*, *Serafin and Joaquin Alvarez Quintero*. In correct Spanish they are
*Doña*, *Serafín*, *Joaquín*, *Álvarez*. Write them as the question paper writes
them; both forms are accepted. **Don** and **Doña** are respectful titles placed
before a first name, roughly like "Sir" and "Madam" — so "Don Gonzalo" means
something close to "the gentleman Gonzalo".
:::

## 19.2 The characters

| Character | Who they are |
|---|---|
| **Dona Laura** | "a handsome, white-haired old lady of about seventy, refined in appearance", with bright eyes and undimmed wits; once Laura Llorente, "The Silver Maiden" of the villa Maricela |
| **Don Gonzalo** | "an old gentleman of seventy, gouty and impatient"; once the gallant horseman who fought a duel over her |
| **Petra** | Dona Laura's maid, about twenty, in love with a park guard |
| **Juanito** | Don Gonzalo's servant, who "plays havoc with the nursemaids" |

The two servants are not decoration. Petra's guard sends violets and Juanito's
flirting keeps him away from the bench; the young lovers' easy, thoughtless
courtship runs in the background of a love affair that was destroyed fifty years
ago. The violets that end the play come from Petra's romance, not from Laura's.

## 19.3 The play, step by step

### 1. The bench and the birds

The scene is "a retired corner of a park in Madrid. Autumn." Dona Laura arrives on
Petra's arm, pleased to find "my seat" unoccupied, and sends the girl off to her
guard with the instruction not to stay more than ten minutes. She then feeds the
pigeons with the tenderness of a woman who has nobody else to talk to: "These are
for the spryest, these for the gluttons, and these for the little ones which are
the most persistent… That one takes his piece and flies up to that branch alone.
He is a philosopher."

That little speech does a great deal of work. It shows her warmth, her humour, her
loneliness and her habit of turning everything into a small story — the same habit
that will later produce a magnificent lie about a woman drowning in the sea.

### 2. The quarrel

Don Gonzalo arrives grumbling about priests who are "idling their time away", wants
a bench to himself, orders Juanito to "rout them out", and then walks straight
through Laura's pigeons. She rebukes him — "You have scared away the birds who were
feeding on my crumbs" — and the two fall at once into a delightful bad-tempered
duel of words:

- "What do I care about the birds?" / "But I do."
- "This is a public park." / "Then why do you complain that the priests have taken
  your bench?"
- "Do you use your handkerchief as a shoe brush?" / "Why not?" / "Do you use a
  shoe brush as a handkerchief?"

He mutters that she is a "senile old lady" who "ought to be at home knitting and
counting her beads"; she tells him she is "not going to leave just to please you."
Both are showing off, and both are enjoying themselves far more than they admit.

### 3. The truce: snuff

He calls for his book and puts on "an enormous pair of glasses" plus a reading
glass; she says she thought he was taking out a telescope. They boast — he about
hunting hares and partridges and a wild boar's head in his study, she about a
tiger's skin in her boudoir. Then he offers her a pinch of snuff, they sneeze three
times each, and the quarrel is over. Her aside is one of the best lines in the
play: **"The snuff has made peace between us."**

It is worth noticing how the reconciliation is achieved — not by apology or
argument, but by a shared physical comedy and a small courtesy. The play's method
throughout is to let objects do the work that speeches would do in a lesser piece.

### 4. The poem

Don Gonzalo reads aloud from Ramón de Campoamor: "All love is sad, but sad as it
is, it is the best thing that we know," and then a line he takes to be humorous —
"The daughters of the mothers I once loved kiss me now as they would a graven
image." Laura laughs; the audience is beginning to be uneasy.

Then he reads a title: "Twenty years pass. He returns." She claims she can read
without glasses, takes the book, and recites:

> "Twenty years pass. He returns. And each, beholding the other, exclaims —
> Can it be that this is he? Heavens, is it she?"

Her aside gives the game away to the audience: **"I know every word by heart."**
This is superb dramatic construction. The poem describes exactly what is happening
on the bench; the audience understands this before either character does; and the
same two lines will come back as the final words of the play.

### 5. Recognition

Gonzalo mentions Valencia, his native city; Laura mentions a villa near the sea,
hidden among lemon and orange trees, called **Maricela**. He is startled. He
remembers the most beautiful woman he ever saw there — "Laura — Laura — Laura
Llorente." She is startled in turn, recovers, and says he has reminded her of "my
best friend."

From this moment both know, and each believes the other does not. They construct
the fiction together: he is speaking of his **cousin**, she of her **friend**.
Behind that thin screen they reconstruct their own love affair in perfect detail —
the window with the red roses, the horseman who tossed up a bouquet each morning
and caught the flowers she threw down each afternoon, the merchant her parents
wanted her to marry, the insult under the window, the duel at sunrise on the beach.

They even catch each other out: "You seem to know the story well." "And so do
you." And two asides confirm what the audience already knows: "This is Laura!"
and "Why tell him? He does not suspect."

### 6. The two lies

Each now has to explain why the lovers never met again, and each invents a
magnificent death.

**Gonzalo's version:** the letters were intercepted by Laura's parents, so she
never answered; Gonzalo, "in despair, believing his love lost to him forever,
joined the army, went to Africa, and there, in a trench, met a glorious death,
grasping the flag of Spain and whispering the name of his beloved Laura." His
private comment: "I could not have killed myself more gloriously." Hers: "What an
atrocious lie!"

**Laura's version:** the Silver Maiden waited "days, months, a year", then walked
at sunset to the beach, wrote his name on the sand, sat on a rock gazing at the
horizon, and was swept out to sea by the rising tide; the fishermen say it was a
long time before the waves washed the name away. Her private comment: "You will
not get ahead of me in decorating my own funeral." His: "She lies worse than I
do."

Then the two asides that tell the truth, and that always get the biggest laugh in
performance:

- LAURA: "I will not tell him that I married two years later."
- GONZALO: "In three months I ran off to Paris with a ballet dancer."

### 7. The violets, and the decision not to speak

The mood softens. They agree the morning has been curious, he admits "I was
unreasonable, perhaps", and she asks — "(Sweetly.)" — whether he is coming
tomorrow. He promises to bring crumbs for the birds.

Then, with their backs to one another, each makes the same decision in the same
words. Gonzalo: "No, no, I will not reveal myself. I am grotesque now. Better that
she recall the gallant horseman who passed daily beneath her window tossing
flowers." Laura: "No, I am too sadly changed. It is better he should remember me
as the black eyed girl tossing flowers as he passed among the roses in the
garden."

Petra brings violets from her guard, and a few fall as Laura takes them. The
lovers say goodbye "until tomorrow — if it is sunny." And then, "trembling and
with a great effort", Don Gonzalo stoops to pick up the fallen violets — and Laura
turns and sees him do it. The two asides land together: "Yes, it is he!" — "It is
she, and no mistake." They wave, quote Campoamor's two lines to each other, "smile
once more, as if she were again at the window and he below in the rose garden,"
and go out on the arms of their servants.

tenderness, and every stage of it is produced by a small physical object rather
than by a speech.

## 19.5 The two central questions

### When do they recognise each other?

This is the question examiners ask most often, and the answer has to be given in
stages, because the play deliberately separates *suspecting*, *knowing* and
*admitting*.

1. **The first tremor** comes with the name **Maricela**, at which Gonzalo is
   "(Startled.)", and then with **Laura Llorente** and "The Silver Maiden", at
   which Laura is startled in her turn. The stage direction says "They look at
   each other intently."
2. **Certainty** arrives during the story of the duel, and the play marks the
   exact moment with two asides: Gonzalo's "This is Laura!" and Laura's "Gonzalo!"
   From here each knows, and each wrongly believes the other has not guessed —
   "She is entirely innocent", "He does not suspect."
3. **Mutual, acknowledged recognition** is the last beat of the play. Gonzalo
   stoops painfully for the violets she has dropped; Laura turns and sees; and the
   asides finally agree — "Yes, it is he!" / "It is she, and no mistake."

So they recognise each other in the middle of the play, but they let each other
*know* it only in the final seconds, and even then without a word spoken directly.

### Why do neither of them tell the truth?

Two reasons, and the second is the important one.

**Vanity — but a tender kind.** Each is ashamed of what age has done. "I am
grotesque now," says Gonzalo. "I am too sadly changed," says Laura. Neither can
bear to be seen as the ruin of the person the other loved.

**Protection of the memory.** Look at how both sentences end. Gonzalo: "Better
that she recall the gallant horseman who passed daily beneath her window tossing
flowers." Laura: "It is better he should remember me as the black eyed girl
tossing flowers as he passed among the roses in the garden." Each is protecting
not his own dignity but *the other person's memory*. Confession would cost them
both the only beautiful thing they still possess. Their silence is therefore an
act of love, not of cowardice — and this is why the ending feels happy rather than
sad.

There is a third, smaller reason, and it is comic: each has told an enormous lie
about the other's death, and neither can now confess without also confessing the
lie.

## 19.6 Themes

**Love survives everything, including the lovers.** Fifty years, a duel, a
marriage, a ballet dancer in Paris and two elaborate lies have not touched what
Laura and Gonzalo felt. The proof is not a speech but an action: a gouty old man
bending down, "trembling and with a great effort", for three fallen violets.

**Age, and the comedy of it.** The play is completely honest about old age — the
enormous spectacles, the reading glass, the gout, the dragging feet, the
grumbling, the boasting about hunting. But it laughs *with* them, never at them.
The joke is that these two quarrelsome pensioners still have all the energy of
twenty-year-olds; they simply spend it on benches and birds instead of balconies
and duels.

**Illusion as kindness.** The lies in this play are not sins. They protect, they
flatter, and they preserve. The play quietly argues that what we remember matters
as much as what happened, and that it can be right to leave a beautiful memory
undisturbed.

**Appearance and reality.** Almost nothing in the play is what it first appears —
the rude old man is a devoted lover, the "cousin" is himself, the "best friend" is
herself, the glorious death in Africa was a trip to Paris, and the quarrel is a
courtship.

**Chance and Providence.** "The ways of Providence are strange," says Laura, and
"Fate is curious." The entire reunion depends on three priests refusing to give up
a bench.

**Youth beside age.** Petra and Juanito are flirting at the edge of the stage
throughout, and the violets that end the play are the young guard's gift. Love
looks careless and easy at twenty and costs a great effort at seventy; the play
puts both on stage at once.

## 19.7 Humour, irony and technique

**The comedy of bad temper.** The opening quarrel is pure comedy of manners: two
proud people competing over a public bench, each claiming ownership of what they
have just called public property. Gonzalo demands the priests be "routed out" from
"that one over there — it is mine", and thirty seconds later insists "the benches
here are public property." Laura catches him at it instantly.

**Repartee.** The dialogue is built on quick reversals in which the second speaker
turns the first speaker's own words around: shoe brush and handkerchief; "Yes,
kill time. That is all you kill"; the wild boar's head answered by a tiger's skin.

**Comic exaggeration.** The telescope-sized glasses, the sneezing three times each
in alternation, "You must have gone with Columbus in one of his caravels!", and
the two competing deaths — which are really a contest in melodrama, as their
asides admit.

**Dramatic irony**, and lots of it. The audience knows who they are long before
they admit it; the audience knows both stories are false; the audience knows that
each has guessed. So every line carries a second meaning. "The daughters of the
mothers I once loved kiss me now as they would a graven image" is read out as a
joke by a man who is sitting beside a woman he once loved. "You seem to know the
story well" is a trap that the speaker has also walked into. Perhaps the finest
touch is Gonzalo's calm "Very sad" when Laura mentions her friend's sad love
affair; the affair was his own.

**The aside.** This is the play's central device, and you should be able to name
it. An **aside** is a remark spoken to the audience which the other characters on
stage are supposed not to hear. The Quinteros use asides as a private channel of
truth running underneath the public channel of lies, and almost every important
fact in the play — the marriage, the ballet dancer, "This is Laura!", "I know
every word by heart" — reaches us that way.

**Narrated flashback.** There is no change of scene and no dramatised memory; the
past is delivered entirely through the two characters' talk about "my cousin" and
"my friend". This is far more effective than staging it would be, because the
reconstruction is also a recognition scene: with every detail they add, they come
closer to naming each other.

**Foreshadowing through the quoted poem.** "Twenty years pass. He returns. And
each, beholding the other, exclaims — Can it be that this is he? Heavens, is it
she?" The Campoamor lines predict the ending in the middle of the play, and by
giving those same lines to the two lovers as the last words of the play the
brothers turn a poem the audience has already heard into the curtain line.

**Symbolism.**
- **The sunny morning** — warmth and late happiness; the play's condition for
  meeting again is "if it is sunny."
- **Autumn** — the season of the year that matches the season of their lives:
  beautiful, mild, and late.
- **The birds and the crumbs** — Laura's need to give affection to something. His
  promise to bring crumbs tomorrow is the play's real proposal of marriage.
- **The violets** — a gift of love passed down from the young couple to the old
  one; picking them up is the play's one physical act of devotion.
- **The flowers of the past** — the bouquet thrown up to the balcony and thrown
  back down, which the last stage direction recalls: they smile "as if she were
  again at the window and he below in the rose garden."

| Device | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Aside | "In three months I ran off to Paris with a ballet dancer." | Gives the audience the truth the characters are hiding |
| Dramatic irony | "Very sad." (of his own love affair) | Makes ordinary sentences carry a hidden second meaning |
| Repartee | "Do you use a shoe brush as a handkerchief?" | Establishes that these two are equals, and enjoy the fight |
| Foreshadowing | "Twenty years pass. He returns…" | Predicts the ending and later supplies the curtain line |

::: definition Aside and soliloquy
An **aside** is a short remark a character speaks to the audience (or to himself)
which the other characters on stage are conventionally unable to hear. A
**soliloquy** is a whole speech delivered by a character alone on stage. *A Sunny
Morning* is built almost entirely on asides; there is no soliloquy in it, so do
not use that word in an answer about this play.
:::

::: tip The one-sentence answer to "what is this play about?"
It is about two people who recognise each other and choose not to say so, in order
to protect the memory each has of the other's youth. Every question about theme,
irony, title or ending can be answered from that sentence.
:::

## Chapter summary

- *A Sunny Morning* (*Mañana de sol*, 1905) is a one-act comedy by the Spanish
  brothers **Serafín (1871–1938)** and **Joaquín (1873–1944) Álvarez Quintero** of
  Andalusia, translated by Lucretia Xavier Floyd.
- Setting: a retired corner of a park in Madrid, on a sunny autumn morning; a
  single bench; four characters; continuous action.
- Dona Laura, seventy, is feeding pigeons when Don Gonzalo, seventy and gouty,
  scatters them. They quarrel over the bench and the birds; a pinch of **snuff**
  makes peace.
- He reads Campoamor aloud; she recites "Twenty years pass. He returns…" without
  glasses, knowing it by heart.
- Mentions of **Valencia**, the villa **Maricela**, **Laura Llorente** and "The
  Silver Maiden" bring the past back. Speaking of "my cousin" and "my friend", they
  retell their own love story: the flowers thrown to the balcony, the merchant,
  the insult, the duel at sunrise.
- Each invents a noble death for the missing lover — his in a trench in Africa,
  hers in the rising tide — and each privately knows the other is lying. Their
  asides reveal the truth: she married two years later, he ran off to Paris with a
  ballet dancer in three months.
- Neither reveals himself. He: "I am grotesque now." She: "I am too sadly
  changed." Each wants the other to keep the young image.
- Petra brings violets; some fall; Gonzalo stoops "trembling and with a great
  effort" to pick them up and Laura sees him. "Yes, it is he!" — "It is she, and no
  mistake."
- They part quoting the poem — "Can it be that this is he?" / "Heavens, is it
  she?" — and agree to meet again tomorrow, if it is sunny, when he will bring
  crumbs for the birds.
- Techniques: **asides**, **dramatic irony**, narrated **flashback**, repartee,
  comic exaggeration, and symbols (sunny morning, autumn, birds, violets, the
  quoted verses).

   other? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
4. When does Dona Laura realise that Don Gonzalo was her former lover? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. Why do Dona Laura and Don Gonzalo spin fictitious stories about themselves? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. How do Dona Laura and Don Gonzalo feel about each other at the end of the
   play? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
7. What part do the snuff and the violets play in the action? <span class="marks">[2]</span>

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** His behaviour from the moment he enters. He grumbles that the people in the
park are "idling their time away", demands a bench to himself, orders Juanito to
"rout out" three priests who are sitting on "his" bench, and then walks straight
through Dona Laura's pigeons, scattering the birds she is feeding. When she
protests he answers, "What do I care about the birds?" and refuses even to speak to
her because "we have not met". Her verdict is immediate: "What an ill-natured old
man! Why must people get so fussy and cross when they reach a certain age?" She is
also, of course, delighted when he fails to get the other bench — which shows she
is quite as combative as he is.

**2.** Because each is ashamed of having grown old, and each wants to protect the
other's memory of their youth. The two asides are almost identical in feeling.
Gonzalo: "No, no, I will not reveal myself. I am grotesque now. Better that she
recall the gallant horseman who passed daily beneath her window tossing flowers."
Laura: "No, I am too sadly changed. It is better he should remember me as the black
eyed girl tossing flowers as he passed among the roses in the garden." Their
silence is therefore not deceit but consideration — each would rather be
remembered beautifully than be recognised. A comic reason reinforces it: each has
just invented a heroic death for the other's beloved, and confessing now would mean
confessing the lie.

**3.** They begin to recognise each other when the conversation turns to **Valencia
and the villa Maricela**. Gonzalo says he is a native of Valencia; Laura mentions a
villa near the sea, hidden among lemon and orange trees, "Maricela" — and the stage
direction reads "(Startled.) Maricela?" He then produces the name "Laura
Llorente", and it is her turn to be startled: "(Startled.) Laura Llorente?" "They
look at each other intently." From that exchange onwards both suspect, and by the
end of the duel story both are certain, as their asides show — "This is Laura!" and
"Gonzalo!"

**4.** Laura is certain during the retelling of the duel, when Gonzalo describes
"my cousin" hiding in his house, escaping to Seville and then Madrid, and writing
letters that were never answered. Her aside "Gonzalo!" comes even earlier, at the
words "the gallant lover". But the *final*, acknowledged realisation comes at the
very end, when she turns her head and sees him stooping, "trembling and with a
great effort", to pick up the violets she has dropped, and says to herself, "Yes,
it is he!" The sequence matters: she knows who he is quite early, but she knows he
still loves her only in the last few seconds.

**5.** For three reasons. First, **self-defence** — each has to explain why the
lovers never met again, and the truth is unflattering: he ran off to Paris with a
ballet dancer within three months, and she married someone else within two years.
Second, **romance** — having decided to keep the past beautiful, each decorates it,
turning a shabby ending into a glorious death in Africa and a tragic drowning at
sunset. Third, **competition**, which is where the comedy lies: Laura's aside "You
will not get ahead of me in decorating my own funeral" shows that once he has told
a splendid lie she is determined to tell a better one. Each privately sees through
the other: "What an atrocious lie!" and "She lies worse than I do."

**6.** With deep and undiminished affection, mixed with amusement and a little
sadness. They begin as enemies and end unable to bear parting: they agree to meet
again tomorrow on the same bench, he promises to bring crumbs for her birds, and he
tells her the morning "has been a great honour and a great pleasure." The feeling
is proved by actions rather than statements — his painful stooping for the violets,
her "(Sweetly.) Are you coming again tomorrow?" — and it is sealed when they quote
the Campoamor lines to each other and smile "as if she were again at the window and
he below in the rose garden."

**7.** Both are small objects that carry the whole movement of the play. The
**snuff** ends the quarrel: he offers his box, she accepts, they sneeze three times
each, and she says in an aside, "The snuff has made peace between us." It is the
hinge of the play — without that shared courtesy the two would never have talked.
The **violets**, brought by Petra from her guard, end the play: as Laura takes them
a few fall, and Gonzalo stoops painfully to pick them up. That action reveals his
feeling and brings about the final recognition. Together they show the Quinteros'
method: feeling is conveyed through objects and gestures rather than declarations.

**Long answer questions (5 marks each)**

1. What is the effect of the flashback in the play, when Dona Laura and Don
   Gonzalo learn that they were lovers in the past? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Discuss how the play is built around humour and irony. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. How is the title "A Sunny Morning" justifiable? Discuss. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. Was it wise for Dona Laura and Don Gonzalo to keep their identities secret?
   What do you predict will happen at their next meeting? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
5. Write a summary of the play. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** The flashback in *A Sunny Morning* is unusual: nothing is staged, no scene
changes, and no character steps back in time. The past is delivered entirely
through dialogue, and — this is the crucial point — through a **disguise**. Gonzalo
speaks of "my cousin" and Laura of "my best friend", so that the two lovers narrate
their own history while pretending it belongs to other people. The effects are
several and all of them are dramatic rather than decorative.

**It supplies the whole background without stopping the play.** In three or four
minutes the audience learns of the villa Maricela, the window with the red roses,
the girl called "The Silver Maiden", the horseman who threw up a bouquet each
morning and caught one each afternoon, the merchant her parents preferred, the
insult beneath the window, and the duel at sunrise on the beach. All of it arrives
as conversation between two strangers on a bench, so exposition and action are the
same thing.

**It becomes the recognition scene.** Each detail one of them adds is also a clue
to the other. "You seem to know the story well." "And so do you." The flashback is
therefore not an interruption of the plot but the plot itself: the past is the
mechanism by which the present is discovered.

**It creates the play's central irony.** Because the audience works out the truth
before the characters admit it, every sentence of the flashback carries two
meanings. When Laura says of her friend, "She was very unfortunate and had a sad
love affair," and Gonzalo replies simply "Very sad," we are watching two people
discuss their own tragedy with perfect politeness, which is both funny and
painful.

**It measures the distance travelled.** Setting the black-eyed girl at the window
and the gallant horseman among the roses beside the white-haired lady with a
parasol and the gouty gentleman with a reading glass is what gives the comedy its
undertow of sadness, and what makes both of them decide to stay silent.

**And it sets up the ending.** Because the audience has been shown the bouquet
thrown up to the balcony, the final stage direction — they smile "as if she were
again at the window and he below in the rose garden" — can restore the whole past
in one image, and the violets Gonzalo stoops for are recognisably the same flowers
fifty years later.

**2.** The play is a comedy that keeps turning into a love scene, and the two
effects are produced by the same means.

**The humour of character.** Both old people are vain, quarrelsome and thoroughly
enjoying themselves. Gonzalo demands a bench "to himself" in a public park, wants
three priests "routed out", and then lectures Laura that "the benches here are
public property" — an inconsistency she pounces on at once. He boasts about killing
hares and partridges and about a wild boar's head in his study; she answers, "Yes,
kill time. That is all you kill," and trumps him with a tiger's skin in her
boudoir.

**The humour of physical comedy.** The "enormous pair of glasses" plus a reading
glass, which make Laura say "I thought you were taking out a telescope"; the two of
them sneezing three times each in alternation after the snuff; and the old man
dragging his feet so that "a carriage would not raise more dust."

**The humour of repartee.** "Do you use your handkerchief as a shoe brush?" / "Why
not?" / "Do you use a shoe brush as a handkerchief?" The dialogue works by
reversal, each speaker turning the other's own words round — which is also how we
learn that these two are perfectly matched.

**Verbal irony** is everywhere in the first half. Laura's "You are very polite" to a
man who has just refused to listen to her; her delight when he loses the other
bench too, "Serves him right for scaring the birds."

**Dramatic irony** governs the second half and gives the play its depth. The
audience knows who these people are long before they say so, so ordinary lines
acquire a double meaning: "The daughters of the mothers I once loved kiss me now as
they would a graven image", read out as a joke by a man beside the woman he loved;
"Very sad", said of his own ruined love affair; "Fate is curious. Here are you and
I, complete strangers…" said by a woman who knows they are nothing of the kind. The
two elaborate lies are the high point, because each character is simultaneously
deceiving, being deceived, and privately seeing through the deception — "She lies
worse than I do."

**Situational irony** frames the whole piece: a quarrel about scattered birds
becomes a reunion of lovers, and two people who have spent fifty years apart, when
finally given the chance to speak, agree to say nothing.

Finally, and most importantly, the humour is never cruel. The Quinteros laugh at
old age — the gout, the spectacles, the boasting, the melodrama — while giving
their two old people more dignity and more feeling than most young stage lovers
have. That combination of laughter and tenderness is the play's real achievement,
and it is why the ending can be funny and moving in the same breath.

**3.** The title is justified on at least four levels, and a good answer should
climb from the literal to the symbolic.

**Literally**, it names the setting. The opening stage direction reads, "A sunny
morning in a retired corner of a park in Madrid. Autumn." Dona Laura's first words
are "I am so glad to be here… What a beautiful morning!" and Petra replies that the
sun is hot. The entire action takes place on that one morning, in real time, in
sunshine.

**Structurally**, the sunshine causes the plot. It is the sunny morning that brings
both old people out of doors, fills the benches, leaves the three priests
comfortably settled on the one Gonzalo wants, and so forces him to sit beside the
only lady in the park. He says so himself: "The authorities should place more
benches here for these sunny mornings. Well, I suppose I must resign myself and sit
on the bench with the old lady." Without the sun there is no meeting.

**Symbolically**, the sun is the warmth that grows between them. The play begins
cold — a scattering of birds, insults, "senile old lady" — and ends in a glow of
affection. Set against **autumn**, the season of the play, the sunshine becomes an
exact image of what the Quinteros are describing: brightness late in the year,
beauty in the season of decline, a last warm day in two long lives.

**In the plot's final moments**, the phrase becomes the condition of their future.
"Good-bye until tomorrow." "Until tomorrow." "If it is sunny." "A sunny morning."
The words are repeated four times in nine lines, and by then they mean far more
than weather: a sunny morning is now the name of their appointment, their renewed
courtship, and their agreement to go on living in the present rather than in the
past.

A final point worth making: the title is deliberately *light*. The play could have
been called "The Silver Maiden" or "Twenty Years Pass", and either would have
promised a tragedy. "A Sunny Morning" promises a pleasant hour — which is exactly
the tone the brothers wanted, and exactly the disguise under which they smuggle in
a story about lost love.

**4.** **Was it wise?** There are two defensible answers, and the best response
weighs both.

The case that they were **right** to keep silent is the one the play itself
endorses. Each explains the decision in terms of the other's happiness: "Better
that she recall the gallant horseman"; "It is better he should remember me as the
black eyed girl tossing flowers." A confession would have forced two exposures at
once — the ruin that age has made of both of them, and the shabby truth about the
separation (a marriage two years later, a ballet dancer in Paris within three
months). Silence keeps the memory intact and lets a friendship begin without the
weight of an old grievance. It also preserves the play's delicacy: what they have
agreed to is more intimate than a confession, because each knows, each knows the
other knows, and neither needs to say it.

The case that they were **wrong** is also real. They are seventy, and they have
already lost fifty years to a set of intercepted letters and a misunderstanding.
An open confession would have allowed them the honest companionship of whatever
time remains, instead of a friendship built on two large lies. And lies are
inconvenient: if they meet daily, Gonzalo must keep a dead cousin dead and Laura
must keep a drowned friend drowned.

On balance, the decision looks wise *in the moment* and unsustainable *in the long
run* — which is precisely why the play ends where it does.

**What will happen tomorrow?** A reasonable prediction, grounded in the text:

He will come, and he will bring the crumbs he promised — his own version of the
bouquets he once threw to her balcony. They will sit on the same bench, and the
quarrelling will continue, but as a game. Neither will make a formal confession
straight away; instead the truth will keep leaking out in the same way it did on
the first morning — through a remembered name, a line of Campoamor, another slip.
Since each now knows that the other knows, the pretence will become a shared joke
rather than a defence, and the "cousin" and the "friend" may well go on being
discussed for weeks, with both of them enjoying it.

In the end I think the fiction will simply be allowed to fade rather than be
broken: they will become inseparable companions of the park bench, and the words
"Laura" and "Gonzalo" will be spoken aloud one day without any scene at all. What
the play makes certain is the important part — that the two of them have found
each other again, and that neither intends to be alone tomorrow.

**5.** **Summary.** On a sunny autumn morning in a quiet corner of a park in
Madrid, Dona Laura, a refined white-haired lady of about seventy, arrives on the
arm of her young maid Petra and settles on what she calls "my seat". She sends
Petra off to meet the park guard she is in love with, and amuses herself by
throwing bread crumbs to the pigeons, naming them as she feeds them.

Don Gonzalo, an impatient, gouty gentleman of the same age, enters with his servant
Juanito, grumbling about idlers and about three priests who have taken "his" bench.
Walking about in search of a seat, he scatters Laura's birds. She rebukes him
sharply, and the two exchange insults — he mutters that she is a "senile old lady"
who should be at home counting her beads, she remarks that he is "very polite" —
before he is forced to share her bench. He calls for his book and a huge pair of
glasses; she asks whether he is taking out a telescope. Then he offers her a pinch
of snuff, they sneeze three times each, and, as she notes in an aside, "the snuff
has made peace between us."

He reads aloud from the poet Campoamor, including the lines "Twenty years pass. He
returns. And each, beholding the other, exclaims — Can it be that this is he?
Heavens, is it she?" Laura recites them without spectacles, privately admitting, "I
know every word by heart." Conversation turns to Valencia, and then to a seaside
villa called Maricela, where, Gonzalo remembers, there once lived a beautiful girl
named Laura Llorente, known as "The Silver Maiden".

Both are startled; both realise who the other is; and both decide to say nothing.
Instead, under cover of speaking about "my cousin" and "my best friend", they
retell their own love story — the horseman who tossed flowers up to her window, the
merchant her parents wanted her to marry, the quarrel and the duel at sunrise on
the beach. To explain why the lovers never met again, each invents a splendid
death: Gonzalo says his cousin fell in a trench in Africa clutching the flag of
Spain, and Laura says her friend walked into the rising tide at sunset after
writing his name on the sand. Their asides tell the audience the truth — she
married two years later, and he ran off to Paris with a ballet dancer within three
months — and each privately recognises the other's lie.

The quarrel is long forgotten. He admits he was unreasonable; she asks sweetly
whether he will come again tomorrow; he promises to bring crumbs for the birds. Yet
each, with the other's back turned, resolves not to confess: "I am grotesque now",
"I am too sadly changed" — each preferring that the other keep the memory of their
youth. Petra returns with violets from her guard, and a few fall as Laura takes
them. As they part, Don Gonzalo stoops "trembling and with a great effort" to pick
up the fallen flowers, and Laura turns and catches him at it. "Yes, it is he!" "It
is she, and no mistake." Waving farewell, they quote the poem to each other — "Can
it be that this is he?" "Heavens, is it she?" — smile as though she were at her
window again and he below among the roses, and leave on the arms of their servants,
having agreed to meet tomorrow, if it is sunny.

**Reference to the context**

1. "Yes, you are only twenty. (She sits down on the bench.) Oh, I feel more tired
   today than usual. (Noticing Petra, who seems impatient.) Go, if you wish to
   chat with your guard."
   (i) Who is the speaker? (ii) Who does "you" refer to? (iii) Who is the "guard"
   the speaker is talking about? (iv) What do these lines reveal about the speaker
   and about her situation? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. "DONA LAURA: (Indignantly.) Look out! … DON GONZALO: What do I care about the
   birds? DONA LAURA: But I do. DON GONZALO: This is a public park."
   (i) Whom is Dona Laura addressing when she says "Look out"? (ii) What was she
   doing? (iii) Who scared the birds — and are they pet birds? (iv) Where are the
   speakers at the time of this conversation? (v) Why is this quarrel important to
   the play as a whole? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — reference to the context
**1.** (i) The speaker is **Dona Laura**, the white-haired lady of about seventy
who is the heroine of the play. She has just arrived at her favourite bench in a
park in Madrid, leaning on her maid's arm and using her parasol as a walking stick.
(ii) "You" refers to **Petra**, her young maid, who is about twenty years old.
Petra has just said, "The sun is hot," and Laura's reply — "Yes, you are only
twenty" — means that at twenty one feels the heat, while at seventy one is grateful
for it.
(iii) The "guard" is the young **park keeper** whom Petra is in love with and who
is waiting for her among the trees. Laura teases her about him a moment later —
"He belongs more to you than he does to the park" and "Your head is where your
heart is — with the guard." It is this same guard who later sends Laura the bunch
of violets that brings about the recognition at the end of the play.
(iv) Three things. First, Laura's **age and frailty**: "I feel more tired today
than usual," and she must be helped to her seat. Second, her **kindness and
humour**: she notices Petra's impatience, sends her off to her sweetheart without
being asked, and only stipulates ten minutes — she is an indulgent mistress who
enjoys young people's happiness. Third, her **loneliness**: once the maid has gone
she is left alone with a bag of bread crumbs and a flock of pigeons, to whom she
talks as if they were friends. The play needs all three facts in place before Don
Gonzalo arrives, and it establishes them in four lines.

**2.** (i) She is addressing **Don Gonzalo**, the impatient old gentleman who has
just entered with his servant Juanito, looking for a bench of his own and walking
about the park in irritation. At this moment they are complete strangers to each
other — or believe they are.
(ii) She was **feeding the pigeons**. Having sent Petra away, she had risen, walked
towards the trees and thrown three handfuls of bread crumbs, distributing them with
great enjoyment — "These are for the spryest, these for the gluttons, and these for
the little ones which are the most persistent" — and had gone back to her seat to
watch them eat.
(iii) **Don Gonzalo** scared them, by walking towards the birds with Juanito while
hunting for a free bench. No, they are **not pet birds**: they are the wild pigeons
of a public park, which Dona Laura feeds every day and has come to know
individually — "that big one is always first! I know him by his big head." That is
exactly what makes her claim on them so comic and so touching: she has no legal
right to the birds at all, yet they are plainly hers.
(iv) They are in a **park in Madrid**, the capital of Spain — in "a retired corner"
of it, beside a bench, on a sunny autumn morning. Dona Laura is seated on the bench
she regards as her own; Don Gonzalo is standing near the trees where the pigeons
were feeding.
(v) Because everything else grows out of it. Dramatically, it is the **inciting
incident**: it forces two strangers into conversation who would otherwise have sat
in silence, and it is Gonzalo's failure to find another seat that puts him on her
bench. Thematically, it establishes both characters in a few strokes — her
warmth and her fighting spirit, his selfishness and his absurd sense of ownership
(he complains that the priests have taken "his" bench, and is instantly caught out
when he claims that benches are "public property"). And it sets up the play's final
reversal: the bad-tempered old man who scattered her birds in the first minutes
promises, in the last, to bring crumbs for them himself — which is the play's quiet
way of saying that he is in love with her again.
