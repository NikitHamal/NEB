A woman stands at a soldier's grave and does what people do at graves: she brings
gifts. Then she sets out, item by item, what she has given him and what he has
given her in return. The word "gift" is used for both lists, and the whole force
of the poem lies in that one word being made to cover roses on one side and blood
on the other.

::: key What the exam wants from this poem
The poem is built on an **exchange of gifts** between a living woman and her dead
soldier. Learn the two lists and learn to explain the **irony** of calling the
second list gifts. Know that the speaker is a widow, the setting is the Vietnam
War, the form is **free verse**, and the poem reaches us in **translation**.
:::

## 12.1 About the poet

Tran Mong Tu (Trần Mộng Tú) was born in 1943 in northern Vietnam. After the
country was divided in 1954 her family moved to the South, and as a young woman
she worked in Saigon for the Associated Press news agency. When Saigon fell in
April 1975 at the end of the Vietnam War she left the country and settled in the
United States, where she has lived ever since, mainly in Washington State.

She is one of the best-known women poets of the Vietnamese diaspora — the
community of Vietnamese people scattered across the world by the war. She writes
in Vietnamese, chiefly poetry and short prose, and her work has appeared in
Vietnamese-language collections and in English-language anthologies of Vietnamese
and Vietnamese-American writing. Her constant subjects are war, exile, memory and
the losses carried by women.

"The Gift in Wartime" is her best-known poem in English. It was written in
Vietnamese and is read here in **translation**, which is an important fact for
any question on its form: whatever sound-patterns the original had cannot survive
the crossing into English, and what does survive is the poem's structure and its
images.

::: note The war behind the poem
The Vietnam War (roughly 1955–1975) was fought between communist North Vietnam
and the American-backed South. It killed well over a million Vietnamese people,
most of them civilians, and left the country divided for twenty years. Tran Mong
Tu's poem was written from inside that war, not looking back at it from safety.
:::

## 12.2 Summary

The poem is a **dramatic monologue** in free verse, spoken by a young widow
standing at the grave of the soldier she loved. It is arranged as a series of
short stanzas built on one repeated pattern: *I offer you this* — *you give me
that*. The poem therefore reads like a list of accounts being settled, and it
moves in three stages.

### Movement 1 — what she offers

She begins with the gifts of the living. She brings roses and lays them on the
newly dug grave, and she offers the wedding ring that bound her to him. These
are the ordinary objects of love and marriage, and she is giving them to someone
who can no longer receive anything. To them she adds herself: her youth, her
beauty, her love, and all the years of her life that she will now spend alone.
The tone in this movement is tender and formal, like a ritual being performed
properly.

### Movement 2 — what he gives in return

The pattern then turns over, and the same courteous verb is used for gifts of a
very different kind. What the soldier gives her is what war gives: the smell of
blood from his uniform, his body brought back from the field, the medals and
official honours that are handed to the families of the dead, and the loneliness
she must now carry. Every "gift" in this list is an injury dressed in polite
language. The speaker never raises her voice — she simply goes on using the word
"gift", and lets the reader hear how wrong it sounds.

### Movement 3 — the last exchange and the bitter peace

In the closing movement the exchange is completed and the speaker reaches her
hardest thought. The dead man is at rest; he has finished with the war, and the
earth holds him quietly. She is the one still alive, and being alive is now the
harder condition: she must go on counting the days, wearing her grief, and
living out a marriage that has nothing left in it but memory. The poem ends
without comfort and without anger. The last gift the war has given her is the
rest of her life.

## 12.3 Themes

**The cruelty and waste of war.** The poem makes no speech against war. It simply
sets out what one family received from it, and the list does the arguing.

**Love that outlasts death.** She is still giving him presents. The marriage
continues on one side only, which is both moving and unbearable.

**The cost paid by those who survive.** The soldier's suffering has ended; hers
has only begun. The poem's quietest and sharpest idea is that the living are
worse off than the dead.

**Women and war.** The speaker is a woman at a grave, not a soldier in a field.
The poem insists that war's real, long casualty list is made of widows, mothers
and children who were never counted as combatants.

**Irony as protest.** The word "gift" belongs to birthdays and weddings. Forcing
it to carry blood, corpses and medals is the poem's central act of protest.

**Memory and ritual.** Roses, a ring, a grave, honours — the poem is full of
ceremonies that are supposed to give meaning to a death and visibly fail to.

**The emptiness of official honour.** Medals and decorations are handed over as
though they balanced the account. In the poem's arithmetic they are worth nothing
at all.

## 12.4 Form, structure and rhyme

**Form.** The poem is in **free verse** — short lines of unequal length, no fixed
metre, and no rhyme. That is partly a choice and partly a fact of translation: a
poem carried from Vietnamese into English loses its original sound-pattern
entirely, so what an English reader can study is the structure, the images and
the tone.

**Rhyme scheme.** There is **none**. Do not attempt to write out an ABAB pattern
for this poem; the correct answer in an examination is "free verse, unrhymed",
with a sentence explaining that the poem is a translation and that its music comes
from repetition rather than rhyme.

**Metre.** Also none. Free verse has no feet to count, so no line can be scanned
into iambs the way Burns's or Shakespeare's can. Instead the poem builds its
rhythm out of **parallel syntax**: the same grammatical shape — *I offer you …*,
*you give me …* — returning stanza after stanza, like a response chanted in a
ceremony. Read aloud, the poem sounds like a ritual precisely because of this
repetition.

**Structure.** The organising shape is an **exchange**, or a set of accounts. Each
stanza places a gift from her against a gift from him. Because the two columns are
kept in the same polite language, the reader is forced to compare them, and the
comparison is the poem's whole argument. The structure also produces the final
turn: once everything has been exchanged, the only thing left to weigh is her own
survival.

**Tone.** Controlled, formal, almost gentle throughout. There is no shouting and
no direct accusation. A raised voice would have let the reader off; the calm makes
the content unbearable.

**What the form does for the meaning.** War destroys order, so the poem imposes
order: a neat repeating pattern, two matching lists, a quiet voice. The gap
between the tidy form and the terrible contents *is* the poem's meaning.

## 12.5 Poetic devices

Because the poem is modern and in copyright, the table names the images rather
than reprinting the lines.

| Device | Where it appears | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Irony | the word "gift" used for blood, a corpse and medals | The politeness of the word exposes the horror of the thing; this is the poem's central device |
| Apostrophe | the whole poem is addressed to a dead man as "you" | Makes the reader an eavesdropper at a private grave-side conversation |
| Anaphora / parallelism | the repeated *I offer you … you give me …* pattern | Gives the poem the rhythm of a ritual and forces the two lists to be compared |
| Symbolism | the roses | Love, beauty and mourning — and, because cut flowers die, the shortness of what she had |
| Symbolism | the wedding ring | A circle that was meant to be unending, handed back to the earth |
| Symbolism | the medals | Official honour offered in place of a life; the poem treats them as worthless |
| Imagery (of the senses) | the smell of blood on the uniform | The most physical detail in the poem; it makes war something you can smell, not read about |
| Metaphor | love and grief described as things that can be handed over | Turns feelings into objects, so that they can be counted and set against one another |
| Personification | war as a giver of presents | The war is made into a person who arrives with gifts, which is the poem's bitterest joke |
| Hyperbole | the whole of her remaining life offered as a single gift | The scale of the offering shows the scale of the loss |
| Juxtaposition | the wedding ring beside the blood | Two images from the two extremes of a life, set side by side without comment |
| Alliteration | the soft repeated consonants of the English version | Keeps the voice quiet and steady; nothing in the sound raises its voice |
| Enjambment | the short lines running on into one another | The speaker's thought cannot be stopped at the line-ends any more than her grief can |
| Understatement | the flat, unemotional listing of terrible things | Restraint that is far more powerful than protest would be |

## 12.6 Glossary

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| wartime | the period during which a war is being fought |
| offer | to present something to someone as a gift |
| grave | the hole in the ground in which a dead body is buried |
| wedding ring | the ring exchanged at marriage, a symbol of a bond meant to last |
| widow | a woman whose husband has died |
| mourning | grief for someone who has died, and the customs that go with it |
| shroud | the cloth in which a dead body is wrapped for burial |

| casualty | a person killed or injured in a war or accident |
| diaspora | a people scattered from their homeland and living abroad |
| exile | forced absence from one's own country |
| irony | a gap between what the words say and what is really the case |
| understatement | saying less than the situation deserves, so that the reader supplies the rest |
| anaphora | the repetition of the same words at the beginnings of successive lines |
| parallelism | the repeating of the same grammatical structure for effect |
| apostrophe | direct address to someone absent or dead, or to a thing |
| free verse | verse with no fixed metre and no rhyme |
| translation | the carrying of a text from one language into another |
| monologue | an extended speech by one person |
| futility | complete uselessness; the quality of achieving nothing |

## Chapter summary

- "The Gift in Wartime" by Tran Mong Tu (b. 1943, Vietnam; in the United States
  since 1975) is spoken by a young widow at the grave of her soldier.
- It is built as an **exchange of gifts**: she offers roses, her wedding ring, her
  youth and the rest of her life; he gives back the smell of blood, his body,
  official medals, and a lifetime of mourning.
- The central device is **irony** — the word "gift" is made to cover both lists —
  supported by **anaphora**, **symbolism** and **understatement**.
- Form: **free verse**, no metre, **no rhyme scheme**, read in English
  translation; the rhythm comes from parallel syntax, not from feet or rhyme.
- Themes: the waste of war, the cost paid by survivors (especially women), love
  continuing after death, the emptiness of official honour, and protest by irony
  rather than by argument.
- The poem's hardest idea is that the dead soldier is at peace while the living
  widow is not: survival is the heavier burden.

## Practice questions

**Short answer questions (2 marks each)**

1. Who is the speaker of "The Gift in Wartime" and whom does she address? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
2. What gifts does the speaker offer? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
3. What does she receive in return? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
4. Why is the title ironic? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. What do the roses and the wedding ring symbolise? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. Comment on the tone of the poem. <span class="marks">[2]</span>
7. What is the rhyme scheme of the poem, and why? <span class="marks">[2]</span>

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** The speaker is a young Vietnamese widow standing at the grave of the
soldier she loved. She addresses him directly as "you", although he is dead and
cannot answer. The poem is therefore a one-sided conversation, which is part of
its sadness.

**2.** She offers roses, which she lays on his newly dug grave, and her wedding
ring. Beyond these objects she offers herself — her youth, her beauty, her love,
and all the years of the life she must now live without him.

**3.** She receives the things war hands out: the smell of blood from his
uniform, his dead body returned from the field, the medals and official honours
given to the families of the dead, and a lifetime of loneliness and mourning.

**4.** Because a "gift" is something freely given that the receiver wants, and
nothing in the second list is wanted by anybody. Calling blood, a corpse and a
medal "gifts" uses the language of love and celebration for the products of
killing, and the mismatch is the poem's protest against war.

**5.** The roses stand for love, beauty and mourning, and — because cut flowers
die quickly — for how briefly the couple's happiness lasted. The wedding ring is a
circle, a symbol of a bond meant to have no end, and it is now being handed over
to a grave.

**6.** The tone is quiet, formal and controlled — tender at first, then bitterly
ironic, and finally flat and resigned. The speaker never shouts and never accuses
anyone by name. That restraint is deliberate: an angry poem could be argued with,
while this calm listing of facts cannot.

**7.** There is **no rhyme scheme**: the poem is free verse. Two reasons should be
given. First, free verse suits a speaking, grieving voice better than a fixed
pattern would. Second, the poem is a **translation** from Vietnamese, and the
sound-patterns of the original cannot cross into English; what survives is the
structure and the imagery.
:::

**Long answer questions (5 marks each)**

1. Discuss the use of irony in "The Gift in Wartime". <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. "The poem shows that war's worst injuries are done to those who survive it."
   Discuss with reference to the poem. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Examine the structure and tone of the poem and show how they carry its
   meaning. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** Irony means a gap between what is said and what is actually the case, and
this poem is built on one enormous example of it. The word "gift" belongs to
weddings, birthdays and festivals; it implies choice, affection and pleasure. Tran
Mong Tu takes that word and makes it carry the entire traffic of a war. On one
side of the exchange it behaves normally: roses for a grave, a wedding ring, a
young woman's love. On the other side the same word is attached to the smell of
blood on a uniform, a body brought home from the field, and a medal handed to a
widow in place of her husband. Nothing in that second list was chosen, wanted or
given out of love, yet the speaker keeps her courteous vocabulary throughout. The
effect is far stronger than protest would be. If she called the war murderous, a
reader could agree or disagree; because she calls its products "gifts", the reader
has to do the arithmetic and arrive at the anger themselves. A second irony closes
the poem: the medals, which the state offers as honour and compensation, are shown
to be worth nothing beside the life that bought them. The poem never says so
directly. It simply places them in the same list as the blood.

**2.** The poem quietly reverses the usual view that the dead soldier is war's
chief victim. He has suffered and died, but his suffering is finished; the earth
holds him and he is at rest. The woman at the graveside is the one with a future,
and the poem shows exactly what that future contains. She has already given away
everything that made her happy — her youth, her beauty, her wedding ring, her
love — and received in exchange the smell of blood, a corpse, a decoration and an
unending routine of mourning. Her marriage continues, but on one side only: she
goes on bringing presents to a man who cannot receive them. The final movement
makes the reversal explicit by weighing her survival against his rest and finding
survival heavier. This is why the poem is spoken by a woman and set at a grave
rather than on a battlefield. Battlefield poems count the dead; this poem counts
what the dead leave behind, and finds that the real casualty list — widows,
mothers, children — is far longer than the official one and is never published.
The soldier's war ended on a particular day. Hers has no end in the poem at all.

**3.** The poem is written in free verse: short lines, no metre, no rhyme, and a
translation's plain English vocabulary. Into that loose form the poet puts a very
tight structure. Each stanza sets one of her gifts against one of his, using the
same repeated grammatical shape — *I offer you …*, *you give me …*. This
**anaphora** does three things. It gives the poem the steady rhythm of a ritual,
which suits a grave-side. It forces the reader to compare the two columns, since
they arrive in identical packaging. And it builds an expectation that the final
movement can break, when the last thing left to weigh turns out to be her own
survival. The tone is the poem's other great instrument. It begins tender and
formal, as a widow performing the proper ceremonies; it passes through irony as
the second list accumulates; and it settles into a flat, exhausted acceptance at
the end. At no point does it rise. The restraint is a deliberate strategy:
understatement leaves the reader to supply the outrage, and an outrage the reader
has produced is far harder to dismiss than one delivered from the page.
Structure, then, imposes a neat order on the poem, and that neatness is itself
meaningful — it is the desperate tidiness of someone holding herself together at a
funeral.
:::

**Reference to the context**

1. The speaker brings roses to lay on a newly dug grave and offers the wedding
   ring she has worn through her marriage.
   (a) Who is the speaker and where is she? (b) What do these two objects
   symbolise? (c) Why does she give presents to someone who cannot receive
   them? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. In return for her gifts, the speaker says, she has been given the smell of
   blood from his uniform, his body brought back from the field, and medals for
   the dead.
   (a) Who is the giver of these "gifts"? (b) Identify the figure of speech that
   governs the passage and explain it. (c) What comment does the passage make on
   official honours for the dead? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — reference to the context
**1.** (a) The speaker is a young Vietnamese widow, and she is standing at the
freshly made grave of the soldier she loved, during the Vietnam War.
(b) The **roses** stand for love, beauty and mourning; because cut flowers wither
within days, they also stand for how short her happiness was. The **wedding ring**
is a circle with no beginning and no end, the symbol of a bond that was supposed to
last a lifetime — and she is now returning it to the earth.
(c) Because giving is all she has left of the marriage. Ceremony is how human
beings hold on to the dead: bringing flowers, keeping anniversaries, speaking
aloud at a grave. Her gifts are an act of continuing love, and at the same time the
poem shows their futility, since nothing she offers can be received. The
one-sidedness of the exchange is exactly what makes the passage painful.

**2.** (a) The giver is her dead husband — but behind him, and really, the giver
is the **war** itself, which the poem treats as a person arriving with presents.
(b) The governing figure is **irony**. A gift is something freely given that the
receiver wants; nothing here is wanted by anyone. By keeping the polite word
"gift" for blood, a corpse and a medal, the speaker makes the reader feel the
distance between the language and the reality. The passage also uses
**juxtaposition** — the wedding ring of the previous stanza set beside the smell of
blood — and strong **sensory imagery**, since smell is the most physical and
inescapable of the senses.
(c) It reduces them to nothing. Medals are placed in the same list as the corpse
and the blood, as though they were one more thing the war handed over, and no line
is spent praising them. The implication is that official honour is a cheap
substitute offered by the state in place of a life, and that it does not balance
the account by even a little.
:::
