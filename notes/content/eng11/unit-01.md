"The Selfish Giant" is a literary fairy tale by the Irish writer Oscar Wilde,
first published in his 1888 collection *The Happy Prince and Other Tales*. On the
surface it is a simple story for children: a giant shuts children out of his
garden and winter never leaves it. Underneath, it is a carefully built parable
about selfishness, repentance and love, with a clear Christian allegory in its
closing pages. It opens the Grade 11 course because it teaches you to read a
short, easy text on two levels at once — the story, and what the story stands for.

::: key The central idea
A heart closed by selfishness freezes everything around it; a heart opened by
love brings life back. The Giant's garden is not really a garden — it is his own
soul, and the weather in it reports the state of his heart.
:::

## 1.1 About the author

Oscar Wilde (1854–1900) was an Irish poet, playwright, novelist and critic. He
was born in Dublin to a distinguished family — his father a surgeon, his mother a
poet and Irish nationalist — and studied classics at Trinity College Dublin and
then at Magdalen College, Oxford. At Oxford he came under the influence of the
critics John Ruskin and Walter Pater, and became the best-known spokesman of
**Aestheticism**, the late-Victorian movement whose slogan was "art for art's
sake": the belief that a work of art does not have to teach a moral lesson, and
that beauty is valuable in itself.

::: definition Aestheticism
A literary and artistic movement of the late nineteenth century which held that
the purpose of art is beauty rather than instruction, morality or usefulness.
:::

Wilde's fame rests mainly on his plays, which are still performed everywhere:
*Lady Windermere's Fan* (1892), *An Ideal Husband* (1895) and above all *The
Importance of Being Earnest* (1895), a comedy built almost entirely out of
brilliant one-line jokes. His only novel is *The Picture of Dorian Gray* (1890),
about a young man whose portrait ages while he does not. He wrote two books of
fairy tales — *The Happy Prince and Other Tales* (1888), which contains "The
Selfish Giant", and *A House of Pomegranates* (1891) — partly for his own two
sons, Cyril and Vyvyan.

In 1895, at the height of his success, Wilde was tried and sentenced to two
years' hard labour. In prison he wrote the long confessional letter published as
*De Profundis*, and after his release the poem *The Ballad of Reading Gaol*
(1898). He died poor and ill in Paris in 1900, aged forty-six.

What is distinctive about his voice? In the plays and the novel it is the
**epigram** — a short, polished, surprising sentence that turns an accepted idea
upside down. In the fairy tales the voice changes completely. Here Wilde writes
short sentences joined by "and", in the plain, dignified rhythm of the Bible and
of old folk tales. Nature speaks. Objects have feelings. The effect is gentle on
the surface and severe underneath: almost every one of these tales is about
someone who learns generosity too late, or at a great price.

::: caution Not "just a children's story"
Students often write that this is a simple tale teaching children to share. That
answer will not earn full marks. Wilde wrote a **parable**: the little boy with
nail-prints in his hands and feet is Christ, the garden is the human soul, and
the ending is about salvation, not about good manners. Read both levels.
:::

## 1.2 The story

**The garden as it was.** Every afternoon, walking home from school, the village
children played in the Giant's garden — soft green grass, flowers "like stars",
twelve peach-trees that blossomed pink and pearl in spring and bore fruit in
autumn, and birds singing so sweetly that the children stopped their games to
listen. "How happy we are here!" they said.

**The wall.** One day the Giant came back. He had spent seven years with his
friend the Cornish ogre and returned only because he had said everything he had to
say — "his conversation was limited". Finding children in his garden he roared at
them, and they ran. "My own garden is my own garden," he said, and built a high
wall round it with a notice-board: TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED. Wilde adds one
flat verdict: "He was a very selfish Giant."

**The endless winter.** Now the children had nowhere to play; the road was dusty
and stony, so they walked round the outside of the wall and talked about the
garden inside. When Spring came to the rest of the country, this garden stayed
frozen. The birds would not sing where there were no children and the trees forgot
to blossom. One flower put its head out, saw the notice-board, felt sorry for the
children and went back to sleep underground. Snow and Frost were delighted and
decided to live there all year; they invited the North Wind, who blew the
chimney-pots down, and he invited the Hail, who rattled on the roof three hours a
day until most of the slates broke. Summer never came. Autumn gave golden fruit to
every garden but this one — "He is too selfish," she said.

**The linnet and the corner.** One morning the Giant heard lovely music. It was
only a little linnet singing at his window, but he had not heard a bird there for
years. The Hail stopped, the North Wind ceased roaring, and a sweet perfume came
in. Looking out, he saw that the children had crept in through a hole in the wall
and were sitting in the branches; every tree had a child in it and had covered
itself with blossom. Only the farthest corner was still winter. There a very small
boy stood crying under a tree, too little to reach the branches, although the tree
bent them down as low as it could.

**The Giant's change.** The Giant's heart melted. "How selfish I have been!" he
said; "now I know why the Spring would not come here." He went down to lift the
boy into the tree and break the wall. The other children saw him and fled, and the
garden turned to winter again — all but the little boy, whose eyes were too full of
tears to see him coming. The Giant lifted him gently into the tree. It broke at
once into blossom, the birds returned, and the boy flung his arms round the Giant's
neck and kissed him. Seeing this, the children came running back, and Spring came
with them. The Giant took a great axe and knocked down the wall, and the people
going to market at twelve found him playing with the children in the most
beautiful garden they had ever seen.

**The years after.** That evening the little boy had gone; nobody knew where he
lived and none of them had seen him before. The children came every afternoon
after that, but the boy the Giant loved best was never seen again. Years passed
and the Giant grew old and feeble, sitting in a huge armchair to watch the games.
"The children are the most beautiful flowers of all," he said. He no longer hated
winter, knowing it was "merely the Spring asleep".

**The last morning.** One winter morning he saw, in the farthest corner, a tree
covered with white blossoms, its branches golden and silver fruit hanging from
them, and beneath it the little boy. He ran out in joy, but close up his face went
red with anger: there were the prints of two nails on the child's palms and two on
his little feet. "Who hath dared to wound thee?" he cried; "tell me, that I may
take my big sword and slay him." The child answered, "Nay! but these are the
wounds of Love." A strange awe fell on the Giant and he knelt. The child smiled
and said, "You let me play once in your garden, to-day you shall come with me to
my garden, which is Paradise." That afternoon the children found the Giant lying
dead under the tree, covered all over with white blossoms.

## 1.3 Characters

| Character | Who they are | Role in the story |
|---|---|---|
| The Giant | Owner of the castle and the garden; selfish at first, loving at the end | Protagonist; the only character who changes |
| The little boy | A tiny child, too small to climb the tree; later shown with nail-prints | Christ figure; the agent of the Giant's change and of his salvation |
| The children | Village schoolchildren who used the garden as a playground | Represent innocence, joy and life; their presence *is* the spring |
| The Cornish ogre | The Giant's friend, whom he visited for seven years | Never appears; shows the company the Giant kept and how little he had to say |
| Snow, Frost, North Wind, Hail | The winter weathers, personified as guests | The tenants selfishness invites in; the punishment the Giant chooses without knowing it |
| The linnet | A small songbird outside the window | The first sign of grace; what wakes the Giant |
| The trees and flowers | Personified plants | They refuse to blossom without the children — nature takes the children's side |

**The Giant.** He is not a villain in the ordinary sense. He is stupid rather
than cruel — his "conversation was limited", and his whole moral argument is one
childish sentence repeated: "My own garden is my own garden." His selfishness is
the ordinary human kind: he has a legal right, and he uses it without once asking
what it costs anybody else. Notice that his punishment is not sent from outside.
He builds a wall to keep the children out, and the wall keeps Spring out too;
when the birds and blossom go, he is the one left sitting at a cold window
complaining that the weather is late. His change begins not with an argument but
with **pity** — he sees one small child who cannot reach the branches and cannot
stop crying. From that moment he acts: he lifts, he knocks down the wall, he
gives the garden away. By the end he has become the opposite of what he was, and
the proof is that he prefers the children to his flowers.

**The little boy.** He is the smallest and weakest figure in the story and the
most powerful. He cannot climb; he needs to be picked up. He is the one child who
does not run away from the Giant — because he is blinded by tears, which is
Wilde's quiet way of saying that love does not calculate. His kiss, not any
sermon, melts the Giant. On his second and final appearance the nail-prints on
his hands and feet identify him as the crucified Christ, and his words — "these
are the wounds of Love" and the promise of "my garden, which is Paradise" — turn
the whole tale into a parable of redemption. Note also the exchange in which the
allegory is made explicit: the Giant once let the child play in his garden, and so
the child now takes him into his.

**The children.** They work as a group, not as individuals, and none of them is
named. They are what makes a place alive: where they are, there is blossom and
birdsong; where they are not, there is frost. Their fear of the Giant is
important too. When he first comes into the garden to help, they run, and winter
returns instantly — a reminder that a reputation for cruelty outlives the
cruelty, and that trust has to be rebuilt.

## 1.4 Themes

**Selfishness punishes itself.** Nobody sentences the Giant. He fences in what he
loves and discovers that he has fenced out everything that made it worth loving.
The clearest sign is the sentence in which he sits at the window of a cold white
garden and says he cannot understand why Spring is so late. He is not being
punished by the weather; he *is* the weather. Autumn's one line of explanation —
"He is too selfish" — is the story's moral centre.

**Love and repentance can restore what was lost.** The turn happens in a single
paragraph: the Giant's "heart melted", he names his own fault ("How selfish I
have been!"), and he immediately does something about it. Wilde is careful about
the order — first the feeling, then the confession, then the axe that knocks down
the wall. Real repentance in this story is an action, not a mood.

**Childhood, innocence and joy.** Wilde repeatedly ties life itself to children.
The birds "did not care to sing" in a garden with no children; the trees forget
to blossom; and the old Giant's last judgement on his beautiful garden is that
"the children are the most beautiful flowers of all". Innocence in Wilde's tales
is not weak — it is the force that changes adults.

**Walls, exclusion and property.** The wall and the notice-board
(TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED) bring the language of law and ownership into a
fairy tale. The Giant is perfectly within his rights, and that is the point: a
right can be exercised in a way that destroys the person holding it. The flower
that sees the notice-board and goes back to sleep is a small, sharp joke at the
expense of legal language.

**Christian allegory: sacrifice, forgiveness and Paradise.** The final movement
lifts the story out of the nursery. The child who cannot be found, who returns
after many years, who carries the wounds of the nails and calls them "the wounds
of Love", and who leads the old man into a garden that is Paradise, is
unmistakably Christ. The Giant's death is not a tragedy but a reward: he dies
under the tree, covered in white blossom — the colour of innocence — at the
moment of winter, because for him winter is now only "the Spring asleep".

## 1.5 Setting, structure and point of view

**Setting.** The story happens in no named country and no named year — a castle, a
garden, a village school, a market at twelve o'clock. This vagueness is
deliberate: it is the setting of a fairy tale or a parable, so that the events
feel like they could belong to anyone. The important setting is *inside* the
garden, and it changes with the Giant's heart. Three states are worth
distinguishing: the shared garden (all four seasons working normally), the walled
garden (permanent winter), and the restored garden (spring, and finally a single
tree in miraculous blossom in midwinter).

**Structure.** The tale falls into four movements: (i) the garden before the
Giant's return; (ii) the wall and the long winter; (iii) the morning of the
linnet, the little boy, and the knocking down of the wall — the climax; (iv) a
coda covering many years, ending with the child's return and the Giant's death.
The last movement is what makes it a work of literature rather than a moral fable:
the problem is solved at the climax, and Wilde keeps writing, because the story he
actually wants to tell is about what the Giant is *given* in the end.

**Point of view.** A third-person omniscient narrator tells the story — a voice
that can enter the Giant's thoughts ("his heart melted"), report what the Snow
and the Autumn say, and pass judgement in its own person ("He was a very selfish
Giant."). The register is deliberately simple, with many short sentences beginning
"And". At the very end the language shifts into archaic, biblical English —
*hath*, *thee*, *thou*, *Nay* — which signals to the reader that we have moved
from a tale to a sacred story.

## 1.6 Literary devices

| Device | Example from the text | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Personification | Snow "covered up the grass with her great white cloak"; the Frost "painted all the trees silver"; Autumn refuses to give fruit | Turns weather into characters who take sides, so that nature itself judges the Giant |
| Symbolism | The wall; the notice-board; the garden; the twelve peach-trees; the farthest corner | Lets a very short story carry a large moral argument without preaching |
| Allegory | The little boy with nail-prints; "my garden, which is Paradise" | Converts the whole tale into a Christian parable of sacrifice and salvation |
| Irony | The Giant sits in a frozen garden wondering why Spring is late; the notice threatens to prosecute trespassers, yet the Giant becomes the prisoner | The victim of the wall turns out to be the man who built it |
| Foreshadowing | The one corner that stays winter; the boy who cannot be found afterwards and whom nobody has ever seen | Prepares the reader for the final revelation, so the ending surprises without cheating |
| Imagery (colour and season) | Pink and pearl blossom, silver trees, golden branches, silver fruit, white blossoms | Makes the moral states visible: bright colour for love, grey and white for a closed heart |
| Pathetic fallacy | The weather in the garden exactly matches the Giant's feelings | Compresses the character's inner life into one image the reader can see |
| Repetition and parallel structure | "How happy we are here!" becomes "How happy we were there"; "Who hath dared to wound thee?" said twice | Marks change over time and raises the emotional pitch at the climax |
| Biblical diction | *hath*, *thee*, *thou*, *Nay*, "a strange awe fell on him" | Lifts the ending from fairy tale to scripture |

::: definition Allegory
A story in which the characters, places and events stand for something else —
usually moral, political or religious ideas — so that the whole narrative can be
read twice, literally and figuratively.
:::

::: tip Exam technique for a two-level story
When a question asks "why does Spring not come to the Giant's garden?", give both
levels in two sentences. Literal: the children are shut out, so the birds do not
sing and the trees do not blossom. Symbolic: the Giant's heart is closed, and the
garden's weather mirrors his heart. Examiners award the second sentence.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Oscar Wilde (1854–1900), Irish, leader of Aestheticism; "The Selfish Giant"
  comes from *The Happy Prince and Other Tales* (1888).
- A Giant returns after seven years with the Cornish ogre, drives the children
  out of his garden, walls it and posts TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED.
- Spring, Summer and Autumn never come; Snow, Frost, North Wind and Hail settle
  in — the Giant's selfishness becomes his own punishment.
- A linnet's song and the sight of one small crying boy melt the Giant's heart; he
  lifts the boy into the tree, breaks down the wall and gives the garden to the
  children.
- The boy disappears. Years later he returns with nail-prints in his hands and
  feet, calls them "the wounds of Love", and takes the dying Giant to Paradise.
- Themes: selfishness punishes itself; love and repentance restore life; the value
  of childhood innocence; walls and exclusion; Christian sacrifice and salvation.
- Devices: personification of the seasons, pathetic fallacy, symbolism of the wall
  and garden, situational irony, Christian allegory, biblical diction at the close.
- Structure: four movements, third-person omniscient narration, with the real
  climax followed by a long coda covering many years.

## Practice questions

**Short answer questions (2 marks each)**

1. Why did the Giant build a wall around his garden? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
2. Who lived in the garden all the year round after the wall was built, and why were they pleased? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
3. What made the Giant realise how selfish he had been? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
4. Why did the little boy not run away when the Giant came into the garden? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. What did the Giant mean when he said that winter was "merely the Spring asleep"? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. What were the wounds on the little boy's hands and feet, and how did he explain them? <span class="marks">[2]</span>

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** The Giant came home after seven years and found the village children
playing in his garden. He believed that "my own garden is my own garden" and
would allow nobody but himself to enjoy it, so he built a high wall and put up a
notice-board warning that trespassers would be prosecuted.

**2.** The Snow and the Frost stayed all year, and they invited the North Wind
and then the Hail. They were pleased because Spring had forgotten the garden,
which left them free to live there permanently and spoil it — the Snow covering
the grass, the Frost silvering the trees, the Hail breaking the roof slates.

**3.** One morning he heard a linnet singing and looked out to see the children
sitting in the blossoming trees, having crept in through a hole in the wall. In
one corner a tiny boy was crying because he could not reach the branches. That
sight made the Giant's heart melt and he said, "How selfish I have been!"

**4.** He did not run because his eyes were so full of tears that he simply did
not see the Giant coming. On the symbolic level, his staying suggests that
innocent love does not fear, and it is this one child who remains who becomes the
means of the Giant's change and salvation.

**5.** He meant that winter is not death but a pause: the flowers are only
resting under the ground and will come back. It shows how much he has changed —
the man who once complained that Spring was late can now wait patiently, because
he has learned that love, like spring, returns.

**6.** There were the prints of two nails on his palms and two on his little feet
— the marks of the Crucifixion. When the Giant angrily demanded who had dared to
wound him, the child answered, "Nay! but these are the wounds of Love,"
identifying himself as Christ, who was wounded out of love for humankind.
:::

**Long answer questions (5 marks each)**

1. "The Selfish Giant" is far more than a children's story. Discuss the story as
   a Christian allegory. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Trace the change in the Giant's character from the beginning of the story to
   his death, and explain what brings that change about. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. How does Wilde use the garden and the seasons to show the inner condition of
   the Giant? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** On the surface Wilde tells a simple tale, but its details point steadily
towards Christian teaching. The garden stands for the human soul; the high wall
with its legal notice stands for selfishness and pride; and the endless winter
stands for a life from which love has been shut out. The turning point is caused
by the weakest character in the story, a child too small to climb a tree — an
echo of the Gospel idea that the least are the greatest. When the child returns
at the end, the allegory becomes explicit: he bears the prints of two nails on
his hands and two on his feet, and when the Giant demands the name of the person
who wounded him, he answers that "these are the wounds of Love". This is Christ
crucified, and his wounds are voluntary. His final words make the exchange plain:
because the Giant once let him play in his garden, he will now take the Giant to
his own garden, "which is Paradise". The Giant's death is therefore not a
punishment but a reward, and the white blossom that covers his body is the colour
of forgiveness and innocence. Read this way, the story is about repentance,
grace and salvation, not merely about learning to share.

**2.** At the start the Giant is selfish, ignorant and lonely. He has spent seven
years with the Cornish ogre and has nothing left to say; his only moral idea is
the possessive sentence "my own garden is my own garden". He drives the children
out, walls the garden, threatens the law, and Wilde ends the paragraph with a
blunt verdict: "He was a very selfish Giant." His punishment is automatic —
the garden freezes — yet he is too dull to see the cause, and sits at the window
wondering why Spring is late. The change is brought about by two small things, a
linnet's song and the sight of one crying boy who cannot reach the branches. Pity
does what argument could not. He admits his fault, lifts the boy into the tree,
takes an axe to his own wall, and gives the garden to the children for ever. In
old age he is a different man: gentle, patient, no longer hating winter, and
valuing the children above his flowers. The love he receives from the child —
first a kiss, and finally the promise of Paradise — completes a change that began
in pity and ended in humility, for he kneels before the child at the last.

**3.** Wilde makes the garden a picture of the Giant's inner life, so that the
reader can *see* his moral state. While the children are welcome, all four
seasons work: blossom in spring, fruit in autumn, birds in the trees. The moment
he builds the wall, the garden is cut off from the ordinary year and locked into a
single season. Nature is personified to make the judgement clear — the birds "did
not care to sing" where there were no children, the trees "forgot to blossom", and
Autumn refuses fruit with the words, "He is too selfish." Snow, Frost, the North
Wind and the Hail move in as guests, so that the Giant's chosen isolation is
staffed by cold, noise and damage. This is **pathetic fallacy**: the weather
carries the feeling. When the children creep back, blossom returns tree by tree,
and the one corner that stays frozen marks the one part of the Giant not yet
reached by love. At the end the garden goes beyond nature altogether: in
midwinter a single tree stands in white blossom with golden branches and silver
fruit — no longer a report on the Giant's heart but a promise of Paradise.
:::

**Reference to the context**

1. "My own garden is my own garden."

   (a) Who says this, and to whom? (b) What action follows immediately from this
   statement? (c) What does the sentence reveal about the speaker's character?
   (d) How does the story finally answer this claim? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

2. "Nay! but these are the wounds of Love."

   (a) Who speaks these words, and in reply to what question? (b) What are the
   wounds referred to? (c) Explain the significance of this line in the story.
   (d) What happens immediately afterwards? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — reference to the context
**1.** (a) The Selfish Giant says it, speaking to himself in the presence of the
children he has just frightened away from his garden, immediately after returning
from seven years with the Cornish ogre. (b) He at once builds a high wall all
round the garden and puts up a notice-board reading TRESPASSERS WILL BE
PROSECUTED. (c) The repetition of "my own" in a single short sentence shows a
mind that can think only in terms of ownership. He is not cruel so much as
narrow: he has a legal right and never asks what using it will cost the children.
The flat statement that follows — "He was a very selfish Giant" — confirms the
narrator's judgement. (d) The story answers him by showing that the wall shuts
out Spring as well as the children, so the owner loses the very thing he was
guarding. Later he reverses the sentence himself: "It is your garden now, little
children," he says, and knocks the wall down with an axe.

**2.** (a) The little boy — the Christ figure — speaks them, in answer to the
Giant's furious question, "Who hath dared to wound thee?", asked twice when he
saw the marks on the child. (b) The wounds are the prints of two nails on the
palms of the child's hands and two on his small feet: the marks of the
Crucifixion. (c) The line turns the fairy tale into a Christian allegory. The
Giant assumes the wounds are an injury to be avenged with his big sword; the child
corrects him by saying they were accepted willingly, out of love. Love in this
story is therefore not comfortable feeling but sacrifice, and the Giant — who
learned love from this child years before — is being shown its full meaning.
(d) A strange awe falls on the Giant and he kneels before the child, who smiles
and says that because the Giant once let him play in his garden he shall today
come to the child's garden, which is Paradise. That afternoon the children find
the Giant lying dead under the tree, covered all over with white blossoms.
:::
