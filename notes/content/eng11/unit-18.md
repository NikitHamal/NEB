A man has been strangled in his bed. Three men search the house for evidence and
find nothing. Two women, left behind in the kitchen among the dirty pans and the
sewing basket, find everything — and say nothing. That is the whole of *Trifles*,
and it is one of the most perfectly built one-act plays ever written.

::: key What the exam wants from this play
The **plot** (who found the body, what the women discover, what they do with it);
the **characters**, especially the two women and the absent Mrs Wright; the
**symbols** — the birdcage, the canary, the quilt, the preserves, the cold
kitchen; the **irony of the title** and the **dramatic irony** that the women know
what the men do not; the **setting** as a cause rather than a background; and the
**themes** — gender, isolation, loyalty between women, and the difference between
law and justice.
:::

## 18.1 About the author

**Susan Glaspell (1876–1948)** was an American playwright, novelist, journalist
and actress. She first became known for her short stories — about fifty were
published — and she went on to write nine novels, fifteen plays and a biography.
Her work typically explores contemporary social issues such as gender, ethics and
dissent, and it is known for deep, sympathetic characters. Her first novel, *The
Glory of the Conquered*, appeared in 1909, and she wrote three best-selling
novels: *Brook Evans* (1928), *Fugitive's Return* (1929) and *Ambrose Holt and
Family* (1931).

```photo id="eng11/susan-glaspell" caption="Susan Glaspell (1876–1948). She reported a real Iowa murder trial as a young newspaperwoman, and sixteen years later turned what she had seen in that farmhouse kitchen into her first play." width="34"
```

*Trifles* (1916) was her **first play**, and it was based on a murder trial she
had covered as a young reporter in Des Moines. She wrote it for the Provincetown
Players, the small experimental theatre company she had helped to found, and she
later rewrote the same material as a short story, *A Jury of Her Peers* (1917) —
a title that says out loud what the play only implies. Her play *Alison's House*
(1930) won the **Pulitzer Prize for drama in 1931**. Glaspell is recognised today
as a pioneering feminist writer and as America's first important modern woman
playwright.

::: caution The real case behind the play
As a young newspaperwoman Glaspell reported the trial of a farmer's wife in rural
Iowa accused of killing her husband as he slept. What stayed with her was not the
legal argument but the farmhouse itself — the cold kitchen, the isolation, the
silence of a marriage that no court could enter. When she came to write the play
she changed the weapon, the names and the ending, and she made one radical
decision that the courtroom could never make: she left the accused woman offstage
and let the kitchen speak for her. You may mention the real trial as background;
do not treat the play as a report of it.
:::

## 18.2 The characters

| Character | Who they are |
|---|---|
| **George Henderson** | the County Attorney; a young man, ambitious, ironic, with "the gallantry of a young politician" |
| **Henry Peters** | the Sheriff; middle-aged, comfortable, sure that there is "nothing here but kitchen things" |
| **Lewis Hale** | a neighbouring farmer, who discovered the body and gives the opening narration |
| **Mrs Peters** | the Sheriff's wife; "a slight wiry woman, a thin nervous face"; begins as the voice of the law and changes completely |
| **Mrs Hale** | a neighbouring farmer's wife; larger, "more comfortable looking", but disturbed; the play's conscience |
| **Minnie Wright** | the accused; **never appears on stage** |
| **John Wright** | the murdered husband; dead before the play begins |

::: caution The two most important characters never appear
Neither Minnie nor John Wright is ever seen. Everything the audience knows about
the marriage is reconstructed from objects and from what the neighbours remember.
This is the boldest thing in the play: it forces the audience to do exactly the
detective work the women are doing, so that we convict John Wright and acquit his
wife by the same method the County Attorney is too proud to use.
:::

## 18.3 The play, scene by scene

The play has no act divisions; it runs continuously in the kitchen of the Wright
farmhouse. It can be followed in six movements.

### 1. The kitchen and the entrance

The stage direction sets everything up before a word is spoken: "the kitchen in
the now abandoned farmhouse of John Wright, a gloomy kitchen, and left without
having been put in order — unwashed pans under the sink, a loaf of bread outside
the bread-box, a dish-towel on the table — other signs of incomplete work."

Five people come in. The three men go straight to the stove; the two women "come
in slowly, and stand close together near the door." That grouping is the whole
play in one picture: the men own the space and take its warmth, the women hover at
the edge of it. Mrs Peters is offered the fire and answers, "I'm not — cold," and
the hesitation in that dash is the first sign that she is not as composed as she
looks.

### 2. Hale's narration

Lewis Hale tells what he saw the previous morning. He had called to ask John
Wright about sharing a **party telephone** — and he lets slip, without knowing
what he is revealing, that Wright had refused because "folks talked too much
anyway, and all he asked was peace and quiet", and that he had thought of asking
in front of Wright's wife, "though I said to Harry that I didn't know as what his
wife wanted made much difference to John." In two sentences the dead man is drawn:
a man who wanted no talk in his house and did not consult his wife.

Hale found Mrs Wright rocking, pleating her apron, and answering in flat little
sentences: "Is it?"… "He's dead"… "He died of a rope round his neck." When Harry
asked how she could have slept through it, she said only, "I sleep sound." Hale
tells all this as evidence of guilt; the audience will come to hear it as shock.

### 3. The men dismiss the kitchen

The County Attorney opens a cupboard and finds the preserves burst by the frost:
"Here's a nice mess." Mrs Peters explains that Mrs Wright had been anxious about
exactly this. The Sheriff laughs — "Well, can you beat the women! Held for murder
and worryin' about her preserves" — and Hale adds the line that gives the play its
title: **"Well, women are used to worrying over trifles."**

The County Attorney then inspects the dirty towel and the pans and asks, "Not much
of a housekeeper, would you say, ladies?" Mrs Hale answers stiffly that there is a
great deal of work to be done on a farm, and, when he presses, that "those towels
get dirty awful quick. Men's hands aren't always as clean as they might be." He
calls her "loyal to your sex" — a joke that turns out to be a prophecy. The men
then go upstairs and out to the barn, where they expect the evidence to be.

### 4. The women alone: the first discoveries

Left in the kitchen, the women begin to read the room, though at first they hardly
know they are doing it.

- The **bread** set to rise and left out of the box; the **half-wiped table**, one
  side clean and one side messy: someone stopped in the middle of her work.
- The **rocking chair**. Mrs Hale is about to sit in it, realises which chair it
  is, and steps back; the empty chair goes on rocking while both women watch. It
  is the play's most economical piece of theatre — the absent woman is suddenly
  present.
- The **clothes**. Examining the shabby skirt, Mrs Hale remembers: "Wright was
  close" — that is, mean with money — and that Minnie "used to wear pretty clothes
  and be lively, when she was Minnie Foster, one of the town girls singing in the
  choir. But that — oh, that was thirty years ago."
- The **apron and shawl** Mrs Wright has asked for in jail, which strike Mrs Hale
  as proof that she is not a murderer: "Asking for an apron and her little shawl.
  Worrying about her fruit."

Then the **quilt**. They admire the log-cabin pattern and wonder aloud whether she
meant to quilt it or knot it. The men come down, overhear, and laugh; the women
look abashed. And immediately after the laughter Mrs Hale finds the block Minnie
was working on last: "All the rest of it has been so nice and even. And look at
this! It's all over the place! Why, it looks as if she didn't know what she was
about!" The two women look at each other, glance at the door — and Mrs Hale quietly
unpicks the bad stitching and sews it properly. That is the first act of
concealment, and it is done almost without thought.

### 5. The birdcage and the box

Looking for paper and string, Mrs Peters finds a **birdcage** with its door broken
and one hinge pulled apart. "Looks as if someone must have been rough with it."
Mrs Hale remembers a man selling canaries cheap, and remembers too that Minnie
"used to sing real pretty herself."

The talk deepens. Mrs Hale admits her own guilt — "I wish I had come over
sometimes when she was here… It's a lonesome place and always was" — and
describes John Wright precisely: "he didn't drink, and kept his word as well as
most, I guess, and paid his debts. But he was a hard man… Like a raw wind that
gets to the bone." Of Minnie she says, "she was kind of like a bird herself — real
sweet and pretty, but kind of timid and — fluttery. How — she — did — change."

Then, in the sewing basket, the pretty box. Inside, wrapped in silk, is the
canary, its neck broken. "Somebody — wrung — its — neck." The stage direction is
exact: "Their eyes meet. A look of growing comprehension, of horror."

Everything is now clear. A man who could not bear a sound in his house wrung the
neck of the one thing his wife loved; she strangled him with a rope as he slept.
The motive the County Attorney needs is lying on the kitchen table.

### 6. The choice

When the men return, the women lie for the first time. Asked about the empty cage,
Mrs Hale says, "We think the — cat got it," and Mrs Peters supports her. Then,
alone again, the two speak in the manner of "feeling their way over strange
ground": Mrs Peters remembers the boy who killed her kitten with a hatchet, and
that if they had not held her back she would have hurt him; she remembers the
stillness of the Dakota homestead after her first baby died. Mrs Hale names the
other crime: "I wish you'd seen Minnie Foster when she wore a white dress with
blue ribbons and stood up there in the choir and sang… That was a crime! That was
a crime! Who's going to punish that?" And she states the play's moral discovery in
one sentence: **"We live close together and we live far apart. We all go through
the same things — it's all just a different kind of the same thing."**

Mrs Peters, who has been saying "the law is the law" and "the law has got to
punish crime", is the one who first tries to hide the box in her bag. Her hands
fail her; Mrs Hale snatches it and puts it in her coat pocket. The County Attorney
comes back joking — he has just told Mrs Peters that a sheriff's wife is "married
to the law" — and asks his little question one last time. Mrs Hale, standing with
her hand against the pocket that holds the evidence, gives the last line of the
play: **"We call it — knot it, Mr Henderson."**

## 18.4 The chain of clues

The women's method is not deduction from a single piece of evidence but the slow
assembling of small domestic facts, each meaningless by itself. It is worth
setting the chain out, because most examination questions about the play are
really questions about one link in it.

## 18.5 The characters in detail

**Mrs Hale** is the play's conscience and its memory. She is the one who knew
Minnie Foster before her marriage, and therefore the only person on stage who can
measure what has been lost. She is blunt with the County Attorney from the start,
defending Minnie's housekeeping and her own sex. But her most important quality is
**guilt**: she reproaches herself for having stayed away — "I stayed away because
it weren't cheerful — and that's why I ought to have come." Her decision to hide
the box is partly an act of justice and partly an attempt to repay a debt she
knows she cannot repay.

**Mrs Peters** is the character who actually changes, and she is therefore the
dramatic centre. She enters as an extension of her husband — nervous, correct,
repeating that "the law is the law" and "the law has got to punish crime." Three
things break her open: the broken birdcage, which makes her remember the boy who
killed her kitten and the violence she felt then; the stillness she remembers from
Dakota after her first baby died; and the bird itself. The County Attorney's joke
that a sheriff's wife is "married to the law" is said over the head of a woman who
has, minutes before, tried to smuggle the evidence out in her handbag. Her failure
of nerve at the last moment — she cannot touch the dead bird — is what makes her
believable: this is a conversion, not a conspiracy.

**Minnie Wright** exists only in other people's memory and in her own objects.
There are two of her. There is **Minnie Foster**, one of the town girls, who wore
a white dress with blue ribbons and sang in the choir; and there is Mrs Wright,
thirty years later, in a lonesome house in a hollow, in shabby clothes, with no
children, no telephone, no Ladies' Aid, and a caged bird. The play's method is to
let the audience feel the distance between those two women, and to understand that
somebody travelled it.

**John Wright** is convicted in half a dozen sentences, none of them hostile.
"They say he was a good man." "He didn't drink, and kept his word as well as most,
I guess, and paid his debts. But he was a hard man… Like a raw wind that gets to
the bone." He refused the party telephone because "folks talked too much anyway."
He was "close" with money. This is the play's quietest and most frightening point:
a man can be respectable by every public measure and still destroy someone
privately, and the law has no name for what he did.

**The men as a group** — Henderson, Peters and Hale — are not villains. They are
competent, jocular, and entirely blinded by their assumption that nothing
important happens in a kitchen. The County Attorney is the sharpest of them and
the most wrong: he actually stands beside the box of quilt pieces, moves a few
things about, and steps back, saying, "No, Mrs Peters doesn't need supervising."

## 18.6 Symbolism

**The birdcage.** A pretty enclosure with a door wrenched off its hinge. It is the
Wright house, and Minnie is what was kept inside it. The broken door tells two
stories at once: a man's violence in getting at the bird, and the fact that
something finally got out.

**The canary.** Minnie "used to sing real pretty herself"; she was "kind of like a
bird herself — real sweet and pretty, but kind of timid and — fluttery." The bird
is her own lost voice and her one remaining pleasure, bought cheap from a passing
salesman. Its wrung neck and John Wright's rope-marked neck are deliberately the
same injury; Glaspell makes Mrs Hale say it: "His neck. Choked the life out of
him." When Minnie wraps the bird in silk and puts it in her prettiest box, she is
burying the last of Minnie Foster.

**The quilt.** The needlework is Minnie's handwriting; the even blocks are the
years of control and the wild block is the night of the murder. And the question
the men laugh at — "quilt it or knot it?" — becomes the answer: a quilt is sewn
through, but a knot is tied and pulled tight, which is exactly how John Wright
died. The last line of the play is a confession that only the women can hear.

**The preserves.** The frozen, burst jars are Minnie's work of a whole hot summer,
destroyed in one cold night — like her life. The men laugh that she worries about
them while held for murder; the women understand that a woman who still cares
about her fruit has not stopped being herself. The single unbroken jar of cherries
that Mrs Hale decides to take her — with the lie that none was lost — is the play's
one act of tenderness.

**The rocking chair.** Mrs Wright was rocking in it when Hale found her, and the
empty chair rocks by itself when Mrs Hale touches it. An unoccupied chair that
moves is the play's image of a woman who is present everywhere and visible
nowhere.

**The cold, and the stove.** The play opens below zero. The men take the fire; Mrs
Peters says "I'm not — cold"; the fruit freezes because the fire went out. Warmth
in this play means company and kindness, and the Wright house has run out of both.

## 18.7 Setting, structure and technique

**Setting.** The whole play happens in one room, in real time, on a bitterly cold
morning in a farmhouse "down in a hollow" where "you don't see the road." The
setting is not a background; it is the cause. Isolation is what destroyed Minnie
Wright — no telephone, no neighbours, no children, no Ladies' Aid — and the play
makes the audience share that isolation by never letting them out of the kitchen.
Confining the action to the room the men despise is also the structural joke on
which everything depends: the audience is locked in with the evidence.

**Structure.** *Trifles* is a model of the well-made one-act play: a single
setting, a single continuous action, and a plot in which every step is also a
discovery.

- *Exposition* — the entrance and Hale's narration, which give us the murder.
- *Rising action* — the men's dismissal of the kitchen, and the women's succession
  of small finds: the preserves, the bread, the rocker, the clothes, the quilt.
- *Climax* — the box is opened and the dead canary is found. "Their eyes meet."
- *Falling action* — the two women talk their way to an understanding, and lie to
  the men about the cat.
- *Resolution* — the box goes into Mrs Hale's pocket, and the curtain line is a
  pun that the men cannot hear.

**Dramatic irony.** From the moment the box is opened, the audience and the two
women know the motive, and the three investigators do not. Every line the men
speak afterwards is therefore double: "it's all perfectly clear except a reason
for doing it"; "I guess they're not very dangerous things the ladies have picked
out"; "a sheriff's wife is married to the law"; "Has the bird flown?" The tension
of the last five minutes comes entirely from the audience's fear that one of these
casual sentences will accidentally hit the truth.

**Irony of the title.** *Trifles* is the men's word — "women are used to worrying
over trifles" — and the play spends thirty minutes proving that the trifles are
the evidence. The irony is structural, not decorative: the men fail *because* they
use the word.

**Dialogue and silence.** Glaspell writes rural American speech exactly —
"s'pose", "ain't it", "readied-up", "close" for mean. But her real instrument is
the pause. The dashes in "How — she — did — change" and "Somebody — wrung —
its — neck" do more than any speech could, and the stage directions repeatedly ask
the actresses to communicate without words: "Their eyes meet"; "The two women sit
there not looking at one another, but as if peering into something"; "her own eyes
point the way to where the box is concealed."

::: definition Dramatic irony
**Dramatic irony** occurs when the audience knows something that one or more
characters on stage do not. It creates tension, because we watch people act on
incomplete knowledge, and it can create either comedy or dread. In *Trifles* the
audience shares the women's knowledge, so the irony makes us accomplices: we are
hiding the box too.
:::

## 18.8 Themes

**The trivialisation of women's lives.** The men's error is not cruelty but
contempt: kitchens, preserves, sewing and birds are beneath notice. The play
answers by making a kitchen the only place where the truth can be found.

**Isolation.** Minnie Wright had no telephone because her husband liked quiet, no
new clothes because he was close, no children, and no visitors because the house
was not cheerful. Mrs Hale's cry — "I might have known she needed help!" — extends
the charge from John Wright to the whole neighbourhood, and to us.

**Law against justice.** Mrs Peters begins with "the law is the law" and ends by
helping to defeat it. The play does not claim that Minnie is innocent of the
killing; it claims that the law is about to try only half of the case. Mrs Hale
names the untried half: "That was a crime! That was a crime! Who's going to punish
that?"

**Female solidarity, and the guilt that produces it.** The two women are not
friends at the start, and neither of them knows Minnie well. What unites them is
the recognition that her life could have been theirs: "We all go through the same
things — it's all just a different kind of the same thing." That sentence, not the
dead bird, is the real discovery of the play.

**The invisible marriage.** The play is finally about what cannot be seen from
outside a house. John Wright was, in public, a good man. Everything that destroyed
his wife happened where no one was looking — which is exactly where the play sets
its stage.

| Device | Example | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Irony of title | "women are used to worrying over trifles" | The despised trifles turn out to be the entire case |
| Dramatic irony | "Well, ladies, have you decided whether she was going to quilt it or knot it?" | The audience holds the answer; the speaker does not |
| Symbol | the canary with its neck wrung | Equates the bird's death with the wife's silencing and the husband's murder |
| Parallelism | "Somebody wrung its neck" / "a rope round his neck" | Makes the punishment fit the crime without a word of comment |
| Pun | "knot it" / "not it" | The curtain line is simultaneously a sewing term, a method of murder and a denial |
| Understatement | "But he was a hard man… Like a raw wind that gets to the bone" | Condemns a man in one simile, with no raised voice |
| Foreshadowing | the empty rocking chair rocking by itself | Announces the absent woman before any clue is found |
| Stage silence | "Their eyes meet. A look of growing comprehension, of horror." | The play's climax happens with no dialogue at all |
| Contrast | men upstairs and in the barn / women in the kitchen | Turns stage geography into the play's argument |

::: tip The one thing to get right
Never write that the women "solve the murder". They do more and less than that.
They discover the **motive** — the thing the County Attorney says he needs — and
then they suppress it. The play's subject is not detection but **judgement**: two
ordinary women quietly decide that the law, as it stands, is not competent to try
this case, and act on that decision.
:::

## Chapter summary

- *Trifles* (1916) is Susan Glaspell's first play, based on a murder trial she
  covered as a young reporter in Des Moines; she later rewrote it as the story *A
  Jury of Her Peers*.
- John Wright has been strangled with a rope in his bed; his wife Minnie, who says
  she "slept sound", is in custody. Neither of them appears on stage.
- The Sheriff, the County Attorney and the neighbour Hale search upstairs and in
  the barn for evidence of a **motive** and find nothing, dismissing the kitchen as
  holding "nothing here but kitchen things" and women's "trifles".
- Left in the kitchen, Mrs Hale and Mrs Peters read the room: unfinished work, a
  burst jar of preserves, shabby clothes, an empty rocking chair, and a quilt whose
  last block is stitched wildly.
- They find a **birdcage with its door broken** and then, in a pretty box, the
  **canary with its neck wrung**. The motive is complete: a hard, silent husband
  killed the one thing that sang, and his wife answered in kind.
- Mrs Peters, who begins by insisting that "the law is the law", is changed by
  memories of her murdered kitten and of the stillness after her baby's death; Mrs
  Hale is driven by guilt at never having visited.
- The women lie to the men ("we think the cat got it") and hide the box. The
  closing pun — "We call it — knot it, Mr Henderson" — is a confession the men
  cannot hear.
- Symbols: the birdcage (the marriage), the canary (Minnie's voice), the quilt
  (her state of mind, and the method of killing), the preserves (a life's work
  destroyed in one cold night), the empty rocker, and the cold itself.
- Themes: the trivialising of women's lives, isolation, the gap between **law and
  justice**, and the solidarity that grows from the recognition that "we all go
  through the same things — it's all just a different kind of the same thing."

## Practice questions

**Short answer questions (2 marks each)**

1. Do you believe that Mrs Wright killed her husband? Explain. <span class="marks">[2]</span>
2. Do you think Mr Wright's death would have been uncovered if Mr Hale hadn't
   stopped by the Wrights' home? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
3. Why does Mrs Hale think that Mrs Wright's worries about her preserves indicate
   her innocence? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
4. How does Mrs Peters' homesteading experience connect her to Mrs Wright? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. How do the women's perspectives on men differ? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. Why is the play called *Trifles*? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
7. What do the women do with the dead canary, and why? <span class="marks">[2]</span>

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** Yes. The play never shows the killing, but the evidence the women assemble
points only one way: the rope was the Wrights' own, there was "no sign at all of
anyone having come from the outside", Minnie was in the bed beside him, and the
wildly stitched quilt block shows her hands shaking that night. The broken cage
and the strangled canary supply the motive — the husband killed the one thing she
loved, and she answered with the same injury to his neck. Mrs Hale's protest that
"I don't think she did it" is not really a claim about the facts; it is a refusal
to let the word "murderer" stand for the whole of what happened in that house.

**2.** Probably yes, but not so soon, and perhaps not in a way that would have
convicted her. The Wrights lived in a lonesome hollow where "you don't see the
road", and had refused a party telephone, so news travelled slowly; Minnie herself
had notified nobody and was simply sitting in her rocker pleating her apron when
Hale arrived. Sooner or later a neighbour, the Ladies' Aid or a delivery would have
brought someone to the door. But the delay would have mattered: the kitchen might
have been tidied, the bird disposed of, and the story rehearsed. Hale's accidental
call on the morning after is what freezes the scene exactly as Minnie left it —
which is why the kitchen can be read at all.

**3.** Because a woman who has just killed her husband and been dragged to jail
would not, Mrs Hale reasons, still be anxious about a summer's work in jars — and
because the same woman asked for an apron and her little shawl, the small
belongings of an ordinary life. "Asking for an apron and her little shawl.
Worrying about her fruit." To Mrs Hale these are the concerns of a housewife, not
of a criminal. The judgement is of course sentimental rather than logical, and the
play knows it: Mrs Hale will shortly find the dead canary. But the observation is
still true in a deeper way. Minnie is not a hardened killer; she is an ordinary
woman who broke once, and the preserves prove it.

**4.** Mrs Peters tells how she and her husband homesteaded in Dakota and how her
first baby died at two years old, leaving her with no other child: "I know what
stillness is." That is the precise link. Minnie Wright lived for thirty years in a
silent house with no children and a husband who wanted only "peace and quiet", and
the canary was the one sound in it. Because Mrs Peters has herself lived through a
stillness that nearly destroyed her, she can imagine what it would be "if there'd
been years and years of nothing, then a bird to sing to you… still, after the bird
was still." Her own past, not any argument of Mrs Hale's, is what turns the
sheriff's wife into Minnie's defender.

**5.** Mrs Hale judges men freely and is ready to say so aloud. She answers back to
the County Attorney about the towels ("Men's hands aren't always as clean as they
might be"), resents them "snooping around and criticizing" in another woman's
kitchen, and describes John Wright to his neighbours' faces as "a hard man… Like a
raw wind that gets to the bone." Mrs Peters begins by excusing the men at every
turn — "Of course it's no more than their duty", "they've got awful important
things on their minds", "the law is the law" — because she is married to the
Sheriff and identifies with his authority. By the end the difference has almost
vanished: Mrs Peters is the first to reach for the box. The play measures its own
progress by the closing of this gap.

**6.** The title comes from Hale's dismissive line, "Well, women are used to
worrying over trifles," spoken when the men laugh at Mrs Wright for worrying about
her frozen preserves. It is deeply **ironic**, because the trifles the men despise
— the preserves, the bread, the quilt, the birdcage and the dead canary — are
precisely the evidence that reveals the motive for the murder. The men search the
bedroom and the barn and find nothing; the women stay among the trifles and find
everything. The title therefore states the men's prejudice and the play's argument
in a single word.

**7.** Mrs Peters first tries to hide the box in the bag she is carrying, but it is
too big, and when she opens it she cannot bring herself to touch the bird; Mrs
Hale then snatches the box and thrusts it into the pocket of her big coat, where it
is when she speaks the last line of the play. They hide it because it is the one
thing that would supply the motive the County Attorney says he needs — "something
to show anger, or sudden feeling" — and because they have decided, without ever
saying so, that a court which would hang Minnie Wright while never trying John
Wright is not entitled to the evidence.
:::

**Long answer questions (5 marks each)**

1. What is the main theme of the play? Discuss it with reference to the text. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Discuss the symbolism used in the play. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Discuss the setting of the play. Does it have an impact on the theme? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. Analyse Glaspell's use of dramatic irony. What information is crucial to the
   play? How does the playwright use it to create irony, and what effect does that
   irony have on the audience? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
5. "The credibility of a character is determined not only by the character's
   thoughts and actions but also by what other characters say and think about him
   or her." Discuss in relation to the characters of *Trifles*. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** The main theme of *Trifles* is the **systematic undervaluing of women and
of women's knowledge**, and the injustice that follows from it. Every other theme
in the play — isolation, marriage, law against justice, female solidarity —
branches from this one.

The theme is announced in the stage directions before anyone speaks. The men walk
in and go straight to the stove; the women "come in slowly, and stand close
together near the door." It is stated in words when the Sheriff laughs at a woman
"held for murder and worryin' about her preserves" and Hale adds, "women are used
to worrying over trifles." It governs the plot: the men search the bedroom, the
barn and the windows, and the County Attorney concludes that "it's all perfectly
clear except a reason for doing it", while the women, sitting in the room he has
dismissed as holding "nothing here but kitchen things", find the reason within
half an hour.

The theme then deepens from a joke about housekeeping into something far more
serious. What the kitchen reveals is a marriage in which a woman was slowly
extinguished: no telephone because "folks talked too much anyway", no money for
clothes because "Wright was close", no Ladies' Aid, no children, no visitors, and
finally no bird. John Wright committed no offence the law recognises, and Mrs Hale
says so with real anger: "I wish you'd seen Minnie Foster when she wore a white
dress with blue ribbons and stood up there in the choir and sang… That was a
crime! That was a crime! Who's going to punish that?" The play's answer is that
nobody will, because the institution that decides what counts as a crime is made
up of men who cannot see a kitchen.

Out of that recognition grows the play's third movement: **solidarity**. Mrs Hale
and Mrs Peters are not Minnie's friends; what binds them to her is the discovery
that her life is a version of their own — "We live close together and we live far
apart. We all go through the same things — it's all just a different kind of the
same thing." Having understood that, they do the only thing available to them.
They cannot testify, argue or sit on the jury; they can hide a small box. The
theme therefore ends not in a complaint but in an action, and in the quiet pun of
the last line, which is both a sewing term and a verdict.

**2.** Glaspell's symbols are all ordinary household objects, which is itself part
of the argument: in a world that notices nothing a woman touches, the meaning has
to be hidden in plain sight.

**The birdcage** is the central symbol. It is a pretty container built to keep a
singing creature from flying away — an exact image of the Wright marriage — and
when the women find it, its "door. It's broke. One hinge is pulled apart." The
damage is violent and recent, and it carries two meanings simultaneously: the
husband's brutality in getting at the bird, and the fact that something in that
house finally broke out of its enclosure.

**The canary** is Minnie herself. Mrs Hale remembers that Minnie "used to sing
real pretty herself" and was "kind of like a bird herself — real sweet and pretty,
but kind of timid and — fluttery." When John Wright wrings the bird's neck he is
killing the last audible remnant of Minnie Foster, and the symbol is completed by
the method of his own death: a rope round the neck. Mrs Hale's flat repetition —
"His neck. Choked the life out of him" — with her hand resting on the cage, is the
moment the symbol closes.

**The quilt** works on two levels. As an object it is Minnie's mind made visible:
the blocks are "so nice and even" until the last one, which is "all over the
place… as if she didn't know what she was about." As a word it produces the play's
final irony, since the men's mocking question — quilt it or knot it? — is answered
by the murder itself. A knot is pulled tight, and so was the rope.

**The preserves** stand for a woman's labour and its fragility. A whole summer's
work in the hot weather is destroyed in one freezing night because the fire went
out, which is a fair description of Minnie's life. The men see only "a nice mess";
Mrs Hale sees the work, and her decision to carry the one surviving jar of cherries
to the jail with a comforting lie is the play's single act of pure kindness.

Two smaller symbols matter. **The empty rocking chair**, which rocks by itself
after Mrs Hale touches it, brings the absent woman physically into the room. And
**the cold** — the sub-zero morning, the men crowding the stove, the fire that went
out, Mrs Peters' "I'm not — cold" — is the play's constant image for a house, and a
marriage, with no warmth left in it.

**3.** The setting of *Trifles* is a single room: the kitchen of the Wrights' now
abandoned farmhouse, on a bitterly cold morning in rural Iowa, the day after the
murder. The play observes the classical unities almost exactly — one place, one
continuous action, roughly real time — and every element of the setting is doing
thematic work.

**The place is remote.** The house lies "down in a hollow and you don't see the
road", and Mrs Hale calls it "a lonesome place and always was." John Wright had
refused a party telephone. There are no children and no near neighbours. This
isolation is not atmosphere; it is the cause of the tragedy. A woman cut off from
every human contact, married to a man who wanted only "peace and quiet", had
nothing left but a canary — which is why killing the canary was enough to kill
him.

**The room is a kitchen**, and the entire structure of the play depends on that
choice. By confining the action to the one space the men consider worthless,
Glaspell locks the audience in with the evidence and makes them see what the
investigators cannot. Had she set the play in the bedroom where the body lay, or
in a courtroom, it would have been an ordinary detective piece. Set in the
kitchen, it becomes an argument about what counts as knowledge.

**The disorder is legible.** The opening stage direction lists unwashed pans, a
loaf of bread outside the bread-box, a dish-towel on the table, "other signs of
incomplete work", and the play later adds a table wiped clean on one half only.
To the County Attorney this means "not much of a housekeeper"; to the women it
means a woman interrupted in the middle of her morning. The same room supplies
opposite conclusions depending on who reads it, and that is the play's subject.

**The cold organises the staging.** It is below zero; Frank has been sent to make
a fire; the men go at once to the stove and keep returning to it; the preserves
have burst in the frost. Warmth in this play means human company, and there is
none in this house.

So yes — the setting does not merely support the theme, it produces it. The
isolation creates the crime, the kitchen creates the irony, the disorder creates
the evidence, and the cold supplies the emotional temperature of a marriage.

**4.** **The crucial information** is the dead canary with its neck wrung, hidden
in the pretty box under the quilt pieces, together with everything that follows
from it: that John Wright killed the bird, and that his wife therefore had the
motive the County Attorney keeps saying he lacks — "something to show anger, or
sudden feeling… a thing that would connect up with this strange way of doing it."

**How Glaspell uses it.** She gives this knowledge to the two women, and through
them to the audience, at the play's midpoint — "Somebody — wrung — its — neck.
(Their eyes meet. A look of growing comprehension, of horror.)" — and withholds it
from the three investigating men until the curtain. From that moment the play runs
on a double track. Every line the men speak means one thing to them and another to
us:

- "Has the bird flown?" — asked idly, while the bird lies six feet away, dead.
- "I guess they're not very dangerous things the ladies have picked out," said as
  he actually disturbs the quilt pieces covering the box and steps back.
- "No, Mrs Peters doesn't need supervising. For that matter a sheriff's wife is
  married to the law" — said to a woman who has just decided to break it.
- "It's all perfectly clear except a reason for doing it" — said in the presence of
  the reason.
- And the closing exchange about quilting and knotting, in which the County
  Attorney asks, as a joke, the one question whose answer is the truth.

**The effect.** First, **suspense**: for the last third of the play the audience
watches three men move around a small kitchen containing a box they must not open,
and every casual gesture is frightening. Second, **complicity** — this is the
important one. Because we learned the secret with the women and at the same
moment, we want it kept, and we find ourselves hoping the officers of the law will
fail. Glaspell has made the audience part of the jury that acquits Minnie Wright,
and she has done it without a single speech of argument. Third, **satire**: the
irony exposes the men's confidence as ignorance, and turns their own catchword,
"trifles", back on them. And finally it produces the ending's peculiar quietness.
No one is arrested, no one confesses, nothing is explained — the play simply stops
on a pun, and the audience is left holding the knowledge.

**5.** *Trifles* is the strongest possible illustration of this principle, because
its two most important characters are never on stage at all. Minnie and John
Wright exist entirely in what other people say, remember and infer — and the play
demonstrates that such evidence can be more revealing than direct presence.

**John Wright** is the clearest case. No one attacks him. The Sheriff's wife
reports the common opinion neutrally: "They say he was a good man." Mrs Hale then
gives the fullest testimony in the play, and it begins as agreement: "Yes — good;
he didn't drink, and kept his word as well as most, I guess, and paid his debts."
Only then comes the turn: "But he was a hard man, Mrs Peters. Just to pass the
time of day with him — (shivers). Like a raw wind that gets to the bone." Two
further details arrive from an unlikely witness — Hale, who admires him — and are
all the more damaging for being accidental: Wright refused a telephone because
"folks talked too much anyway", and Hale had assumed "what his wife wanted made
much difference to John." A man's whole character is established by four
neighbours who think they are being fair to him.

**Minnie Wright** is built the same way, out of memory and objects. Hale's account
of the woman rocking and pleating her apron and saying "he died of a rope round
his neck" would, by itself, make her a monster. Mrs Hale supplies the other
half — the girl in a white dress with blue ribbons singing in the choir, "one of
the town girls", thirty years ago — and the credibility of the character lies
precisely in the gap between the two reports. Then the objects testify: the shabby
skirt, the log-cabin quilt, the jar of cherries, the apron she asks for in jail,
the bird in the silk. Because we never hear her defend herself, and because
everything we learn comes from people with no reason to lie, the portrait is far
more convincing than a confession would be.

The principle also works on the characters who *are* present. **Mrs Peters** tells
us she believes in the law; her husband tells us she is "married to the law"; the
County Attorney jokes that she needs no supervising. All three judgements are
wrong, and the play lets us watch them being wrong — which is how we measure her
change. **Mrs Hale** is called "loyal to your sex" by the County Attorney as a
mild sneer, and the rest of the play turns that sneer into a description of
heroism. And the **men** are characterised chiefly by what the women observe about
them: "I'd hate to have men coming into my kitchen, snooping around and
criticizing."

The lesson of the play, then, is also a lesson about reading character in general.
Credibility is cumulative. It is built out of many small, indirect, apparently
trivial testimonies — which is exactly what the title of the play is about.

**Reference to the context**

1. "MRS. PETERS: (glancing around). Seems funny to think of a bird here. But she
   must have had one, or why would she have a cage? I wonder what happened to it?
   MRS. HALE: I s'pose maybe the cat got it."
   (i) Who does "she" refer to? (ii) What does the word "one" stand for?
   (iii) What is the full form of "s'pose"? (iv) What do you mean when Mrs Hale
   says, "the cat got it"? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. "MRS. HALE: Wright was close. … she used to wear pretty clothes and be lively,
   when she was Minnie Foster, one of the town girls singing in the choir. But
   that — oh, that was thirty years ago."
   (i) Why does Mrs Hale refer to Mrs Wright as "Minnie Foster"? (ii) What does
   her description tell you about Mrs Wright? (iii) What does Mrs Hale mean by
   "that was thirty years ago"? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — reference to the context
**1.** (i) "She" refers to **Mrs Minnie Wright**, the woman arrested on suspicion
of murdering her husband, in whose kitchen the two women are standing. She never
appears on stage.
(ii) "One" stands for **a bird** — specifically a canary, of the kind a man had
been selling cheap in the district the previous year. Mrs Peters is reasoning from
the object to its owner: an empty cage implies a bird.
(iii) "S'pose" is a contraction of **"suppose"**, and the line in full would read
"I suppose maybe the cat got it." Glaspell writes her farming characters' speech
as it is actually spoken — "ain't it", "readied-up", "wouldn't they just laugh" —
so that the audience hears an ordinary rural kitchen rather than a stage.
(iv) It means that the household cat caught and killed the bird — a completely
ordinary explanation. Two things make the remark important. First, it is a guess
made in innocence: at this point Mrs Hale genuinely does not know what happened,
and Mrs Peters immediately corrects her, because Minnie "didn't have a cat" and
was in fact afraid of them. Second, and much more significantly, the same phrase
returns later as a **lie**. After the women have found the strangled canary and the
County Attorney asks, "Has the bird flown?", Mrs Hale answers, "We think the — cat
got it," while covering the box with quilt pieces. The innocent guess has become
the women's first act of concealment, and the audience, who know there is no cat,
hear the sentence as a deliberate deception.

**2.** (i) Because that was her name and her identity **before her marriage**, and
Mrs Hale is deliberately speaking of a different person from the woman now in
jail. Using the maiden name is how the play separates the girl who sang in the
choir from "Mrs Wright", the silent woman in the shabby skirt; the surname
"Wright" itself marks the moment the change began. It is also an act of sympathy:
Mrs Hale is the only person in the play who remembers her as anything but a
suspect, and by saying "Minnie Foster" she restores to her a self that the
investigation has no interest in.
(ii) It tells us, first, what Minnie once was: pretty, lively, sociable, dressed
well, and — crucially — a **singer**, standing up in the choir with the other town
girls. Second, by contrast, it tells us what she became. The sentence before it —
"Wright was close" — supplies the cause: a husband mean with money, so that she
could not dress decently, would not join the Ladies' Aid because "you don't enjoy
things when you feel shabby", and gradually "kept so much to herself." Third, it
prepares the play's central symbol. A woman who sang, shut in a silent house with
a caged songbird, makes the dead canary intelligible the moment it is found: "she
was kind of like a bird herself — real sweet and pretty, but kind of timid and —
fluttery."
(iii) It means that the Minnie Foster she is describing has been gone for a whole
generation — roughly the length of the Wrights' marriage — and that Mrs Hale is
reaching back past thirty years of a life she did not watch closely enough. The
phrase carries three things at once: the **measure of the change** ("How — she —
did — change"), the **slowness** of it, which is what makes it a tragedy rather
than an accident; and Mrs Hale's own **guilt**, since those are the thirty years
in which she did not call. The catch in "but that — oh, that was thirty years ago"
is the sound of a woman realising how long she has not been paying attention, and
it leads directly to her cry a few pages later: "I might have known she needed
help!"
:::
