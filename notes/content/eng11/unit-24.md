A **modal verb** is a helping verb that does not describe an action but colours it:
it says whether the action is possible, permitted, necessary, advisable or likely.
The nine pure modals are *can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should* and
*must*. NEB Q7 tests them in three ways — choose the right modal for a stated
function, rewrite a sentence with a modal of the same meaning (*It is necessary for
you to go* → *You must go*), or correct a sentence in which the modal is followed
by the wrong verb form.

::: key Four grammar rules that apply to every pure modal
1. A modal is followed by the **bare infinitive** — V1 with no *to*: *She **can
swim***, never ~~can to swim~~ or ~~can swims~~.
2. A modal **never takes -s** in the third person: *He **must** go*.
3. A modal makes its own question and negative with no *do*: *Can you come?*
   *He cannot come.* Never ~~Do you can come?~~
4. Two modals cannot stand together: ~~He will can do it~~ → *He **will be able
   to** do it.*
:::

## 24.1 The complete modal table

| Modal | Function | Example | Negative |
|---|---|---|---|
| can | present ability | She **can** speak four languages. | cannot / can't |
| can | informal permission | **Can** I borrow your notes? | can't (= not allowed) |
| can | general possibility | Landslides **can** block the Prithvi Highway. | can't (= impossible) |
| could | past ability | At five he **could** already read. | couldn't |
| could | polite request / suggestion | **Could** you pass the file? | couldn't |
| could | present possibility (weak) | He **could** be at the library. | couldn't (= impossible) |
| may | formal permission | **May** I come in, sir? | may not |
| may | possibility (about 50 %) | It **may** rain in the afternoon. | may not |
| may | wish, blessing | **May** you live long! | — |
| might | weaker possibility (about 30 %) | She **might** join us later. | might not |
| might | very polite request | **Might** I make a suggestion? | — |
| will | future, prediction, promise | I **will** return the book tomorrow. | will not / won't |
| will | willingness, request | **Will** you help me carry this? | won't (= refuses) |
| will | present habit / characteristic | Boys **will** be boys. | — |
| would | polite request / offer | **Would** you like some tea? | wouldn't |
| would | past habit | We **would** go to the river every Saturday. | wouldn't |
| would | unreal / conditional result | If I had money, I **would** buy it. | wouldn't |
| shall | offer or suggestion with I/we | **Shall** we start? | shan't |
| shall | formal obligation in rules | The tenant **shall** pay by the 5th. | shall not |
| should | advice, mild obligation | You **should** see a doctor. | should not |
| should | expectation | The parcel **should** arrive today. | shouldn't |
| must | strong obligation from the speaker | You **must** submit it by Friday. | must not (prohibition) |
| must | logical certainty | The lights are on — she **must** be at home. | can't (= certainly not) |

## 24.2 Ability: *can*, *could*, *be able to*

- **can** — present or general ability: *Nirmala **can** ride a motorbike.*
- **could** — general ability in the past: *My grandfather **could** walk from Ilam
  to Fikkal in three hours.*
- **was/were able to** — a single successful act in the past ("managed to").
  *The fire was spreading, but the neighbours **were able to** rescue the goats.*
  (*could* is wrong here, because it means general ability, not one success.)
- **be able to** supplies the forms a modal cannot make: *will be able to*,
  *have been able to*, *to be able to*.

::: caution Two modals in a row
English allows only one modal in a verb phrase.
~~She will can join us.~~ → **She will be able to join us.**
~~He must can finish it.~~ → **He must be able to finish it.**
~~You should must apologise.~~ → **You must apologise** / **You should apologise.**
:::

## 24.3 Permission, requests, offers and suggestions

| Function | Least formal → most formal | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Asking permission | can → could → may → might | **May** I leave early today, madam? |
| Giving permission | can, may | You **may** use the library after four. |
| Refusing permission | cannot, may not, must not | Visitors **may not** enter the ward. |
| Request | will → can → could → would | **Could** you lend me Rs 200? |
| Offer | shall, can, would like | **Shall** I carry your bag? |
| Suggestion | shall we, could, why don't we | **Shall we** visit Swayambhu on Saturday? |
| Invitation | would like | **Would you like** to join us for tea? |

Answering a request or permission question keeps the same modal where possible:
*"May I sit here?" — "Yes, you may."* / *"Could you help me?" — "Yes, of course."*
Do not answer *Could you…?* with *Yes, I could*; that sounds like a comment on your
ability, not an agreement.

## 24.4 Obligation, necessity and their negatives

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| must | obligation the **speaker** imposes; also an internal duty | You **must** wear a helmet. |
| have to / has to | obligation from **outside** — a rule, a law, circumstances | Passengers **have to** show a ticket. |
| had to | past obligation (there is no past of *must*) | We **had to** walk because of the strike. |
| will have to | future obligation | You **will have to** repeat the test. |
| should / ought to | advice, what is right | You **should** revise every day. |
| had better | strong advice, with a warning of a bad result | You **had better** leave now, or you will miss the bus. |
| need to | necessity | I **need to** renew my licence. |
| be supposed to | expected by a rule or arrangement | We **are supposed to** be there by nine. |

The negatives carry completely different meanings. This is the single most
examined point in the unit.

| Negative | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| must not / mustn't | **prohibition** — it is forbidden | You **mustn't** touch the wires. |
| do not have to / don't have to | **no obligation** — it is not necessary | It is Saturday, so you **don't have to** get up early. |
| need not / needn't | **no obligation** (same as *don't have to*) | You **needn't** bring your own lunch. |
| should not | advice against | You **shouldn't** eat so late. |
| cannot / can't | inability, or "certainly not true" | He **can't** be in Nepalgunj; I saw him an hour ago. |

::: caution *mustn't* is not the negative of *must*
The opposite of *You must go* is **You don't have to go** (it is not necessary),
not *You mustn't go* (you are forbidden to go). Q7 items often ask you to rewrite
*It is not necessary for you to come* — the answer is *You **needn't** come* or
*You **don't have to** come*, never ~~You mustn't come~~.
:::

::: caution *had better* has no *to*, and *ought* keeps its *to*
~~You had better to go now.~~ → **You had better go now.**
~~You ought go now.~~ → **You ought to go now.**
Also note *had better* refers to the present or future, not the past, in spite of
the word *had*.
:::

## 24.5 Degrees of certainty — deduction

When a modal expresses how sure the speaker is, the whole set forms a scale.

**The same scale in the past** puts *have* + V3 after the modal:

| Present deduction | Past deduction | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| He **must be** ill. | He **must have been** ill. | I am sure it was so. |
| She **may be** at home. | She **may have been** at home. | Perhaps it was so. |
| They **might know**. | They **might have known**. | Perhaps, less likely. |
| It **can't be** true. | It **can't have been** true. | I am sure it was not so. |

## 24.6 Past forms of modals and what they criticise

| Form | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| should have + V3 | the right thing was **not** done — regret or criticism | You **should have told** me earlier. |
| shouldn't have + V3 | a wrong thing **was** done | She **shouldn't have shouted** at the porter. |
| ought to have + V3 | same as *should have* | He **ought to have checked** the ticket. |
| could have + V3 | it was possible but did not happen | We **could have caught** the earlier bus. |
| needn't have + V3 | it was done, but it was unnecessary | You **needn't have paid**; entry was free. |
| didn't need to + V1 | it was unnecessary, so it was not done | I **didn't need to buy** a ticket, so I walked in. |
| would have + V3 | an unreal past result | I **would have come** if you had called. |
| must have + V3 | a confident conclusion about the past | The road **must have been** blocked. |

::: caution *needn't have done* versus *didn't need to do*
*You **needn't have waited*** — you did wait, and it turned out to be unnecessary.
*You **didn't need to wait*** — it was unnecessary, and (normally) you did not wait.
The two are not interchangeable, and NEB has set this contrast in Q7.
:::

## 24.7 Semi-modals

Semi-modals behave partly like modals in meaning but like ordinary verbs in
grammar: they change for tense and person, and they need *do* for questions and
negatives.

| Semi-modal | Meaning | Note |
|---|---|---|
| be able to | ability | supplies the missing forms of *can* |
| have to / have got to | outside obligation | changes tense: *had to, will have to* |
| need to | necessity | *Does he need to come?* |
| used to + V1 | a discontinued past habit or past state | *I **used to** cycle to school.* Question: *Did you use to…?* |
| be going to + V1 | intention, or prediction from evidence | *She **is going to** apply for the scholarship.* |
| be about to + V1 | on the point of | *The programme **is about to** begin.* |
| had better + V1 | strong advice | no *to*, no tense change |
| would rather + V1 | preference | *I **would rather** walk than wait.* |
| be supposed to | expected by rule or plan | *You **are supposed to** wear the uniform.* |
| dare / need | can be modal or ordinary verb | *He **dare** not speak* / *He **doesn't dare to** speak.* |

::: caution *used to* versus *be used to*
***used to** + V1* = a past habit that has stopped: *I **used to drink** milk tea.*
***be/get used to** + V-ing / noun* = be accustomed to: *I **am used to drinking**
black tea now.* The second one keeps the *-ing*, and it can be in any tense.
:::

::: tip Recognising the function the question wants
Q7 modal items almost always name the function in brackets or in the instruction:
*(obligation)*, *(possibility)*, *(prohibition)*, *(advice)*, *(past ability)*,
*(request)*. Match the label to the modal before you write anything:
obligation → *must/have to*; prohibition → *mustn't*; no obligation → *needn't*;
advice → *should/ought to/had better*; possibility → *may/might/could*;
certainty → *must*; impossibility → *can't*; past ability → *could*;
one past success → *was able to*.
:::

::: example Worked transformation 24.1 — a rewrite with the same meaning
**Problem.** Rewrite using a modal: *It is not necessary for you to attend the
Saturday class.*

**Solution — step by step.**

1. **Name the function.** "It is not necessary" = **absence of obligation**.
2. **Pick the modal for that function.** Absence of obligation is *needn't* or
   *don't have to*. It is **not** *mustn't*, which would mean the class is
   forbidden.
3. **Fit the subject and the bare infinitive.** *you* + *needn't* + *attend*.

**Answer.** *You **needn't attend** the Saturday class.*
(Also correct: *You **don't have to** attend the Saturday class.*)
:::

::: example Worked transformation 24.2 — a deduction about the past
**Problem.** Express as a deduction: *I am sure Anita did not take the bus, because
her bicycle is missing.*

**Solution — step by step.**

1. **Is the statement positive or negative?** Negative — "did not take".
2. **How sure is the speaker?** Completely sure. On the certainty scale, "certain
   it is NOT true" is **can't**.
3. **Is the event past?** Yes, so add **have + V3** after the modal:
   *can't have taken*.
4. **Assemble.**

**Answer.** *Anita **can't have taken** the bus, because her bicycle is missing.*

Writing *mustn't have taken* is wrong: *mustn't* means prohibition, never negative
deduction.
:::

::: example Worked transformation 24.3 — correcting a modal error
**Problem.** Correct: *When he was young he can run very fast, but now he will not
can run at all because he had better to rest his knee.*

**Solution — step by step.**

1. ***he can run* in a past sentence.** The time is past and the meaning is general
   ability → **could run**.
2. ***will not can run*.** Two modals cannot stand together. Replace the second with
   the semi-modal → **will not be able to run**.
3. ***had better to rest*.** *Had better* takes the bare infinitive → **had better
   rest**.

**Answer.** *When he was young he **could** run very fast, but now he **will not be
able to** run at all because he **had better rest** his knee.*
:::

## Chapter summary

- A modal takes the bare infinitive, never adds *-s*, needs no *do* for questions
  and negatives, and cannot be joined to another modal.
- Ability: *can* (now), *could* (past, general), *was/were able to* (one past
  success), *be able to* (all other forms).
- Permission rises in formality: *can → could → may → might*.
- Obligation: *must* (from the speaker), *have to* (from outside), *had to* (past),
  *should / ought to / had better* (advice).
- *mustn't* = forbidden; *needn't* and *don't have to* = not necessary. They are
  opposites, not synonyms.
- Certainty scale: *must* (sure it is) > *will* > *should* > *may* > *might/could*
  > *may not* > *can't* (sure it is not).
- For the past, add *have* + V3: *must have gone, might have gone, can't have gone*.
- *should have* + V3 criticises what was not done; *needn't have* + V3 says
  something done was unnecessary.
- Semi-modals — *be able to, have to, need to, used to, be going to, had better,
  would rather, be supposed to* — carry modal meanings but behave like ordinary
  verbs.
