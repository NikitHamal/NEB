A carboxylic acid contains the **carboxyl group**, —COOH: a carbonyl group and a
hydroxyl group on the same carbon. Neither group behaves as it would alone. The
—OH loses its proton far more readily than an alcohol does, and the >C=O refuses
the addition reactions of Unit 13. Both facts come from one cause — delocalisation
in the carboxylate ion. This unit then follows the acid into its four
derivatives: acid halides, anhydrides, esters and amides.

::: key What the examiner wants from this unit
Three questions dominate: (1) **why is a carboxylic acid more acidic than phenol
and alcohol**, explained with the resonance of RCOO⁻; (2) the **effect of
substituents** on acid strength, with $pK_a$ values; (3) a **comparison of the
four derivatives** — preparation from the acid, hydrolysis, ammonolysis,
alcoholysis, reduction, and the reactivity order RCOCl > (RCO)₂O > RCOOR′ >
RCONH₂. Add the four named reactions: HVZ, Claisen, Hofmann bromamide and
Schotten–Baumann.
:::

## 14.1 Introduction, nomenclature and isomerism

A **monocarboxylic acid** has one —COOH group; the saturated open-chain series is
CₙH₂ₙO₂, general formula R—COOH. Acids with two carboxyl groups are
**dicarboxylic**. When —COOH is attached directly to a benzene ring the acid is
**aromatic**.

::: definition Carboxylic acid
A carboxylic acid is an organic compound containing the carboxyl group, —COOH,
in which a hydroxyl group is attached to a carbonyl carbon. In water it ionises
to give a carboxylate ion and a hydrogen ion:
RCOOH + H₂O ⇌ RCOO⁻ + H₃O⁺.
:::

**IUPAC rules.** Replace the final *-e* of the parent alkane by *-oic acid*. The
carboxyl carbon is **always C-1**, so it never takes a locant. Two carboxyl
groups give *-dioic acid* (and the *-e* is kept). For the aromatic series the
parent name is *benzoic acid*.

| Formula | IUPAC name | Common name |
|---|---|---|
| HCOOH | methanoic acid | formic acid |
| CH₃COOH | ethanoic acid | acetic acid |
| CH₃CH₂COOH | propanoic acid | propionic acid |
| CH₃(CH₂)₂COOH | butanoic acid | butyric acid |
| (CH₃)₂CHCOOH | 2-methylpropanoic acid | isobutyric acid |
| ClCH₂COOH | 2-chloroethanoic acid | chloroacetic acid |
| HOOC—COOH | ethanedioic acid | oxalic acid |
| HOOC—CH₂—COOH | propanedioic acid | malonic acid |
| C₆H₅COOH | benzoic acid | benzoic acid |
| C₆H₅CH₂COOH | phenylethanoic acid | phenylacetic acid |
| o-HOC₆H₄COOH | 2-hydroxybenzoic acid | salicylic acid |

**Isomerism.** Carboxylic acids show:

1. **Chain isomerism** — butanoic acid and 2-methylpropanoic acid, both C₄H₈O₂.
2. **Position isomerism** of a substituent — 2-chlorobutanoic,
   3-chlorobutanoic and 4-chlorobutanoic acid. In aromatic acids the *ortho*,
   *meta* and *para* isomers, e.g. the three methylbenzoic (toluic) acids.
3. **Functional isomerism** with **esters** and with hydroxy-carbonyl compounds.
   C₃H₆O₂ is propanoic acid, methyl ethanoate or ethyl methanoate.
4. **Metamerism** among the ester isomers — methyl propanoate, ethyl ethanoate
   and propyl methanoate are all C₄H₈O₂.

C₄H₈O₂ therefore has **two** acids and **four** esters (methyl propanoate, ethyl
ethanoate, propyl methanoate, isopropyl methanoate).

## 14.2 Preparation of monocarboxylic acids

### From aldehydes — oxidation

Any oxidising agent that will attack an aldehyde works; acidified dichromate is
the standard laboratory reagent:

3CH₃CHO + K₂Cr₂O₇ + 4H₂SO₄ → 3CH₃COOH + K₂SO₄ + Cr₂(SO₄)₃ + 4H₂O

Primary alcohols give the same acid, passing through the aldehyde:
CH₃CH₂OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O.

### From nitriles — hydrolysis

A nitrile has the same number of carbons as the acid it gives. Hydrolysis is
done with dilute mineral acid or with alkali followed by acidification:

CH₃CN + 2H₂O + HCl → CH₃COOH + NH₄Cl

CH₃CN + H₂O + NaOH → CH₃COONa + NH₃↑

Because the nitrile itself is made from a haloalkane (R—X + KCN → R—CN + KX),
this is the standard way of **adding one carbon** to a chain.

### From dicarboxylic acids — decarboxylation

Dicarboxylic acids lose CO₂ on gentle heating:

HOOC—COOH --423 K--> HCOOH + CO₂↑

HOOC—CH₂—COOH --413 K--> CH₃COOH + CO₂↑

### From sodium alkoxide — carbonylation

Sodium alkoxide absorbs carbon monoxide under pressure to give the sodium salt
of the next higher acid, which is then acidified:

CH₃ONa + CO --425 K, 3 atm--> CH₃COONa

CH₃COONa + HCl → CH₃COOH + NaCl

### From trihaloalkanes — hydrolysis

Three halogens on the same carbon hydrolyse to an unstable orthoacid, which
loses two molecules of water:

CHCl₃ + 4NaOH → HCOONa + 3NaCl + 2H₂O

CH₃CCl₃ + 4NaOH → CH₃COONa + 3NaCl + 2H₂O

::: tip Counting carbons tells you which route to pick
Nitrile hydrolysis and the Grignard route (RMgX + CO₂ → RCOOMgX --H₃O⁺-->
RCOOH) **add** one carbon. Oxidation of an alcohol or aldehyde **keeps** the
carbon count. Decarboxylation of a diacid **removes** one carbon. In a
conversion question, count the carbons first and the route chooses itself.
:::

## 14.3 Preparation of benzoic acid from an alkylbenzene

Hot alkaline potassium permanganate attacks the **benzylic** carbon and degrades
the whole side chain to a single —COOH group, however long the side chain is —
provided it carries at least one hydrogen on the carbon joined to the ring:

C₆H₅CH₃ + 2KMnO₄ → C₆H₅COOK + 2MnO₂↓ + KOH + H₂O

C₆H₅COOK + HCl → C₆H₅COOH↓ + KCl

Ethylbenzene, propylbenzene and isopropylbenzene all give benzoic acid too.
*tert*-Butylbenzene, C₆H₅C(CH₃)₃, has no benzylic hydrogen and is **not**
oxidised — a favourite examiner's trap.

::: caution The ring survives, the side chain does not
Students often write the ring being broken open. KMnO₄ cannot oxidise the
aromatic ring under these conditions; it burns the side chain back to the ring
and stops. So C₆H₅CH₂CH₂CH₃ gives benzoic acid, **not** butanoic acid or
phenylpropanoic acid.
:::

## 14.4 Physical properties

The first three acids are pungent-smelling liquids miscible with water;
butanoic acid smells of rancid butter; from about C₁₀ they are waxy,
odourless solids. Pure ethanoic acid freezes at 16.6 °C to an ice-like solid, so
it is called **glacial** acetic acid — in a Kathmandu winter a bottle on the
shelf may set solid.

Carboxylic acids boil **higher than alcohols of similar molar mass** because they
form two hydrogen bonds between each pair of molecules, giving a cyclic **dimer**:

| Compound | M (g mol⁻¹) | Boiling point |
|---|---|---|
| Propane | 44 | −42 °C |
| Ethanal | 44 | 21 °C |
| Propan-1-ol | 60 | 97 °C |
| **Ethanoic acid** | 60 | **118 °C** |

Solubility in water falls steeply as the chain grows: methanoic to butanoic acid
are miscible, pentanoic is partly soluble, and the higher ones are insoluble but
dissolve in ethanol and ether. Aromatic acids are only slightly soluble in cold
water — benzoic acid crystallises out on cooling, which is how it is purified.

## 14.5 Chemical properties

### Acidic reactions

A carboxylic acid is a **weak acid**: $K_a$ of ethanoic acid is
$1.75\times10^{-5}$, so only about 1.3 % of the molecules are ionised in a
0.1 M solution. It is nevertheless a far stronger acid than an alcohol or a phenol,
and it neutralises all four of the following:

CH₃COOH + NaOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O   (with an **alkali**)

2CH₃COOH + CaO → (CH₃COO)₂Ca + H₂O   (with a **metal oxide**)

2CH₃COOH + Na₂CO₃ → 2CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑   (with a **carbonate**)

CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂↑   (with a **bicarbonate**)

2CH₃COOH + 2Na → 2CH₃COONa + H₂↑   (with an **active metal**)

::: tip The sodium bicarbonate test
Brisk effervescence of CO₂ with cold **NaHCO₃ solution** is the standard test for
a carboxylic acid. Phenol, which is also acidic, gives **no** effervescence
because it is too weak to displace carbonic acid. This single test separates an
acid from a phenol in every NEB "distinguish between" question.
:::

### Substitution of the —OH group

**With PCl₃, PCl₅ or SOCl₂** the hydroxyl group is replaced by chlorine, giving an
acid chloride:

3CH₃COOH + PCl₃ → 3CH₃COCl + H₃PO₃

CH₃COOH + PCl₅ → CH₃COCl + POCl₃ + HCl

CH₃COOH + SOCl₂ → CH₃COCl + SO₂↑ + HCl↑

Thionyl chloride is preferred because both by-products are gases and escape,
leaving pure acid chloride.

**With an alcohol** (esterification), conc. H₂SO₄ as catalyst and dehydrating
agent:

CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O

### Reduction with LiAlH₄

Lithium aluminium hydride in dry ether reduces the carboxyl group all the way to
a **primary alcohol**:

CH₃COOH + 4[H] --LiAlH₄, then H₂O--> CH₃CH₂OH + H₂O

::: caution NaBH₄ will not do this job
Sodium borohydride reduces aldehydes and ketones but is **too mild to reduce a
carboxylic acid**. Neither does H₂/Ni. Only LiAlH₄ (or diborane, B₂H₆) works. If
a question offers NaBH₄ on RCOOH, the answer is "no reaction".
:::

### Dehydration

Heating with P₂O₅ removes water from two molecules to give the anhydride:

2CH₃COOH --P₂O₅, Δ--> (CH₃CO)₂O + H₂O

### Decarboxylation

The dry sodium salt heated with soda-lime loses CO₂ as carbonate:

CH₃COONa + NaOH --CaO, Δ--> CH₄↑ + Na₂CO₃

This shortens the chain by one carbon and is the standard laboratory preparation
of methane.

## 14.6 Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ) reaction

An aliphatic acid with at least one **α-hydrogen** is halogenated at the α
carbon by chlorine or bromine in the presence of a small amount of **red
phosphorus**:

CH₃COOH + Cl₂ --red P--> ClCH₂COOH + HCl

ClCH₂COOH + Cl₂ --red P--> Cl₂CHCOOH + HCl

Cl₂CHCOOH + Cl₂ --red P--> Cl₃CCOOH + HCl

The phosphorus is not a catalyst in the ordinary sense: it reacts with the
halogen to form PCl₃, which converts a little of the acid into its acid chloride.
The acid chloride enolises far more readily than the acid does, and it is the
enol that the halogen attacks. The α-halo acid produced then exchanges with more
of the starting acid.

The α-halo acids are valuable intermediates: with aqueous KOH they give
α-hydroxy acids, with alcoholic KOH unsaturated acids, and with NH₃ they give
**α-amino acids** — this is a standard preparation of glycine from ethanoic acid.

::: caution HVZ needs an α-hydrogen
Methanoic acid, HCOOH, has **no** α-carbon at all, and benzoic acid has no α-H
either. Neither undergoes the HVZ reaction. Benzoic acid halogenates on the
**ring** instead (Section 14.7).
:::

## 14.7 Electrophilic substitution of benzoic acid

The —COOH group is strongly electron-withdrawing (−I and −R). It therefore
**deactivates** the ring towards electrophilic attack and directs the incoming
group to the **meta** position, because the *ortho* and *para* positions carry a
partial positive charge in the resonance structures.

| Reaction | Reagent and conditions | Product |
|---|---|---|
| Bromination | Br₂ / FeBr₃, warm | 3-bromobenzoic acid + HBr |
| Nitration | conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄, 373 K | 3-nitrobenzoic acid + H₂O |
| Sulphonation | fuming H₂SO₄ (oleum), Δ | 3-sulphobenzoic acid + H₂O |

C₆H₅COOH + Br₂ --FeBr₃--> m-BrC₆H₄COOH + HBr

C₆H₅COOH + HNO₃ --conc. H₂SO₄, 373 K--> m-O₂NC₆H₄COOH + H₂O

C₆H₅COOH + H₂SO₄ (fuming) → m-HO₃SC₆H₄COOH + H₂O

All three need harsher conditions than benzene itself — higher temperature,
stronger catalyst — which is exactly what "deactivated ring" means. Benzoic acid
does **not** undergo Friedel–Crafts alkylation or acylation at all, because the
AlCl₃ complexes with the carboxyl group and the ring is too deactivated.

## 14.8 Effect of substituents on acid strength; methanoic acid

### Why a carboxylic acid is acidic at all

Removing the proton gives the **carboxylate ion**, RCOO⁻, in which the negative
charge is shared *equally* between the two oxygen atoms. Both carbon–oxygen bonds
in ethanoate are the same length, 127 pm — between a C=O (122 pm) and a C—O
(143 pm). This delocalisation lowers the energy of the anion a great deal, so the
ionisation equilibrium lies much further to the right than it does for an
alcohol, whose anion RO⁻ has the charge stuck on one atom.

The same argument, applied three times, gives the order that NEB asks for every
year:

**RCOOH > H₂CO₃ > C₆H₅OH > H₂O > ROH**

Ethanoic acid ($pK_a$ 4.76) beats phenol ($pK_a$ 10.0) because in phenoxide the
charge is spread over carbon atoms, which are less electronegative than oxygen,
and only two of the contributing structures carry it. Ethanol ($pK_a$ 16) has no
delocalisation at all.

### Effect of substituents

An **electron-withdrawing** group (−I) pulls charge away from the carboxylate
ion, spreads it further and stabilises it, so the acid is **stronger**. An
**electron-releasing** group (+I) pushes charge onto the ion, destabilises it,
and makes the acid **weaker**. The effect dies away rapidly with distance — it is
roughly one-third as strong at each successive carbon.

Read off the trends:

- **Number of substituents:** CH₃COOH 4.76 → ClCH₂COOH 2.87 → Cl₂CHCOOH 1.29 →
  Cl₃CCOOH 0.66. Trichloroethanoic acid is about 12 000 times stronger than
  ethanoic acid.
- **Electronegativity of the halogen:** F > Cl > Br > I (2.59 < 2.87 < 2.90 <
  3.18), exactly the order of −I strength.
- **Distance:** 2-chlorobutanoic acid ($pK_a$ 2.86) is far stronger than
  4-chlorobutanoic acid ($pK_a$ 4.52), which is barely stronger than butanoic
  acid itself (4.82).
- **Alkyl groups weaken:** HCOOH 3.75 < CH₃COOH 4.76 < CH₃CH₂COOH 4.87 <
  (CH₃)₃CCOOH 5.05.

For aromatic acids, benzoic acid ($pK_a$ 4.20) is stronger than ethanoic acid
because the sp² ring carbon is more electronegative than an sp³ alkyl carbon.
Electron-withdrawing ring substituents (—NO₂, —Cl) strengthen it further, and
electron-releasing ones (—CH₃, —OCH₃) weaken it.

### Abnormal behaviour of methanoic acid

Methanoic acid, H—COOH, is the only carboxylic acid whose carboxyl group is
attached to hydrogen and not to carbon. It therefore contains a complete
**aldehydic (—CHO) grouping**, and behaves partly as an aldehyde:

1. It is the **strongest** of the simple aliphatic acids ($pK_a$ 3.75) because
   there is no alkyl group to push electrons in.
2. It is a **reducing agent**. It gives a silver mirror with Tollens' reagent and
   a red precipitate with Fehling's solution, which no other carboxylic acid
   does:

   HCOOH + 2[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 2OH⁻ → 2Ag↓ + CO₂↑ + 4NH₃ + 2H₂O

   HCOOH + 2CuO → Cu₂O↓ + CO₂↑ + H₂O

3. It decolourises acidified KMnO₄ and reduces mercury(II) chloride to
   mercury(I) chloride.
4. It **cannot form an anhydride**. With concentrated sulphuric acid it simply
   dehydrates to carbon monoxide: HCOOH --conc. H₂SO₄, Δ--> CO↑ + H₂O. This is
   the laboratory preparation of CO.
5. It has **no α-hydrogen**, so it gives no HVZ reaction.
6. Heating its sodium salt gives sodium oxalate and hydrogen, not a ketone.

## 14.9 The four derivatives and their preparation from the acid

Replacing the —OH of —COOH by —Cl, —OCOR, —OR′ or —NH₂ gives the four
derivatives. All of them can be hydrolysed back to the parent acid, which is why
they are called *derivatives* of it.

| Derivative | General formula | Example | Preparation from CH₃COOH |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acid chloride | RCOCl | ethanoyl chloride, CH₃COCl | CH₃COOH + SOCl₂ → CH₃COCl + SO₂ + HCl |
| Anhydride | (RCO)₂O | ethanoic anhydride, (CH₃CO)₂O | 2CH₃COOH --P₂O₅, Δ--> (CH₃CO)₂O + H₂O |
| Ester | RCOOR′ | ethyl ethanoate, CH₃COOC₂H₅ | CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O (conc. H₂SO₄) |
| Amide | RCONH₂ | ethanamide, CH₃CONH₂ | CH₃COOH + NH₃ → CH₃COONH₄ --Δ--> CH₃CONH₂ + H₂O |

The anhydride can also be made more cleanly from the acid chloride and the
sodium salt:

CH₃COCl + CH₃COONa → (CH₃CO)₂O + NaCl

::: memory Naming the derivatives
Drop *-ic acid* and add: *-yl chloride* (ethanoyl chloride), *-ic anhydride*
(ethanoic anhydride), *-amide* (ethanamide). For an ester, name the alkyl group
of the alcohol first, then the acid part as *-oate*: C₂H₅ + CH₃COO— = **ethyl
ethanoate**.
:::

## 14.10 Comparative physical and chemical properties

### Physical properties

| Property | CH₃COCl | (CH₃CO)₂O | CH₃COOC₂H₅ | CH₃CONH₂ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| M (g mol⁻¹) | 78.5 | 102 | 88 | 59 |
| State at 25 °C | liquid | liquid | liquid | white solid |
| Boiling point | 51 °C | 140 °C | 77 °C | 221 °C (m.p. 82 °C) |
| Smell | sharp, irritating | sharp | pleasant, fruity | almost none |
| Action of water | fumes and hydrolyses at once | slow at 25 °C, fast on warming | very slow without a catalyst | needs boiling acid or alkali |

Amides are much the highest boiling because the —NH₂ group forms **intermolecular
hydrogen bonds**; the other three have no N—H or O—H at all and rely on
dipole–dipole attraction.

### The common mechanism: nucleophilic acyl substitution

All four derivatives react with nucleophiles by the same two-step path —
**addition then elimination**. This is why the C=O of a derivative gives
substitution while the C=O of an aldehyde gives addition: the aldehyde has no
group that can leave as an anion.

### Hydrolysis

| Derivative | Reagent | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Acid chloride | cold water, violent | CH₃COCl + H₂O → CH₃COOH + HCl |
| Anhydride | warm water | (CH₃CO)₂O + H₂O → 2CH₃COOH |
| Ester | dilute acid (reversible) | CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH |
| Ester | alkali (irreversible) | CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + C₂H₅OH |
| Amide | boiling dilute acid | CH₃CONH₂ + H₂O + HCl → CH₃COOH + NH₄Cl |
| Amide | boiling alkali | CH₃CONH₂ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + NH₃↑ |

Alkaline hydrolysis of an ester is called **saponification** — it is the reaction
that makes soap from a triglyceride.

### Ammonolysis and reaction with amines

CH₃COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + NH₄Cl

(CH₃CO)₂O + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + CH₃COONH₄

CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + C₂H₅OH

With a primary amine the product is an N-substituted amide:

CH₃COCl + C₂H₅NH₂ → CH₃CONHC₂H₅ + HCl

C₆H₅NH₂ + (CH₃CO)₂O → C₆H₅NHCOCH₃ + CH₃COOH   (acetanilide)

Acylation of an amine or a phenol with an acid chloride in the presence of
aqueous NaOH is the **Schotten–Baumann reaction**; the alkali mops up the HCl.

### Alcoholysis

CH₃COCl + C₂H₅OH → CH₃COOC₂H₅ + HCl

(CH₃CO)₂O + C₂H₅OH → CH₃COOC₂H₅ + CH₃COOH

Both give esters in one step and, unlike direct esterification, neither is an
equilibrium — which is why acid chlorides are used when a high yield matters.

### Reduction

| Derivative | Reagent | Product |
|---|---|---|
| CH₃COCl | LiAlH₄ (4[H]) | CH₃CH₂OH + HCl |
| CH₃COCl | H₂, Pd/BaSO₄ poisoned (**Rosenmund**) | CH₃CHO + HCl |
| (CH₃CO)₂O | LiAlH₄ (8[H]) | 2CH₃CH₂OH + H₂O |
| CH₃COOC₂H₅ | LiAlH₄ (4[H]) | CH₃CH₂OH + C₂H₅OH |
| CH₃CONH₂ | LiAlH₄ (4[H]) | CH₃CH₂NH₂ + H₂O |

Note the odd one out: an **amide is reduced to an amine**, not to an alcohol,
because nitrogen is a much poorer leaving group than oxygen.

## 14.11 Claisen condensation, Hofmann bromamide, amides and reactivity

### Claisen condensation

An ester with an α-hydrogen, treated with its own sodium alkoxide, condenses with
a second molecule of ester to give a **β-keto ester**:

2CH₃COOC₂H₅ --C₂H₅ONa, then H⁺--> CH₃COCH₂COOC₂H₅ + C₂H₅OH

The product, ethyl 3-oxobutanoate (**ethyl acetoacetate**), is a key synthetic
intermediate. The mechanism parallels the aldol condensation of Unit 13 — the
alkoxide removes an α-hydrogen to give an ester enolate, which attacks the
carbonyl carbon of the second ester — but here the tetrahedral intermediate
expels ethoxide, so the result is substitution, not addition. Esters with no
α-hydrogen, such as ethyl benzoate, cannot undergo it.

### Hofmann bromamide reaction

An amide treated with bromine in excess alkali loses its carbonyl carbon as
carbonate and gives a **primary amine with one carbon fewer**:

CH₃CONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → CH₃NH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O

C₆H₅CONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → C₆H₅NH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O

Check the first: C 1 + 1 = 2 ✓, H 5 + 4 = 5 + 4 ✓, N 1 ✓, O 1 + 4 = 3 + 2 ✓,
Na 4 = 2 + 2 ✓, Br 2 ✓. It is a **descending** reaction, the counterpart of the
ascending nitrile route of Section 14.2.

### Amphoteric nature of amides

An amide is almost neutral to litmus, yet it reacts with both acids and bases:

**As a base** — the nitrogen lone pair accepts a proton from a strong acid:

CH₃CONH₂ + HCl → CH₃CONH₂·HCl

**As an acid** — the N—H hydrogen can be removed by sodium or mercury(II) oxide:

2CH₃CONH₂ + 2Na → 2CH₃CONHNa + H₂↑

2CH₃CONH₂ + HgO → (CH₃CONH)₂Hg + H₂O

The reason for both is the same delocalisation: the nitrogen lone pair is pulled
into the carbonyl group, which makes the nitrogen a much weaker base than an
amine, and at the same time makes the N—H bond polar enough to be attacked by a
strong base.

### Relative reactivity of the derivatives

**RCOCl > (RCO)₂O > RCOOR′ > RCOOH > RCONH₂**

Two effects explain the order, and they reinforce each other:

- **Leaving-group ability.** Cl⁻ is the conjugate base of a strong acid and so a
  very weak base — it leaves easily. NH₂⁻ is the conjugate base of ammonia, a
  very strong base — it will not leave.
- **Resonance donation into the carbonyl group.** The lone pair on the attached
  atom feeds into the C=O and reduces the δ+ charge. Nitrogen (2p–2p overlap,
  low electronegativity) donates best, oxygen less, chlorine (3p–2p, poor
  overlap, high electronegativity) hardly at all.

This order also means a **more reactive derivative can be converted into a less
reactive one, but not the reverse**: an acid chloride gives an anhydride, an
ester or an amide directly, but an amide cannot be turned into an acid chloride
in one step.

::: example Worked example 14.1 — gas volume from the bicarbonate test
**Problem.** 6.00 g of ethanoic acid is added to excess sodium hydrogencarbonate
solution. What volume of carbon dioxide, measured at STP, is evolved?
(C = 12, H = 1, O = 16; molar volume at STP = 22.4 dm³ mol⁻¹.)

**Solution.** M(CH₃COOH) = 2(12) + 4(1) + 2(16) = 60 g mol⁻¹.

$$ n(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}) = \frac{6.00}{60} = 0.100\ \text{mol} $$

From CH₃COOH + NaHCO₃ → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂ the ratio is 1 : 1, so
$n(\text{CO}_2) = 0.100$ mol.

$$ V = 0.100 \times 22.4 = 2.24\ \text{dm}^{3} $$

**Answer: 2.24 dm³ (2240 cm³) of CO₂ at STP.**
:::

::: example Worked example 14.2 — finding an acid by titration
**Problem.** 0.150 g of a pure monocarboxylic acid needs exactly 25.0 cm³ of
0.100 M sodium hydroxide for complete neutralisation. Find its molar mass and
name the acid.

**Solution.** Moles of NaOH used:

$$ n(\text{NaOH}) = 0.100 \times \frac{25.0}{1000} = 2.50\times10^{-3}\ \text{mol} $$

A monocarboxylic acid is monobasic, so RCOOH + NaOH → RCOONa + H₂O reacts 1 : 1
and $n(\text{acid}) = 2.50\times10^{-3}$ mol.

$$ M = \frac{0.150}{2.50\times10^{-3}} = 60.0\ \text{g mol}^{-1} $$

CₙH₂ₙO₂ with M = 60 gives 14n + 32 = 60, so n = 2. The acid is **ethanoic acid,
CH₃COOH**.
:::

::: example Worked example 14.3 — percentage yield in esterification
**Problem.** 12.0 g of ethanoic acid is refluxed with excess ethanol and a little
concentrated sulphuric acid. 12.3 g of ethyl ethanoate is obtained. Calculate the
percentage yield.

**Solution.** $n(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}) = 12.0/60 = 0.200$ mol. The acid is the
limiting reagent because the alcohol is in excess, and

CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O

is 1 : 1, so the theoretical yield is 0.200 mol of ester.
M(CH₃COOC₂H₅) = 4(12) + 8(1) + 2(16) = 88 g mol⁻¹.

$$ m_{\text{theoretical}} = 0.200 \times 88 = 17.6\ \text{g} $$

$$ \text{percentage yield} = \frac{12.3}{17.6}\times 100 = 69.9\ \% $$

**Answer: about 70 %.** The yield is limited because esterification is an
equilibrium; removing the ester by distillation as it forms pushes it higher.
:::

::: example Worked example 14.4 — comparing acid strengths numerically
**Problem.** At 298 K, $K_a$(CH₃COOH) $= 1.75\times10^{-5}$ and
$K_a$(ClCH₂COOH) $= 1.36\times10^{-3}$. Calculate both $pK_a$ values, state how
many times stronger chloroethanoic acid is, and explain the difference.

**Solution.**

$$ pK_a = -\log_{10}K_a $$

For ethanoic acid: $pK_a = -\log(1.75\times10^{-5}) = 5 - 0.243 = 4.76$.

For chloroethanoic acid: $pK_a = -\log(1.36\times10^{-3}) = 3 - 0.134 = 2.87$.

$$ \frac{K_a(\text{ClCH}_2\text{COOH})}{K_a(\text{CH}_3\text{COOH})}
= \frac{1.36\times10^{-3}}{1.75\times10^{-5}} = 77.7 $$

So chloroethanoic acid is about **78 times stronger**.

**Reason.** Chlorine is strongly electronegative and exerts a −I effect. It
withdraws electron density along the C—C bond, spreading the negative charge of
ClCH₂COO⁻ over a larger volume and stabilising it. The more stable the anion,
the further the ionisation equilibrium lies to the right, so the acid is
stronger. In CH₃COO⁻ the methyl group does the opposite (+I).
:::

::: example Worked example 14.5 — a derivative puzzle
**Problem.** A compound **P**, C₃H₆O₂, gives brisk effervescence with NaHCO₃.
With SOCl₂ it gives **Q**, and **Q** with excess ammonia gives **R**, C₃H₇NO.
**R** with bromine and excess NaOH gives **S**, a basic compound of formula
C₂H₇N. Identify P, Q, R and S and write the equations.

**Solution.** Effervescence with NaHCO₃ proves a —COOH group, and C₃H₆O₂ with
one —COOH is **propanoic acid, CH₃CH₂COOH = P**.

CH₃CH₂COOH + SOCl₂ → CH₃CH₂COCl + SO₂ + HCl, so **Q = propanoyl chloride**.

CH₃CH₂COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂CONH₂ + NH₄Cl. Ethanamide would be C₂H₅NO; here
C₃H₇NO fits **propanamide, CH₃CH₂CONH₂ = R** ✓.

The last step is the Hofmann bromamide reaction, which removes one carbon:

CH₃CH₂CONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O

so **S = ethylamine, C₂H₅NH₂ = C₂H₇N** ✓, which is basic as required.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Carboxylic acids are R—COOH, CₙH₂ₙO₂; the carboxyl carbon is always C-1. They
  are functional isomers of esters (C₃H₆O₂ = propanoic acid, methyl ethanoate,
  ethyl methanoate).
- Preparations: oxidation of a 1° alcohol or aldehyde, hydrolysis of a nitrile
  (+1 C), decarboxylation of a diacid (−1 C), carbonylation of a sodium alkoxide,
  hydrolysis of a trihaloalkane; benzoic acid from any alkylbenzene with a
  benzylic H by hot alkaline KMnO₄.
- They form hydrogen-bonded **dimers**, so they boil above alcohols of the same
  molar mass (ethanoic acid 118 °C against propan-1-ol 97 °C).
- Acidity: RCOOH > H₂CO₃ > C₆H₅OH > H₂O > ROH, because RCOO⁻ is resonance
  stabilised with both C—O bonds 127 pm. Effervescence with NaHCO₃ distinguishes
  an acid from a phenol.
- −I substituents strengthen the acid ($pK_a$: CH₃COOH 4.76, ClCH₂COOH 2.87,
  Cl₂CHCOOH 1.29, Cl₃CCOOH 0.66); +I groups weaken it; the effect falls off
  sharply with distance.
- Methanoic acid is abnormal: strongest simple acid, reduces Tollens' and
  Fehling's, gives CO with conc. H₂SO₄, gives no anhydride and no HVZ reaction.
- HVZ (Cl₂/red P) halogenates the α-carbon; —COOH is *meta*-directing and
  deactivating on a benzene ring.
- Derivatives RCOCl, (RCO)₂O, RCOOR′, RCONH₂ all react by **nucleophilic acyl
  substitution** (addition then elimination) and all hydrolyse back to the acid.
  Reactivity RCOCl > (RCO)₂O > RCOOR′ > RCOOH > RCONH₂.
- Named reactions: Claisen (2 esters → β-keto ester), Hofmann bromamide
  (amide → 1° amine, one C fewer), Schotten–Baumann (acylation in aq. NaOH).
  Amides are amphoteric and are reduced by LiAlH₄ to **amines**.

4. 3.00 g of ethanoic acid is treated with excess sodium carbonate solution.

5. Write the reactions of ethanoyl chloride with (a) water, (b) ethanol,
   (c) ammonia, (d) aniline and (e) H₂ over poisoned Pd, naming each product.

::: note Answers to Group B
**2.** (CH₃)₃CCOOH (5.05) < CH₃COOH (4.76) < HCOOH (3.75) < ClCH₂COOH (2.87) <
Cl₃CCOOH (0.66) in order of *increasing* strength. Alkyl groups are +I and
destabilise the anion; H has no inductive effect; chlorine is −I and stabilises
it, and three chlorines do so three times over.

n(CO₂) = 0.0250 mol and V = 0.0250 × 22.4 = **0.560 dm³**. The acid : salt ratio
is 1 : 1, so n(CH₃COONa) = 0.0500 mol; M(CH₃COONa) = 82 g mol⁻¹, giving
m = 0.0500 × 82 = **4.10 g**.

(b) CH₃COCl + C₂H₅OH → CH₃COOC₂H₅ + HCl (ethyl ethanoate).
(c) CH₃COCl + 2NH₃ → CH₃CONH₂ + NH₄Cl (ethanamide).
(d) CH₃COCl + C₆H₅NH₂ → C₆H₅NHCOCH₃ + HCl (acetanilide).
(e) CH₃COCl + H₂ --Pd/BaSO₄, S--> CH₃CHO + HCl (ethanal, Rosenmund reduction).

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. (a) Define a carboxylic acid and explain its acidic character using the
   resonance of the carboxylate ion. <span class="marks">[3]</span>
   (b) Discuss the effect of electron-withdrawing and electron-releasing
   substituents on acid strength, using $pK_a$ data. <span class="marks">[3]</span>
   (c) Why is benzoic acid a stronger acid than ethanoic acid?
   <span class="marks">[2]</span>
2. (a) Name the four derivatives of ethanoic acid and give one equation for the
   preparation of each **from ethanoic acid**. <span class="marks">[4]</span>
   (b) Compare their reactivity towards hydrolysis, explain the order, and write
   the equation for the alkaline hydrolysis of ethyl ethanoate and of ethanamide.
   <span class="marks">[4]</span>
3. An organic compound **A** of formula C₇H₈ is oxidised by hot alkaline KMnO₄ and
   then acidified to give **B**, C₇H₆O₂, which dissolves in NaHCO₃ with
   effervescence. **B** with SOCl₂ gives **C**; **C** with ammonia gives **D**,
   C₇H₇NO; **D** with Br₂ and excess NaOH gives **E**, C₆H₇N, which gives an
   orange dye when coupled after diazotisation. Identify A to E and write all the
   equations. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Answers to Group C
**1.** (c) The carbon of the benzene ring is sp² hybridised and therefore more
electronegative than the sp³ carbon of a methyl group. It withdraws electron
density from the carboxyl group (−I) instead of releasing it, so benzoate is
better stabilised than ethanoate: $pK_a$ 4.20 against 4.76.

**2.** (b) RCOCl > (RCO)₂O > RCOOR′ > RCONH₂, because the weaker the base L⁻ the
faster it leaves, and the less the atom L donates its lone pair into the C=O.
CH₃COOC₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + C₂H₅OH (saponification);
CH₃CONH₂ + NaOH → CH₃COONa + NH₃.

**3.** C₇H₈ with a benzene ring and a side chain is **toluene, C₆H₅CH₃ = A**.

C₆H₅CH₃ + 2KMnO₄ → C₆H₅COOK + 2MnO₂ + KOH + H₂O; then
C₆H₅COOK + HCl → C₆H₅COOH + KCl, so **B = benzoic acid, C₇H₆O₂** ✓ (it
effervesces with NaHCO₃, confirming —COOH).

C₆H₅COOH + SOCl₂ → C₆H₅COCl + SO₂ + HCl, so **C = benzoyl chloride**.

C₆H₅COCl + 2NH₃ → C₆H₅CONH₂ + NH₄Cl, so **D = benzamide, C₇H₇NO** ✓.

C₆H₅CONH₂ + Br₂ + 4NaOH → C₆H₅NH₂ + Na₂CO₃ + 2NaBr + 2H₂O, so
**E = aniline, C₆H₇N** ✓ — and aniline is diazotised and coupled to give azo
dyes, as required.
:::
