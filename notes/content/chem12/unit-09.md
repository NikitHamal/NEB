A haloarene is what you get when a halogen atom is bonded **directly to a carbon of a benzene ring**. Chlorobenzene, C₆H₅Cl, is the parent member. On paper it looks like a close cousin of chloroethane, but its chemistry is completely different: it refuses to react with aqueous alkali, silver nitrate gives no precipitate with it, and it will not form a Grignard reagent under ordinary conditions. That single contrast — *why is the C–Cl bond of chlorobenzene so unreactive?* — is the heart of this short unit, and it is asked in almost every board paper.

::: key What the exam wants from this unit
Three things, in order of how often they appear: (1) the **resonance explanation** of the short, strong, low-polarity C–X bond and the resulting low reactivity towards nucleophiles; (2) the **named reactions** — Sandmeyer/Gattermann, Fittig, Wurtz–Fittig, and chloral → DDT, with correct reagents and conditions; (3) **electrophilic substitution**, where –Cl is *deactivating but ortho–para directing*. Learn to write every equation balanced.
:::

## 9.1 Introduction; nomenclature and isomerism

Organic halogen compounds that contain a benzene ring fall into two families, and telling them apart is the first thing you must do in any question.

| Family | Halogen sits on | Example | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Haloarene** (aryl halide) | a **ring** carbon (sp²) | chlorobenzene, C₆H₅Cl | very unreactive to nucleophiles |
| **Aralkyl halide** (side-chain halide) | a carbon of the **side chain** (sp³) | benzyl chloride, C₆H₅CH₂Cl | reacts like a haloalkane — very reactive |

::: caution Benzyl chloride is not a haloarene
C₆H₅CH₂Cl contains a benzene ring, but the chlorine is on the side chain, so it is an *aralkyl* halide. It gives an instant white precipitate with alcoholic AgNO₃ and is hydrolysed by warm water; chlorobenzene does neither. Examiners set this pair deliberately.
:::

**Naming.** Number the ring so that the substituents get the lowest possible locants, and cite the halogen as a prefix (chloro–, bromo–, iodo–, fluoro–). For disubstituted rings the old prefixes *ortho* (1,2), *meta* (1,3) and *para* (1,4) are still used and are accepted by NEB.

| Structure | IUPAC name | Common name |
|---|---|---|
| C₆H₅Cl | chlorobenzene | phenyl chloride |
| C₆H₅Br | bromobenzene | phenyl bromide |
| 1,2-C₆H₄Cl₂ | 1,2-dichlorobenzene | *o*-dichlorobenzene |
| 1,4-C₆H₄Cl₂ | 1,4-dichlorobenzene | *p*-dichlorobenzene |
| 4-CH₃–C₆H₄–Cl | 1-chloro-4-methylbenzene | *p*-chlorotoluene |
| 4-O₂N–C₆H₄–Cl | 1-chloro-4-nitrobenzene | *p*-nitrochlorobenzene |
| C₆H₅CH₂Cl | (chloromethyl)benzene | benzyl chloride |

**Isomerism.** A monohaloarene such as C₆H₅Cl has no positional isomer — all six ring carbons are equivalent. Isomerism starts with the second substituent. C₆H₄Cl₂ has exactly **three** positional isomers, and their melting points are a classic data question.

| Isomer | m.p. / °C | b.p. / °C | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,2- (*ortho*) | −17 | 180 | low symmetry, poor packing |
| 1,3- (*meta*) | −25 | 173 | lowest symmetry, packs worst |
| 1,4- (*para*) | **+53** | 174 | symmetrical, packs tightly in the crystal |

The *para* isomer melts far higher than the other two because its symmetrical, almost linear shape lets the molecules stack neatly in the solid lattice, so more energy is needed to break the lattice. Boiling points, which depend on molecular attraction in the *liquid*, are almost the same for all three.

Note also that C₆H₄ClBr gives three isomers, while a trisubstituted ring C₆H₃Cl₃ gives three (1,2,3-, 1,2,4- and 1,3,5-).

## 9.2 Preparation of chlorobenzene from benzene and from benzene diazonium chloride

### From benzene — direct halogenation

Benzene reacts with chlorine in the **dark**, in the presence of a Lewis-acid halogen carrier (anhydrous AlCl₃, FeCl₃ or iron filings), to give chlorobenzene:

- **C₆H₆ + Cl₂ --(anhyd. AlCl₃, dark)--> C₆H₅Cl + HCl**

The catalyst works by generating the electrophile: AlCl₃ + Cl₂ → AlCl₄⁻ + Cl⁺. Sunlight must be excluded, because in **UV light and without a catalyst** chlorine adds to the ring instead of substituting, giving benzene hexachloride (BHC, gammexane):

- **C₆H₆ + 3Cl₂ --(UV light)--> C₆H₆Cl₆**

On an industrial scale chlorobenzene is made by the **Raschig process**, in which benzene vapour, hydrogen chloride and air are passed over a copper(II) chloride catalyst at about 500 K:

- **2C₆H₆ + 2HCl + O₂ --(CuCl₂, 500 K)--> 2C₆H₅Cl + 2H₂O**

### From benzene diazonium chloride

Direct halogenation cannot be stopped cleanly at one substitution and cannot put a halogen at a chosen position. The diazonium route can. Aniline is diazotised at 273–278 K:

- **C₆H₅NH₂ + NaNO₂ + 2HCl --(273–278 K)--> C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + NaCl + 2H₂O**

The diazonium salt is then decomposed. Two named methods are examinable:

| Name | Reagent | Equation |
|---|---|---|
| **Sandmeyer reaction** | CuCl dissolved in conc. HCl, warm | C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ --(CuCl/HCl)--> C₆H₅Cl + N₂↑ |
| **Gattermann reaction** | freshly prepared Cu powder + conc. HCl | C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ --(Cu/HCl)--> C₆H₅Cl + N₂↑ |

Both give bromobenzene with CuBr/HBr. Iodobenzene needs no catalyst at all — warming the diazonium solution with KI is enough:

- **C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + KI → C₆H₅I + KCl + N₂↑**

Fluorobenzene is made by a different route, the **Balz–Schiemann reaction**: the diazonium fluoroborate is precipitated with HBF₄ and then heated dry.

- **C₆H₅N₂⁺BF₄⁻ --Δ--> C₆H₅F + N₂↑ + BF₃**

::: example Chlorobenzene from benzene
**Problem.** 39.0 g of benzene is treated with excess chlorine over anhydrous AlCl₃ in the dark. If the yield of chlorobenzene is 80 %, what mass is obtained?

**Solution.**
Molar mass of C₆H₆ = (6 × 12) + (6 × 1) = 78 g mol⁻¹.
Moles of benzene = 39.0 / 78 = 0.500 mol.

The equation C₆H₆ + Cl₂ → C₆H₅Cl + HCl is 1 : 1, so theoretical moles of chlorobenzene = 0.500 mol.

Molar mass of C₆H₅Cl = (6 × 12) + (5 × 1) + 35.5 = 112.5 g mol⁻¹.
Theoretical mass = 0.500 × 112.5 = 56.25 g.

Actual mass = 80 % of 56.25 g = 0.80 × 56.25 = **45.0 g**.
:::

::: example The diazonium route, with the gas evolved
**Problem.** 9.30 g of aniline is diazotised completely and the diazonium salt is decomposed by the Sandmeyer reaction. Calculate (i) the volume of N₂ liberated at STP and (ii) the mass of chlorobenzene formed if the Sandmeyer step is 75 % efficient.

**Solution.**
Molar mass of C₆H₅NH₂ = (6 × 12) + (5 × 1) + 14 + (2 × 1) = 93 g mol⁻¹.
Moles of aniline = 9.30 / 93 = 0.100 mol.

Each mole of aniline gives one mole of C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻, which gives one mole of N₂ on decomposition.

(i) Moles of N₂ = 0.100 mol. At STP, 1 mol occupies 22.4 L, so
volume = 0.100 × 22.4 = **2.24 L**.

(ii) Theoretical chlorobenzene = 0.100 × 112.5 = 11.25 g.
At 75 % efficiency, mass = 0.75 × 11.25 = **8.44 g** (3 s.f.).
:::

## 9.3 Physical properties

| Property | Chlorobenzene | Bromobenzene | Iodobenzene |
|---|---|---|---|
| State at 298 K | colourless liquid | colourless liquid | colourless liquid |
| Melting point / °C | −45 | −31 | −31 |
| Boiling point / °C | 132 | 156 | 188 |
| Density / g cm⁻³ | 1.11 | 1.49 | 1.83 |
| Dipole moment / D | 1.69 | 1.70 | 1.70 |
| Solubility in water | insoluble | insoluble | insoluble |

The trends to remember:

- **All haloarenes are denser than water** and are **insoluble in water** but freely soluble in benzene, ether and other organic solvents. They cannot form hydrogen bonds with water, and a lot of energy would be needed to break the water–water hydrogen bonds to make room for the large non-polar molecule.
- **Boiling point rises down the halogen group** (Cl → Br → I) because the molecule gets larger and more polarisable, so van der Waals forces get stronger.
- For isomeric dihalobenzenes the *para* isomer always has the **highest melting point** (symmetry and packing), as the table in §9.1 shows.
- Haloarenes are **colourless, sweet-smelling, poisonous liquids** and are **steam-volatile**, which is how chlorobenzene is separated from the reaction mixture in the laboratory.

::: key Why chlorobenzene is less polar than chloromethane
Both have one C–Cl bond, yet μ(C₆H₅Cl) = 1.69 D while μ(CH₃Cl) = 1.86 D. Two reasons: the chlorine's lone pair is partly delocalised **into** the ring (an electron flow *towards* Cl's own carbon opposing the inductive pull), and the carbon holding the Cl is **sp²** (33 % s-character), which is more electronegative than the sp³ carbon of CH₃Cl, so it lets go of its electrons less easily. A shorter bond also means a smaller charge separation distance.
:::

```figure caption="Resonance in chlorobenzene. Delocalisation of a chlorine lone pair into the ring gives the C–Cl bond partial double-bond character and puts negative charge at the *ortho* and *para* positions."

from matplotlib.patches import Circle, FancyArrowPatch
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.2, 2.7))
R = 0.62

def seg(p, q, c=INK, lw=1.4):
    ax.plot([p[0], q[0]], [p[1], q[1]], color=c, lw=lw,
            solid_capstyle='round', zorder=2)

    ax.text(p[0], p[1], s, ha='center', va='center', fontsize=fs, color=c,
            bbox=dict(fc='white', ec='none', pad=0.5), zorder=4)

def ring(cx, cy, dbl=(), arom=False):
    a = np.radians(np.arange(6) * 60 + 90)
    P = [np.array([cx + R * np.cos(t), cy + R * np.sin(t)]) for t in a]
    for i in range(6):
        seg(P[i], P[(i + 1) % 6])

        ax.add_patch(Circle((cx, cy), R * 0.56, fc='none', ec=INK, lw=1.3,
                            zorder=2))

        mid = (p + q) / 2
        v = np.array([cx, cy]) - mid
        v = v / np.hypot(*v) * 0.15
        d = (q - p) * 0.14
        seg(p + v + d, q + v - d)
    return P

## 9.4 Low reactivity compared with haloalkanes in nucleophilic substitution

Warm chloroethane with aqueous KOH and you get ethanol in minutes. Boil chlorobenzene with aqueous KOH for hours and **nothing happens**. The measured rates differ by a factor of the order of 10⁵ to 10⁶.

```figure caption="Hard evidence for the resonance picture: the ring C–Cl bond is shorter and the molecule less polar than in chloromethane, and the C–Cl bond is much harder to break."
import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(5.2, 2.4))
names = ['CH₃Cl', 'C₆H₅Cl']
sets = [([177, 169], 'C–Cl bond length / pm', (150, 190), SERIES[0]),
        ([1.86, 1.69], 'dipole moment / D', (0, 2.3), SERIES[2]),
        ([339, 400], 'C–Cl bond enthalpy\n/ kJ mol⁻¹', (0, 480), SERIES[1])]
for ax, (vals, ylab, ylim, col) in zip(axes, sets):
    ax.bar(names, vals, color=[MUTED, col], width=0.55)
    for i, v in enumerate(vals):
        ax.text(i, v + (ylim[1] - ylim[0]) * 0.03, str(v), ha='center',
                fontsize=7.4, color=INK)
    ax.set_ylabel(ylab, fontsize=7.6)
    ax.set_ylim(*ylim)
    ax.tick_params(labelsize=7.4)
    ax.spines[['top', 'right']].set_visible(False)
    ax.grid(axis='y', alpha=0.35)
fig.subplots_adjust(wspace=0.62, bottom=0.14)
```

There are four reasons, and a full-mark answer gives all four.

**1. Resonance shortens and strengthens the C–Cl bond.** As the resonance figure shows, a lone pair on chlorine overlaps with the ring π system. The C–Cl bond therefore acquires **partial double-bond character**. A double bond is shorter and stronger than a single bond, so the C–Cl bond length falls from 177 pm in chloromethane to 169 pm in chlorobenzene, and the bond becomes much harder to break. A nucleophile must break this bond, so substitution is slow.

**2. The carbon is sp² hybridised.** In chlorobenzene the carbon carrying Cl is sp² (33 % s-character); in chloromethane it is sp³ (25 % s-character). The sp² carbon is more electronegative, holds its bonding electrons closer, and so forms a **shorter, stronger and less polar** C–Cl bond. A less polar bond means a smaller δ⁺ on carbon — exactly the site a nucleophile is looking for.

**3. Repulsion by the π electron cloud.** The benzene ring is a thick sandwich of π electron density above and below the plane. An approaching nucleophile, which is itself an electron-rich species, is **repelled** before it can reach the carbon.

**4. Neither mechanism is available.**

| | why it is blocked |
|---|---|
| S<sub>N</sub>1 | would require a **phenyl cation**, C₆H₅⁺. The empty orbital lies *in* the ring plane, perpendicular to the π cloud, so it gets **no resonance stabilisation**. The cation is far too unstable to form. |
| S<sub>N</sub>2 | requires the nucleophile to attack from **directly behind** the C–Cl bond. That position is occupied by the rest of the benzene ring — the attack is physically impossible. |

::: caution "Chlorobenzene + NaOH → phenol" is only true under ferocious conditions
A very common error is to write C₆H₅Cl + NaOH → C₆H₅OH at room temperature. It does not happen. The industrial **Dow process** needs 10 % aqueous NaOH at **623 K and about 300 atm**, and the product is sodium phenoxide, which must then be acidified:

- C₆H₅Cl + 2NaOH --(623 K, 300 atm)--> C₆H₅ONa + NaCl + H₂O
- C₆H₅ONa + HCl → C₆H₅OH + NaCl

Similarly, ammonolysis to aniline needs NH₃ at 475 K and 60 atm over Cu₂O.
:::

**Electron-withdrawing groups switch the reactivity back on.** If a strongly –R group such as –NO₂ sits **ortho or para** to the halogen, the negative charge of the intermediate can be delocalised onto the nitro oxygen atoms, and nucleophilic substitution becomes easy. The effect is dramatic:

| Compound | Conditions needed for hydrolysis |
|---|---|
| chlorobenzene | 623 K, 300 atm NaOH |
| *p*-nitrochlorobenzene | aqueous NaOH, 435 K |
| 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene | warm aqueous Na₂CO₃ |
| 2,4,6-trinitrochlorobenzene (picryl chloride) | **boiling water alone** |

A nitro group at the *meta* position has almost no effect, because the negative charge of the intermediate never reaches the meta carbon. That test — does the activating group sit ortho/para or meta? — is a favourite Group B question.

## 9.5 Reduction of chlorobenzene; electrophilic substitution reactions

### Reduction

Chlorobenzene is reduced to benzene by nascent hydrogen. Nickel–aluminium alloy in aqueous alkali is the standard laboratory reagent; zinc dust and hydrochloric acid, or hydrogen over a palladium catalyst, also work.

- **C₆H₅Cl + 2[H] --(Ni–Al alloy / NaOH)--> C₆H₆ + HCl**

Reduction is **not** a nucleophilic substitution — the hydrogen atom arrives as a neutral radical-like species, not as a hydride nucleophile, which is why it succeeds where OH⁻ fails.

### Electrophilic substitution

Although the ring is deactivated, it is still aromatic and still reacts with strong electrophiles. Chlorine is one of that small group of substituents that are **deactivating but ortho–para directing**, and you must be able to explain the apparent contradiction:

- **Deactivating**, because the –I (inductive) effect of the electronegative chlorine pulls electron density *out* of the ring, so the ring is less nucleophilic than benzene and reacts more slowly.
- **Ortho–para directing**, because the +R (resonance) effect pushes lone-pair density *into* the ring, and the resonance structures put that extra electron density specifically at the **ortho and para** carbons. Whatever attack does occur therefore happens there.

The *para* product usually dominates, because the bulky electrophile and the chlorine atom get in each other's way at the ortho position (steric hindrance).

| Reaction | Reagents and conditions | Products |
|---|---|---|
| Nitration | conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄, 320 K | *o*-nitrochlorobenzene (≈30 %) + *p*-nitrochlorobenzene (≈70 %) |
| Halogenation | Cl₂, anhyd. AlCl₃ (or FeCl₃) | *o*- and *p*-dichlorobenzene (*p* major) |
| Sulphonation | conc. (or fuming) H₂SO₄, Δ | *o*- and *p*-chlorobenzenesulphonic acid (*p* major) |
| Friedel–Crafts alkylation | CH₃Cl, anhyd. AlCl₃ | *o*- and *p*-chlorotoluene |
| Friedel–Crafts acylation | CH₃COCl, anhyd. AlCl₃ | *o*- and *p*-chloroacetophenone |

::: example Working out the mass of the para product
**Problem.** 45.0 g of chlorobenzene is nitrated. 90 % of it is converted into mononitro products, in which the *ortho* : *para* ratio is 30 : 70. Calculate the mass of *p*-nitrochlorobenzene obtained. (C = 12, H = 1, N = 14, O = 16, Cl = 35.5)

**Solution.**
Moles of C₆H₅Cl = 45.0 / 112.5 = 0.400 mol.

Moles converted = 0.400 × 0.90 = 0.360 mol.
Moles of the *para* isomer = 0.360 × 0.70 = 0.252 mol.

Molar mass of C₆H₄ClNO₂ = (6 × 12) + (4 × 1) + 35.5 + 14 + (2 × 16)
= 72 + 4 + 35.5 + 14 + 32 = 157.5 g mol⁻¹.

Mass = 0.252 × 157.5 = **39.7 g** (3 s.f.).

(The *ortho* isomer accounts for 0.108 mol, that is 17.0 g.)
:::

## 9.6 Action with sodium: Fittig and Wurtz–Fittig reactions; action with chloral

### Fittig reaction

Two molecules of a haloarene are coupled by sodium in dry ether. The product is a **biaryl**:

- **2C₆H₅Cl + 2Na --(dry ether)--> C₆H₅–C₆H₅ + 2NaCl**

The product, biphenyl (diphenyl), is a white crystalline solid, m.p. 71 °C, used as a heat-transfer fluid.

### Wurtz–Fittig reaction

A haloarene and a **haloalkane** together, with sodium in dry ether, give an alkylbenzene. This is the standard way of putting a straight alkyl chain on a ring without the rearrangements that spoil Friedel–Crafts alkylation:

- **C₆H₅Cl + 2Na + CH₃Cl --(dry ether)--> C₆H₅CH₃ + 2NaCl**
- **C₆H₅Br + 2Na + C₂H₅Br --(dry ether)--> C₆H₅C₂H₅ + 2NaBr**

::: tip Why the yield of the "mixed" product is never 100 %
Three couplings compete in a Wurtz–Fittig mixture: aryl + aryl (Fittig, gives biphenyl), alkyl + alkyl (Wurtz, gives an alkane) and aryl + alkyl (the product you want). The ether must be **absolutely dry**, because sodium reacts violently with water and the organosodium intermediate is destroyed by even a trace of moisture.
:::

### Action with chloral — the synthesis of DDT

Chloral (trichloroethanal, CCl₃CHO) condenses with **two** molecules of chlorobenzene in the presence of concentrated sulphuric acid:

- **CCl₃CHO + 2C₆H₅Cl --(conc. H₂SO₄)--> (4-ClC₆H₄)₂CH–CCl₃ + H₂O**

The product is **DDT**, systematically 1,1,1-trichloro-2,2-bis(4-chlorophenyl)ethane, C₁₄H₉Cl₅. Check the balance: carbon 2 + 12 = 14, hydrogen 1 + 10 = 11 (9 in DDT + 2 in water), chlorine 3 + 2 = 5, oxygen 1 = 1. The reaction is an electrophilic substitution in which the protonated aldehyde is the electrophile and the chlorine atoms direct the attack to the *para* positions.

DDT was the first modern synthetic insecticide and saved millions of lives from malaria and typhus, but it is chemically very stable, fat-soluble and non-biodegradable. It therefore **bioaccumulates** up food chains. Its agricultural use is banned in Nepal and in most countries under the Stockholm Convention, though small quantities are still permitted for malaria vector control.

**Problem.** 14.74 g of chloral is condensed with an excess of chlorobenzene in concentrated sulphuric acid. Calculate (i) the mass of chlorobenzene consumed and (ii) the mass of DDT formed, assuming complete reaction. (Molar masses: chloral 147.4, chlorobenzene 112.5, DDT 354.5 g mol⁻¹)

**Solution.**
Moles of chloral = 14.74 / 147.4 = 0.1000 mol.

From CCl₃CHO + 2C₆H₅Cl → DDT + H₂O, the ratio chloral : chlorobenzene : DDT is 1 : 2 : 1.

(i) Moles of chlorobenzene = 2 × 0.1000 = 0.200 mol.
Mass = 0.200 × 112.5 = **22.5 g**.

(ii) Moles of DDT = 0.1000 mol.
Mass = 0.1000 × 354.5 = **35.45 g**.

Check by mass conservation: 14.74 + 22.5 = 37.24 g in; 35.45 g DDT + (0.100 × 18) = 1.80 g water = 37.25 g out. The small difference is rounding.

## 9.7 Uses

| Compound | Use |
|---|---|
| Chlorobenzene | solvent for paints, rubber, waxes and adhesives; heat-transfer medium |
| Chlorobenzene | feedstock for **phenol** (Dow process) and for **aniline** (ammonolysis) |
| Chlorobenzene | intermediate for *o*- and *p*-nitrochlorobenzene → azo dyes and drugs |
| Chlorobenzene + chloral | manufacture of **DDT** (vector control only) |
| *p*-Dichlorobenzene | moth repellent, toilet deodorant block, space fumigant |
| Benzotrichloride, C₆H₅CCl₃ | hydrolysed to benzoic acid |
| Iodobenzene, bromobenzene | laboratory reagents for making Grignard and organolithium compounds |
| Chlorinated aromatics generally | starting materials for pesticides, fungicides and pharmaceuticals |

Most of these uses are now tightly regulated. Chlorinated aromatics are persistent in soil and water and are toxic to aquatic life, so laboratory residues must never be poured down the sink.

## Chapter summary

- A **haloarene** has the halogen on an sp² ring carbon (C₆H₅Cl); an **aralkyl halide** has it on the side chain (C₆H₅CH₂Cl) and behaves like a haloalkane.
- C₆H₄Cl₂ has three positional isomers; the *para* isomer has much the highest melting point because of symmetrical crystal packing.
- Preparation: **C₆H₆ + Cl₂ --(anhyd. AlCl₃, dark)--> C₆H₅Cl + HCl**; industrially **2C₆H₆ + 2HCl + O₂ --(CuCl₂, 500 K)--> 2C₆H₅Cl + 2H₂O** (Raschig).
- From the diazonium salt: **Sandmeyer** (CuCl/HCl) and **Gattermann** (Cu/HCl), both giving C₆H₅Cl + N₂↑; KI alone gives iodobenzene.
- Haloarenes are colourless, sweet-smelling, steam-volatile liquids, **denser than and insoluble in water**; boiling point rises Cl → Br → I.
- Low reactivity to nucleophiles has four causes: resonance gives **partial double-bond character** (C–Cl 169 pm vs 177 pm), the **sp² carbon** is more electronegative, the **π cloud repels** the nucleophile, and neither S<sub>N</sub>1 (unstable phenyl cation) nor S<sub>N</sub>2 (no backside approach) is possible.
- –NO₂ groups **ortho or para** to the halogen switch nucleophilic substitution on; picryl chloride is hydrolysed by boiling water alone.
- –Cl is **deactivating but ortho–para directing**: –I effect slows the ring down, +R effect decides where the electrophile lands. Nitration gives about 30 % *ortho* and 70 % *para*.
- Named reactions: reduction **C₆H₅Cl + 2[H] → C₆H₆ + HCl**; **Fittig 2C₆H₅Cl + 2Na → C₆H₅–C₆H₅ + 2NaCl**; **Wurtz–Fittig C₆H₅Cl + 2Na + CH₃Cl → C₆H₅CH₃ + 2NaCl**; **CCl₃CHO + 2C₆H₅Cl --(conc. H₂SO₄)--> DDT + H₂O**.

**Group B — Short answer (5 marks each)**

1. Explain, with resonance structures, why chlorobenzene does not react with aqueous NaOH at room temperature while chloroethane does. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. How is chlorobenzene prepared from aniline? Give all equations and conditions. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Chlorine is a deactivating group, yet it directs an incoming electrophile to the *ortho* and *para* positions. Explain this apparent contradiction and illustrate it with the nitration of chlorobenzene. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. Distinguish between the Fittig and the Wurtz–Fittig reaction with balanced equations. Why must the ether be perfectly dry? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
5. 78.0 g of benzene is chlorinated in the dark over anhydrous AlCl₃. The yield of chlorobenzene is 70 %. Calculate the mass of chlorobenzene obtained and the volume of HCl gas liberated at STP. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Answers to Group B
**1.** A lone pair on chlorine overlaps the ring π system, so the C–Cl bond has partial double-bond character (169 pm, compared with 177 pm in chloroethane) and is much harder to break. The carbon holding the chlorine is sp², hence more electronegative, giving a shorter, stronger and less polar bond with a smaller δ⁺ on carbon. The π electron cloud repels the incoming OH⁻. Finally neither mechanism is open: S<sub>N</sub>1 would need the very unstable phenyl cation (its empty orbital is in the ring plane and gets no resonance help), and S<sub>N</sub>2 would need backside attack, which the ring blocks. Chloroethane has none of these obstacles: its sp³ carbon carries a large δ⁺ and is freely open to backside attack.

**2.** Diazotise, then apply the Sandmeyer reaction.
C₆H₅NH₂ + NaNO₂ + 2HCl --(273–278 K)--> C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + NaCl + 2H₂O
C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ --(CuCl / conc. HCl, warm)--> C₆H₅Cl + N₂↑
The temperature must be held at 273–278 K (an ice–salt bath) or the diazonium salt decomposes to phenol. The Gattermann variant, using copper powder and concentrated HCl, gives the same product in slightly lower yield.

**3.** Chlorine exerts two opposing effects. Its –I effect withdraws electron density from the whole ring, so the ring is less nucleophilic than benzene and every electrophilic substitution is slower — the ring is *deactivated*. Its +R effect donates a lone pair into the ring, and the resonance structures that result carry the extra negative charge specifically on the *ortho* and *para* carbons. So although attack is slow, when it happens it happens at those positions. Nitration with conc. HNO₃ and conc. H₂SO₄ at 320 K gives about 30 % *o*-nitrochlorobenzene and 70 % *p*-nitrochlorobenzene, the *para* isomer dominating because the *ortho* position is sterically crowded by the chlorine atom.

**4.** Fittig couples two aryl halides:
2C₆H₅Cl + 2Na --(dry ether)--> C₆H₅–C₆H₅ + 2NaCl
Wurtz–Fittig couples an aryl halide with an alkyl halide:
C₆H₅Cl + 2Na + CH₃Cl --(dry ether)--> C₆H₅CH₃ + 2NaCl
The ether must be perfectly dry because sodium reacts violently with water, and the organosodium intermediate (C₆H₅Na) is instantly destroyed by moisture, giving benzene instead of the coupled product.

**5.** Moles of benzene = 78.0 / 78 = 1.00 mol.
C₆H₆ + Cl₂ → C₆H₅Cl + HCl, a 1 : 1 : 1 reaction.
Theoretical chlorobenzene = 1.00 × 112.5 = 112.5 g; at 70 % yield, mass = 0.70 × 112.5 = **78.75 g**.
HCl is produced in step with the chlorobenzene, so moles of HCl = 0.70 mol.
Volume at STP = 0.70 × 22.4 = **15.68 L**.
:::

**1.** (a) Direct chlorination, C₆H₆ + Cl₂ --(anhyd. AlCl₃, dark)--> C₆H₅Cl + HCl; and the Sandmeyer reaction, C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ --(CuCl/HCl)--> C₆H₅Cl + N₂↑ (the Raschig process is an acceptable third). H₂SO₄)--> (4-ClC₆H₄)₂CH–CCl₃ + H₂O; C₆H₅Cl + 2[H] --(Ni–Al/NaOH)--> C₆H₆ + HCl. **3.** (a) Rate: the –I effect of chlorine withdraws electron density, making the ring less nucleophilic, so substitution is slower than in benzene — chlorine is deactivating. Orientation: the +R effect feeds the chlorine lone pair into the ring, concentrating electron density at the *ortho* and *para* carbons, so the electrophile attacks there; *para* usually predominates on steric grounds. (b) chlorobenzene < *p*-nitrochlorobenzene < 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene. Each nitro group placed *ortho* or *para* to the chlorine withdraws electrons by –R and stabilises the negatively charged intermediate formed when the nucleophile adds, so the more such groups there are, the milder the conditions needed. (c) The *para* isomer is symmetrical and almost linear, so its molecules pack tightly and regularly into the crystal lattice; more energy is needed to break that lattice. The *ortho* isomer is bent and packs poorly, giving a much weaker lattice and a far lower melting point.
