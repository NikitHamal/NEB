Aldehydes and ketones both contain the **carbonyl group**, >C=O. In an aldehyde
the carbonyl carbon carries at least one hydrogen; in a ketone it carries two
carbon groups. That single difference in substitution controls everything in this
unit — which reagent oxidises which, which compound gives a silver mirror, and
which one condenses with itself. At ten hours this is the longest organic unit in
the course and the most heavily examined.

::: key What the examiner wants from this unit
Four things come back every year: (1) **distinguish** an aldehyde from a ketone
and one aldehyde from another, with reagent, observation and equation; (2) the
**nucleophilic addition** mechanism and the reactivity order HCHO > RCHO > RCOR′;
(3) the named reactions — aldol, Cannizzaro, Clemmensen, Wolff–Kishner, Perkin,
benzoin — with reagent, condition and product; (4) conversions, i.e. "how would
you convert ethanal into butan-1-ol".
:::

## 13.1 Aliphatic aldehydes and ketones: introduction, nomenclature, isomerism

Both families have the general formula CₙH₂ₙO for the saturated open-chain case.
An **aldehyde** is R—CHO (the carbonyl carbon is bonded to H); a **ketone** is
R—CO—R′. The group —CHO is called the *formyl* or aldehydic group; —CO— is the
*keto* group. Ketones are **simple (symmetrical)** when R = R′ and **mixed
(unsymmetrical)** when R ≠ R′.

::: definition Aldehyde and ketone
An **aldehyde** is a compound in which a carbonyl group is attached to one
hydrogen atom and one alkyl or aryl group (or to two hydrogen atoms, as in
methanal). A **ketone** is a compound in which the carbonyl group is attached to
two alkyl or aryl groups.
:::

**IUPAC rules.** For aldehydes, replace the *-e* of the parent alkane by *-al*;
the —CHO carbon is always C-1 and needs no locant. For ketones, replace *-e* by
*-one* and number the chain to give the carbonyl carbon the lowest possible
number. When —CHO is attached to a ring, the suffix is *-carbaldehyde*.

| Formula | IUPAC name | Common name | Derived name |
|---|---|---|---|
| HCHO | methanal | formaldehyde | — |
| CH₃CHO | ethanal | acetaldehyde | — |
| CH₃CH₂CHO | propanal | propionaldehyde | — |
| (CH₃)₂CHCHO | 2-methylpropanal | isobutyraldehyde | — |
| CH₃COCH₃ | propanone | acetone | dimethyl ketone |
| CH₃COCH₂CH₃ | butanone | methyl ethyl ketone | ethyl methyl ketone |
| CH₃COCH₂CH₂CH₃ | pentan-2-one | — | methyl propyl ketone |
| CH₃CH₂COCH₂CH₃ | pentan-3-one | — | diethyl ketone |
| C₆H₅CHO | benzenecarbaldehyde | benzaldehyde | — |
| C₆H₅COCH₃ | 1-phenylethanone | acetophenone | methyl phenyl ketone |

**Isomerism.** Carbonyl compounds show four kinds:

1. **Chain isomerism** — pentanal, 2-methylbutanal, 3-methylbutanal and
   2,2-dimethylpropanal are all C₅H₁₀O aldehydes differing only in the skeleton.
2. **Position isomerism** — in ketones the carbonyl group can sit at different
   positions: pentan-2-one and pentan-3-one.
3. **Metamerism** — the same carbonyl group flanked by different pairs of alkyl
   groups: pentan-2-one (methyl propyl ketone) and pentan-3-one (diethyl ketone).
4. **Functional isomerism** — an aldehyde and a ketone of the same formula are
   functional isomers (propanal and propanone, C₃H₆O). Alkenols and cyclic ethers
   of the same formula are also functional isomers.

C₄H₈O has exactly **three** carbonyl isomers: butanal, 2-methylpropanal and
butanone. Learning to count these quickly is worth a Group A mark.

Carbonyl compounds with an α-hydrogen also show **tautomerism**, a dynamic
equilibrium between the keto and the enol form:

CH₃CHO ⇌ CH₂=CH—OH   (keto ⇌ enol; for ethanal the enol is under 0.01 %)

## 13.2 Preparation

### Dehydrogenation and oxidation of alcohols

A primary alcohol gives an aldehyde, a secondary alcohol gives a ketone, and a
tertiary alcohol gives neither. **Catalytic dehydrogenation** passes the alcohol
vapour over hot copper:

CH₃CH₂OH --Cu, 573 K--> CH₃CHO + H₂

(CH₃)₂CHOH --Cu, 573 K--> CH₃COCH₃ + H₂

**Oxidation** uses acidified potassium dichromate, acidified KMnO₄, or — better
for aldehydes — a mild reagent that stops at the aldehyde:

3CH₃CH₂OH + K₂Cr₂O₇ + 4H₂SO₄ → 3CH₃CHO + K₂SO₄ + Cr₂(SO₄)₃ + 7H₂O

::: caution Why ethanal must be distilled off at once
Ethanal (b.p. 21 °C) boils far below ethanol (78 °C), so it is distilled out as
it forms. If it stays in the flask the dichromate oxidises it further to
ethanoic acid. In the laboratory the controlled reagents are **PCC** (pyridinium
chlorochromate) in dichloromethane, or Cu at 573 K.
:::

### Ozonolysis of alkenes

Ozone adds across the double bond to give an ozonide, which is cleaved by zinc
dust and water. The zinc destroys the hydrogen peroxide formed, so the aldehyde
is not oxidised further (**reductive ozonolysis**):

CH₃CH=CHCH₃ --O₃--> ozonide --Zn / H₂O--> 2CH₃CHO

(CH₃)₂C=CHCH₃ --O₃, then Zn / H₂O--> CH₃COCH₃ + CH₃CHO

Each doubly bonded carbon keeps its own substituents, so ozonolysis run
*backwards* locates a double bond — a standard structure-determination question.

### From acid chlorides: Rosenmund reduction

Hydrogen over palladium supported on barium sulphate, **poisoned** with sulphur
or quinoline, reduces an acid chloride only as far as the aldehyde:

CH₃COCl + H₂ --Pd / BaSO₄, S poison--> CH₃CHO + HCl

Without the poison the catalyst is too active and the product is the alcohol.
Ketones are made from acid chlorides with dialkyl cadmium or a Grignard reagent
at low temperature.

### From gem-dihaloalkanes

Two halogens on the *same* carbon hydrolyse to an unstable gem-diol, which loses
water:

CH₃CHCl₂ + 2NaOH(aq) → CH₃CHO + 2NaCl + H₂O

CH₃CCl₂CH₃ + 2NaOH(aq) → CH₃COCH₃ + 2NaCl + H₂O

A 1,1-dihalide gives a carbonyl compound; a 1,2-dihalide would give a diol.

### Catalytic hydration of alkynes

Water adds across the triple bond in dilute sulphuric acid with mercury(II)
sulphate as catalyst (Markovnikov addition). The enol formed tautomerises:

C₂H₂ + H₂O --HgSO₄ / dil. H₂SO₄, 333 K--> CH₂=CHOH → CH₃CHO

CH₃C≡CH + H₂O --HgSO₄ / dil. H₂SO₄--> CH₃COCH₃

Ethyne is the **only** alkyne that gives an aldehyde; every higher alkyne gives a
ketone.

## 13.3 Physical properties; structure and nature of the carbonyl group

Methanal is a gas, ethanal a volatile liquid (b.p. 21 °C), and the rest up to
about C₁₁ are liquids with characteristic smells. Because there is no O—H bond,
carbonyl compounds **cannot hydrogen bond to each other**, only dipole–dipole
attraction operates between molecules. But the carbonyl oxygen *can* accept a
hydrogen bond from water, so the lower members are freely water-soluble.

**Structure of the carbonyl group.** The carbonyl carbon is **sp² hybridised**.
Three sp² orbitals form σ bonds to oxygen and to the two other groups, all in one
plane at about 120°; the left-over p orbital on carbon overlaps sideways with a p
orbital on oxygen to give the π bond. The C=O bond length is 122 pm — shorter
than the 143 pm of a C—O single bond — and its bond enthalpy is about
740 kJ mol⁻¹.

Oxygen is much more electronegative than carbon, so the π cloud is pulled towards
oxygen. The group is strongly polar (dipole moment of ethanal 2.7 D, of propanone
2.9 D) and is best described by two contributing structures:

>C=O ⇌ >C⁺—O⁻

Three consequences follow, and they organise the whole of the rest of the unit:

- the δ+ carbon is attacked by **nucleophiles** — hence addition of HCN, NaHSO₃,
  Grignard reagents, and reaction with ammonia derivatives;
- the δ− oxygen is **protonated** by acids, which makes the carbon still more
  electrophilic — acid catalysis;
- the α-hydrogen becomes weakly acidic because the resulting carbanion is
  stabilised by the carbonyl group — hence aldol condensation, iodoform
  formation and halogenation.

## 13.4 Distinction by 2,4-DNP, Tollen's reagent and Fehling's solution

The logic is two-step. First prove a **carbonyl group is present** (2,4-DNP or
Schiff's reagent, or the formation of a crystalline derivative). Then find out
whether it is an **aldehyde**, which is easily oxidised, or a ketone, which is
not. Mild oxidising agents such as Tollens' reagent and Fehling's solution
oxidise aldehydes only.

| Test | Reagent | Aldehyde | Ketone | What you see |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2,4-DNP (Brady's) | 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine in acid | ✓ | ✓ | orange to red crystalline precipitate |
| Schiff's | dilute magenta decolourised by SO₂ | ✓ | ✗ | pink/magenta colour restored |
| Tollens' | ammoniacal AgNO₃, warm | ✓ | ✗ | shining **silver mirror** on the tube |
| Fehling's | CuSO₄ + alkaline sodium tartrate | ✓ | ✗ | deep blue → **red-brown Cu₂O** precipitate |
| Benedict's | CuSO₄ + sodium citrate + Na₂CO₃ | ✓ | ✗ | green → yellow → brick-red Cu₂O |
| Iodoform | I₂ + NaOH, warm | only CH₃CHO | only methyl ketones | yellow crystals, antiseptic smell |
| NaHSO₃ | saturated sodium bisulphite | ✓ | ✓ (small ketones) | white crystalline adduct |

The equations, with charges and atoms balanced:

RCHO + 2,4-(NO₂)₂C₆H₃NHNH₂ → RCH=N—NHC₆H₃(NO₂)₂ + H₂O

RCHO + 2[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 3OH⁻ → RCOO⁻ + 2Ag↓ + 4NH₃ + 2H₂O

RCHO + 2Cu²⁺ + 5OH⁻ → RCOO⁻ + Cu₂O↓ + 3H₂O

CH₃CHO + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CHI₃↓ + HCOONa + 3NaI + 3H₂O

CH₃COCH₃ + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CHI₃↓ + CH₃COONa + 3NaI + 3H₂O

::: tip Reading the iodoform test correctly
The iodoform test does **not** separate aldehydes from ketones. It detects the
CH₃—CO— grouping (or CH₃—CH(OH)—, which the iodine oxidises to it). So ethanal,
propanone, butanone, acetophenone, ethanol and propan-2-ol all give iodoform,
while propanal, benzaldehyde and pentan-3-one do not.
:::

::: caution Two aldehydes that break the rules
**Benzaldehyde reduces Tollens' reagent but does not reduce Fehling's solution.**
Fehling's is only mildly oxidising and the resonance-stabilised aromatic aldehyde
resists it. And **methanoic acid, HCOOH, reduces both** even though it is an acid,
because it still contains a —CHO grouping (Unit 14). Never write "Fehling's is
positive" for benzaldehyde.
:::

## 13.5 Addition of H₂, HCN and NaHSO₃

All three are **nucleophilic addition** reactions. The nucleophile attacks the δ+
carbon, the π electrons fold back onto oxygen to give an alkoxide, and the
alkoxide picks up a proton. The carbon changes from sp² (planar) to sp³
(tetrahedral).

**Addition of hydrogen** (reduction) uses H₂ with Ni, Pt or Pd:

CH₃CHO + H₂ --Ni, 413 K--> CH₃CH₂OH   (a primary alcohol)

CH₃COCH₃ + H₂ --Ni--> (CH₃)₂CHOH   (a secondary alcohol)

**Addition of HCN** gives a **cyanohydrin**. The reaction is base-catalysed,
because it is CN⁻ and not HCN that is the nucleophile — a trace of KCN speeds it
up enormously:

CH₃CHO + HCN → CH₃CH(OH)CN   (2-hydroxypropanenitrile)

CH₃COCH₃ + HCN → (CH₃)₂C(OH)CN   (2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile)

Cyanohydrins are useful because the nitrile can then be hydrolysed to an
α-hydroxy acid — this is how lactic acid is made from ethanal.

**Addition of NaHSO₃** gives a crystalline bisulphite adduct. It is reversible on
warming with dilute acid or alkali, so it is used to **purify** and to **store**
aldehydes:

CH₃CHO + NaHSO₃ → CH₃CH(OH)SO₃Na

### Relative reactivity towards nucleophilic addition

HCHO > CH₃CHO > CH₃CH₂CHO > CH₃COCH₃ > CH₃COC₂H₅ > C₆H₅CHO > C₆H₅COCH₃

Two effects act in the same direction:

- **Electronic.** Alkyl groups release electrons (+I), so each one reduces the δ+
  charge on the carbonyl carbon. Methanal has none, ethanal one, propanone two.
  In benzaldehyde the ring donates electron density by resonance, which is why an
  aromatic aldehyde is less reactive than an aliphatic one.
- **Steric.** The nucleophile has to reach a carbon that becomes crowded as it
  turns tetrahedral. Two bulky groups hinder the approach far more than one.

## 13.6 Action with ammonia derivatives

Ammonia derivatives of the type H₂N—Z condense with the carbonyl group,
eliminating water and forming a C=N bond. The products are sharp-melting
crystalline solids, so they are used to **identify** an unknown carbonyl
compound. All these reactions are fastest at about pH 4–5: enough acid to
activate the carbonyl, not so much that the weakly basic nitrogen is all
protonated.

| Reagent | Formula | Product name | Product from CH₃CHO |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydroxylamine | NH₂OH | oxime | CH₃CH=NOH + H₂O |
| Hydrazine | NH₂—NH₂ | hydrazone | CH₃CH=N—NH₂ + H₂O |
| Phenylhydrazine | C₆H₅NHNH₂ | phenylhydrazone | CH₃CH=N—NHC₆H₅ + H₂O |
| 2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine | (NO₂)₂C₆H₃NHNH₂ | 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazone | orange solid + H₂O |
| Semicarbazide | H₂N—NH—CO—NH₂ | semicarbazone | CH₃CH=N—NHCONH₂ + H₂O |

The general equation is

>C=O + H₂N—Z → >C=N—Z + H₂O

and the mechanism is nucleophilic addition of the nitrogen followed by
dehydration of the resulting carbinolamine.

::: memory Ammonia derivatives — the four to remember
**H-H-P-S**: **H**ydroxylamine gives an **oxime**; **H**ydrazine gives a
**hydrazone**; **P**henylhydrazine gives a **phenylhydrazone**;
**S**emicarbazide gives a **semicarbazone**. Every one of them loses one
molecule of water.
:::

## 13.7 Aldol condensation and Cannizzaro's reaction

These two reactions are complementary, and which one a compound gives depends on
a single structural feature — the **α-hydrogen**.

### Aldol condensation (needs α-hydrogen)

Dilute alkali removes an α-hydrogen to give a resonance-stabilised **enolate**,
which attacks the carbonyl carbon of a second molecule. The product is a
β-hydroxy carbonyl compound — an "**ald**ehyde-alcoh**ol**", hence *aldol*. On
warming it loses water to give an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compound:

2CH₃CHO --dil. NaOH, 280 K--> CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO --Δ, −H₂O--> CH₃CH=CHCHO

(3-hydroxybutanal, the aldol → but-2-enal, crotonaldehyde)

2CH₃COCH₃ --dil. Ba(OH)₂--> (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂COCH₃ --Δ, −H₂O--> (CH₃)₂C=CHCOCH₃

(4-hydroxy-4-methylpentan-2-one → 4-methylpent-3-en-2-one, mesityl oxide)

A **cross aldol** between two different carbonyl compounds can give up to four
products, so in practice one partner is chosen to have no α-hydrogen.

### Cannizzaro's reaction (no α-hydrogen)

An aldehyde with **no** α-hydrogen cannot form an enolate. With concentrated
alkali it instead disproportionates: one molecule is reduced to the alcohol and
the other is oxidised to the salt of the acid.

2HCHO + NaOH(conc.) → CH₃OH + HCOONa

2C₆H₅CHO + NaOH(conc.) → C₆H₅CH₂OH + C₆H₅COONa

The hydride ion is transferred from the first molecule to the carbonyl carbon of
the second — an **intermolecular hydride shift**. Methanal, benzaldehyde,
2,2-dimethylpropanal (trimethylacetaldehyde) and furfural all give Cannizzaro;
ethanal, propanal and propanone give aldol.

::: example Worked example 13.1 — deciding aldol or Cannizzaro
**Problem.** Predict the product when (a) propanal and (b) 2,2-dimethylpropanal,
(CH₃)₃CCHO, are warmed with sodium hydroxide. Name the products.

**Solution.**

(a) Propanal, CH₃CH₂CHO, has two α-hydrogens on the CH₂ next to the —CHO, so it
undergoes **aldol condensation**:

2CH₃CH₂CHO → CH₃CH₂CH(OH)CH(CH₃)CHO --Δ, −H₂O--> CH₃CH₂CH=C(CH₃)CHO

The aldol is 3-hydroxy-2-methylpentanal and the final product is
2-methylpent-2-enal.

(b) In (CH₃)₃CCHO the carbon next to —CHO carries three methyl groups and **no
hydrogen**. No enolate is possible, so with concentrated alkali it gives
**Cannizzaro's reaction**:

2(CH₃)₃CCHO + NaOH → (CH₃)₃CCH₂OH + (CH₃)₃CCOONa

i.e. 2,2-dimethylpropan-1-ol and sodium 2,2-dimethylpropanoate.
:::

## 13.8 Clemmensen's reduction and Wolff–Kishner reduction

Both convert >C=O all the way to >CH₂ — a **complete removal of oxygen**, not
just reduction to an alcohol. They differ only in the medium, and that is the
whole point of the pair: one is acidic, the other is basic, so you choose the one
that the rest of the molecule can survive.

**Clemmensen's reduction** — zinc amalgam and concentrated hydrochloric acid:

CH₃COCH₃ + 4[H] --Zn-Hg / conc. HCl--> CH₃CH₂CH₃ + H₂O

**Wolff–Kishner reduction** — hydrazine first, then a strong base at high
temperature. It is a two-step process:

CH₃COCH₃ + NH₂NH₂ → (CH₃)₂C=N—NH₂ + H₂O

(CH₃)₂C=N—NH₂ --KOH / ethylene glycol, 453 K--> CH₃CH₂CH₃ + N₂↑

| Feature | Clemmensen | Wolff–Kishner |
|---|---|---|
| Reagent | Zn-Hg, conc. HCl | NH₂NH₂, then KOH or NaOC₂H₅ |
| Medium | strongly **acidic** | strongly **basic** |
| Temperature | reflux, ~373 K | 453–473 K in ethylene glycol |
| Product | alkane (>CH₂) | alkane (>CH₂) |
| By-product | H₂O | N₂ gas |
| Use it when | the molecule is base-sensitive (e.g. contains an ester) | the molecule is acid-sensitive (e.g. contains an alcohol that would dehydrate) |

::: caution "Reduction" does not always mean the same product
Three different reducing systems give three different products from the same
ketone. H₂/Ni or LiAlH₄ or NaBH₄ stop at the **alcohol**. Zn-Hg/HCl or
NH₂NH₂/KOH go on to the **alkane**. Mg-Hg/H₂O gives a **diol** (pinacol). Read
the reagent before writing the product.
:::

## 13.9 Action with PCl₅ and LiAlH₄; methanal with ammonia and with phenol

**With PCl₅** the carbonyl oxygen is replaced by two chlorine atoms, giving a
gem-dihaloalkane — the reverse of the preparation in Section 13.2:

CH₃CHO + PCl₅ → CH₃CHCl₂ + POCl₃

CH₃COCH₃ + PCl₅ → CH₃CCl₂CH₃ + POCl₃

**With LiAlH₄** (or NaBH₄, or H₂/Ni) the carbonyl group is reduced to an alcohol.
Lithium aluminium hydride delivers a hydride ion, H⁻, to the δ+ carbon; the
reaction is carried out in dry ether and then worked up with water:

CH₃CHO + 2[H] --LiAlH₄, dry ether, then H₂O--> CH₃CH₂OH

CH₃COCH₃ + 2[H] --LiAlH₄--> (CH₃)₂CHOH

Aldehydes give **primary** alcohols; ketones give **secondary** alcohols.

**Methanal with ammonia.** Six molecules of methanal and four of ammonia
condense to a cage-shaped molecule, hexamethylenetetramine, known as
**urotropine**:

6HCHO + 4NH₃ → (CH₂)₆N₄ + 6H₂O

Urotropine is a urinary antiseptic, and on nitration it gives the explosive RDX.
Higher aldehydes and ketones give simple aldimines, RCH=NH, with ammonia instead.

**Methanal with phenol.** In the presence of acid or alkali, phenol attacks
methanal at the *ortho* and *para* positions to give hydroxymethylphenols, which
then condense repeatedly:

C₆H₅OH + HCHO → o-HOC₆H₄CH₂OH --further HCHO, Δ--> **bakelite**

Bakelite is a hard, three-dimensional cross-linked thermosetting plastic used for
electrical switches and handles (Unit 18).

## 13.10 Formalin and its uses

::: definition Formalin
**Formalin** is a 37–40 % (w/v) aqueous solution of methanal, usually containing
about 10 % methanol to stop it polymerising to paraformaldehyde, (HCHO)ₙ.
:::

Methanal itself is a pungent gas (b.p. −19 °C) and very soluble in water. On
standing, its aqueous solution slowly deposits a white solid, paraformaldehyde;
warming the solid regenerates the gas. Dry methanal with a trace of acid gives
the cyclic trimer trioxane.

Uses of formalin:

- **Preserving** biological specimens and **embalming**, because it hardens
  protein by cross-linking it;
- as a **disinfectant and germicide** for surgical instruments and animal sheds;
- manufacture of **bakelite** and of urotropine;
- hardening gelatin films in photography, and tanning leather;
- **seed dressing** against fungal disease, widely used in Nepali agriculture;
- silvering of mirrors, as a reducing agent.

::: caution Formalin is not food-grade
Formalin is toxic and a suspected carcinogen; adulterating fish or milk with it
is illegal in Nepal. "Preservative" here means preserving dead tissue for
study, not preserving food.
:::

## 13.11 Aromatic carbonyl compounds: benzaldehyde and acetophenone

### Benzaldehyde from toluene

Three routes, all attacking the methyl side chain:

**(a) Side-chain chlorination then hydrolysis.** Chlorine in bright light or at
high temperature substitutes in the side chain, not the ring:

C₆H₅CH₃ + 2Cl₂ --hν, 673 K--> C₆H₅CHCl₂ + 2HCl

C₆H₅CHCl₂ + H₂O --Ca(OH)₂, 373 K--> C₆H₅CHO + 2HCl

**(b) Étard reaction.** Chromyl chloride in carbon disulphide gives a brown
solid complex, which is then hydrolysed:

C₆H₅CH₃ + 2CrO₂Cl₂ --CS₂--> C₆H₅CH(OCrOHCl₂)₂ --H₃O⁺--> C₆H₅CHO

**(c) Controlled oxidation** with manganese dioxide in 60 % sulphuric acid at
about 313 K:

C₆H₅CH₃ + 2[O] --MnO₂ / 60 % H₂SO₄--> C₆H₅CHO + H₂O

### Acetophenone from benzene

Friedel–Crafts acylation with ethanoyl chloride or ethanoic anhydride and
anhydrous aluminium chloride:

C₆H₆ + CH₃COCl --anhyd. AlCl₃--> C₆H₅COCH₃ + HCl

C₆H₆ + (CH₃CO)₂O --anhyd. AlCl₃--> C₆H₅COCH₃ + CH₃COOH

Acylation, unlike alkylation, does not rearrange and does not go twice over,
because the —COCH₃ group deactivates the ring.

## 13.12 Properties of benzaldehyde

Benzaldehyde, C₆H₅CHO, is a colourless oily liquid (b.p. 178 °C) that smells of
bitter almonds — it occurs in almond and peach kernels as the glycoside amygdalin.
It oxidises in air to benzoic acid, and the white crystals in an old bottle of
benzaldehyde are benzoic acid.

### Perkin condensation

Benzaldehyde condenses with an aliphatic anhydride in the presence of the
corresponding sodium salt to give an α,β-unsaturated aromatic acid:

C₆H₅CHO + (CH₃CO)₂O --CH₃COONa, 453 K--> C₆H₅CH=CHCOOH + CH₃COOH

The product is **cinnamic acid** (3-phenylpropenoic acid). The mechanism is an
aldol-type attack by the anhydride's enolate followed by dehydration.

### Benzoin condensation

With alcoholic potassium cyanide, two molecules of benzaldehyde join head to
tail to give an α-hydroxy ketone:

2C₆H₅CHO --alc. KCN--> C₆H₅CH(OH)COC₆H₅

The product is **benzoin**. Here CN⁻ acts as a catalyst, not a reagent: it adds,
makes the aldehydic hydrogen acidic, and is expelled again.

### Cannizzaro's reaction

Because there is no α-hydrogen, concentrated alkali makes benzaldehyde
disproportionate (Section 13.7):

2C₆H₅CHO + NaOH(conc.) → C₆H₅CH₂OH + C₆H₅COONa

### Electrophilic substitution

The —CHO group is electron-withdrawing (−I and −R), so it **deactivates** the
ring and directs incoming groups to the **meta** position:

C₆H₅CHO + HNO₃ --conc. H₂SO₄, 328 K--> m-O₂NC₆H₄CHO + H₂O

C₆H₅CHO + H₂SO₄ (fuming) → m-HO₃SC₆H₄CHO + H₂O

C₆H₅CHO + Cl₂ --anhyd. AlCl₃--> m-ClC₆H₄CHO + HCl

::: memory Named reactions of this unit and the next, at a glance
| Reaction | Reagent and conditions | Substrate needs | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Aldol condensation** | dil. NaOH or Ba(OH)₂, 280–290 K | α-hydrogen | β-hydroxy carbonyl, then α,β-unsaturated carbonyl |
| **Cannizzaro** | conc. NaOH or KOH (50 %) | **no** α-hydrogen | alcohol + salt of the acid |
| **Clemmensen** | Zn-Hg / conc. HCl, reflux | any >C=O | alkane + H₂O |
| **Wolff–Kishner** | NH₂NH₂, then KOH / glycol, 453 K | any >C=O | alkane + N₂ |
| **Rosenmund** | H₂, Pd/BaSO₄ poisoned with S | acid chloride | aldehyde + HCl |
| **Perkin** | (RCO)₂O + RCOONa, 453 K | aromatic aldehyde | α,β-unsaturated acid |
| **Benzoin** | alcoholic KCN | aromatic aldehyde | α-hydroxy ketone |
| **Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky** | Cl₂ or Br₂ / red P (Unit 14) | carboxylic acid with α-H | α-halo acid |

::: example Worked example 13.2 — identifying an unknown
**Problem.** A neutral liquid **A** of molecular formula C₃H₆O gives an orange
precipitate with 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine and yellow crystals with iodine and
sodium hydroxide, but gives no silver mirror with Tollens' reagent. Identify
**A** and write the equations for the two positive tests.

**Solution.** C₃H₆O fits both propanal and propanone.

- The 2,4-DNP precipitate proves a **carbonyl group** is present.
- No silver mirror means **A is not an aldehyde**, so it is not propanal.
- The positive iodoform test proves a CH₃—CO— group.

Therefore **A is propanone, CH₃COCH₃**.

CH₃COCH₃ + (NO₂)₂C₆H₃NHNH₂ → (CH₃)₂C=N—NHC₆H₃(NO₂)₂ + H₂O

CH₃COCH₃ + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CHI₃↓ + CH₃COONa + 3NaI + 3H₂O

Check the second equation: C 3 = 1 + 2 ✓, H 6 + 4 = 1 + 3 + 6 ✓, I 6 = 3 + 3 ✓,
Na 4 = 1 + 3 ✓, O 1 + 4 = 2 + 3 ✓.
:::

::: example Worked example 13.3 — a Tollens' calculation
**Problem.** 2.20 g of ethanal is warmed with excess Tollens' reagent until the
reaction is complete. Calculate the mass of silver deposited.
(Ar: C = 12, H = 1, O = 16, Ag = 108.)

**Solution.** Molar mass of CH₃CHO = 2(12) + 4(1) + 16 = 44 g mol⁻¹.

$$ n(\text{CH}_3\text{CHO}) = \frac{2.20}{44} = 0.0500\ \text{mol} $$

From the equation

CH₃CHO + 2[Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ + 3OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + 2Ag + 4NH₃ + 2H₂O

one mole of ethanal gives **two** moles of silver, so
$n(\text{Ag}) = 2 \times 0.0500 = 0.100$ mol.

$$ m(\text{Ag}) = 0.100 \times 108 = 10.8\ \text{g} $$

**Answer: 10.8 g of silver.**
:::

::: example Worked example 13.4 — iodoform from propanone
**Problem.** 5.80 g of propanone is treated with excess iodine and sodium
hydroxide. Calculate the maximum mass of iodoform obtainable.
(Ar: I = 127.)

**Solution.** M(CH₃COCH₃) = 3(12) + 6(1) + 16 = 58 g mol⁻¹, so

$$ n = \frac{5.80}{58} = 0.100\ \text{mol} $$

The equation CH₃COCH₃ + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CHI₃ + CH₃COONa + 3NaI + 3H₂O shows a
1 : 1 ratio, so $n(\text{CHI}_3) = 0.100$ mol.

M(CHI₃) = 12 + 1 + 3(127) = 394 g mol⁻¹.

$$ m(\text{CHI}_3) = 0.100 \times 394 = 39.4\ \text{g} $$

**Answer: 39.4 g of iodoform.** Note that 0.300 mol of I₂ (76.2 g) is consumed,
which is why the test is only a test and not a synthesis.
:::

::: example Worked example 13.5 — a multi-step conversion
**Problem.** How would you convert ethanal into butan-1-ol? Give the reagents and
equations for each step.

**Solution.** Butan-1-ol has four carbons; ethanal has two. Aldol condensation is
the carbon–carbon bond-forming step.

**Step 1 — aldol condensation.** Warm ethanal with dilute sodium hydroxide:

2CH₃CHO --dil. NaOH--> CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO

**Step 2 — dehydration.** Heat the aldol; it loses water:

CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CHO --Δ--> CH₃CH=CHCHO + H₂O   (but-2-enal)

**Step 3 — complete reduction.** Hydrogen over nickel reduces both the C=C and
the C=O:

CH₃CH=CHCHO + 2H₂ --Ni, 423 K--> CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂OH

Atom check on step 3: C₄H₆O + 2H₂ = C₄H₁₀O ✓ — butan-1-ol.

## Chapter summary

- Aldehydes are R—CHO, ketones R—CO—R′; both are CₙH₂ₙO and are **functional
  isomers** of each other. Ketones also show position isomerism and metamerism.
- The carbonyl carbon is **sp², planar, 120°**, C=O = 122 pm, strongly polarised
  Cδ+=Oδ−. This makes the carbon electrophilic, the oxygen basic and the
  α-hydrogen acidic.
- Preparations: oxidation or Cu-dehydrogenation of alcohols (1° → aldehyde,
  2° → ketone), reductive ozonolysis of alkenes, Rosenmund reduction of acid
  chlorides, hydrolysis of gem-dihalides, HgSO₄/H₂SO₄ hydration of alkynes
  (ethyne → ethanal, all others → ketone).
- Both give 2,4-DNP; only aldehydes give Tollens' silver mirror, Fehling's Cu₂O
  and Schiff's pink. Benzaldehyde gives Tollens but **not** Fehling. Iodoform
  tests for CH₃CO—, not for the aldehyde group.
- Nucleophilic addition: HCN → cyanohydrin, NaHSO₃ → bisulphite adduct,
  H₂/LiAlH₄ → alcohol. Reactivity HCHO > RCHO > RCOR′ > ArCHO, from +I and
  steric effects.
- Ammonia derivatives H₂N—Z give >C=N—Z + H₂O: oxime, hydrazone,
  phenylhydrazone, semicarbazone.
- α-hydrogen present → **aldol**; α-hydrogen absent → **Cannizzaro**.
  Clemmensen (Zn-Hg/HCl) and Wolff–Kishner (NH₂NH₂/KOH) both give the alkane.
- Benzaldehyde: Perkin → cinnamic acid; benzoin condensation → benzoin;
  Cannizzaro → benzyl alcohol + sodium benzoate; electrophilic substitution is
  **meta**.
- Formalin is 37–40 % aqueous methanal; 6HCHO + 4NH₃ → (CH₂)₆N₄ + 6H₂O
  (urotropine); HCHO + phenol → bakelite.

5. The correct order of reactivity towards HCN addition is <span class="marks">[1]</span>

::: note Answers to Group A
**1.** (c) — only an aliphatic aldehyde reduces Fehling's; benzaldehyde does not.

::: note Answers to Group B
**2.** (a) Tollens' reagent: ethanal gives a silver mirror, propanone gives no

(b) CH₃CHO + [O] --K₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄--> CH₃COOH.

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. (a) Explain the structure and polar nature of the carbonyl group and give the
   general mechanism of nucleophilic addition. <span class="marks">[4]</span>
   (b) Arrange HCHO, CH₃CHO, CH₃COCH₃ and C₆H₅CHO in order of decreasing
   reactivity towards nucleophilic addition and justify the order.
   <span class="marks">[4]</span>
2. (a) How is benzaldehyde prepared from toluene? Give two methods with
   equations. <span class="marks">[3]</span>
   (b) Write the reactions of benzaldehyde with (i) ethanoic anhydride and sodium
   ethanoate, (ii) alcoholic KCN, (iii) concentrated NaOH, naming each product.
   <span class="marks">[3]</span>
   (c) Why does benzaldehyde undergo meta substitution on nitration?
   <span class="marks">[2]</span>
3. An organic compound **X** of formula C₄H₈O decolourises neither bromine water
   nor KMnO₄, gives an orange solid with 2,4-DNP, gives yellow crystals with
   I₂/NaOH, but does not reduce Tollens' reagent. On reduction with LiAlH₄ it
   gives **Y**, which on heating with concentrated H₂SO₄ gives **Z**, C₄H₈.
   Identify **X**, **Y** and **Z** with reasoning and write all the equations.
   <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Answers to Group C
**1.** (b) HCHO > CH₃CHO > CH₃COCH₃ > C₆H₅CHO. HCHO has no electron-releasing
alkyl group, so its carbonyl carbon carries the largest δ+ and is the least
hindered. Each alkyl group added donates electron density (+I), lowering δ+, and
adds steric bulk. In benzaldehyde the benzene ring donates electron density into
the carbonyl group by resonance, which lowers δ+ further; propanone is therefore
more reactive than benzaldehyde in practice. (Figure of hydration constants:
K = 2.3 × 10³, 1.1 and 2 × 10⁻³ for the first three.)

**2.** (c) —CHO is a −I and −R group. It withdraws electron density from the
ring, and the resonance structures put the positive charge at the *ortho* and
*para* positions. The electrophile therefore attacks the least deactivated
position, the **meta** position.

**3.** No decolourisation of Br₂ water or KMnO₄ ⇒ no C=C. 2,4-DNP positive ⇒
carbonyl group. Tollens' negative ⇒ not an aldehyde ⇒ a ketone. Iodoform
positive ⇒ contains CH₃CO—. The only C₄H₈O ketone is **butanone,
CH₃COCH₂CH₃** = **X**.

CH₃COCH₂CH₃ + 2[H] --LiAlH₄--> CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃, so **Y = butan-2-ol**.

CH₃CH(OH)CH₂CH₃ --conc. H₂SO₄, 443 K--> CH₃CH=CHCH₃ + H₂O, so **Z = but-2-ene**
(the Saytzeff product, C₄H₈).

Also acceptable as supporting evidence: X + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CHI₃ + CH₃CH₂COONa +
3NaI + 3H₂O.
:::
