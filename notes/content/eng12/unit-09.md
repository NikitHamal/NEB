A village minister stands at his door at the end of the day and says a short
prayer out loud. He does not ask for money, or for rain, or for his enemies to
be punished. He asks God to keep an eye on "all poor creatures born to die", to
notice that the people of his town are "not wholly bad or good", and to let them
all have one more day. Then he says good night to the sun — "but just for now".
Sixteen lines, eight rhyming couplets, hardly a word above two syllables. It is
the gentlest poem in your course, and its gentleness is an argument.

::: key What the exam wants from this poem
Who is speaking and from where: the Reverend **Eli Jenkins** in Dylan Thomas's
radio play ***Under Milk Wood***, standing outside his house in the invented
Welsh village of **Llareggub**. The four quatrains built of **rhyming couplets —
aabb ccdd eeff gghh**, every rhyme perfect. The two great lines: "all poor
creatures born to die" and "We are not wholly bad or good". The idea of a God
who is asked to *look kindly*, not to *intervene*. And the last line's
cheerfulness in the face of death: "good-bye – but just for now!"
:::

**Dylan Marlais Thomas** (1914–1953) was born in Swansea, in south Wales, the
son of a grammar-school English master. He left school at sixteen, worked
briefly as a newspaper reporter, and published *18 Poems* at twenty — an
astonishing debut that made him famous almost at once. He was a poet of sound
before he was a poet of sense: he built his lines out of alliteration, internal
rhyme and heavy stress patterns, and he read them aloud in a deep, rolling voice
that became as famous as the poems.

Your textbook rightly calls him "an extension into the 20th century of the
general movement called Romanticism" — his emphasis falls on imagination,
emotion, intuition, spontaneity and organic form rather than on the irony and
learning that dominated English poetry in the 1930s while he was writing. His
best-known poems are "Do not go gentle into that good night" and "Fern Hill".

He began working for the **BBC in 1945**, and radio turned out to suit him
completely: a voice, no scenery, and a national audience. He drank heavily, was
permanently short of money, and died in New York on 9 November 1953, aged
thirty-nine, during an American reading tour.

::: caution Two slips in the textbook blurb to correct in your notes
The blurb prints "nineteenth century **Englnad**" (England), and it says *Under
Milk Wood* is from 1953. Thomas was writing it through 1952–53 and gave its
first public reading in New York on 14 May 1953, but the famous BBC radio
broadcast was on **25 January 1954**, after his death, with Richard Burton
reading First Voice. The book was published in 1954. Also note the title is
*Under Milk Wood*, not "Under **the** Milk Wood", and the village is spelt
**Llareggub**, not "Llaggerub" as the glossary has it.
:::

## 9.2 Where the poem comes from

*Under Milk Wood* is a **radio play** — Thomas subtitled it "A Play for Voices".
There is no plot and there are no stage directions in the ordinary sense.
Instead, two narrators (First Voice and Second Voice) walk the listener through
**one spring day** in a small Welsh seaside town, from the dark before dawn to
the dark after dusk, dipping in and out of the dreams, kitchens, shops and
thoughts of some sixty inhabitants.

The town is called **Llareggub**. Thomas built the name to look like a genuine
Welsh place name; read it backwards and it spells a rude English phrase meaning
"nothing at all". The joke tells you his attitude: he loves the place and refuses
to sentimentalise it. **Milk Wood** is the wood on the hillside above the town,
and the town therefore lives, literally, *under Milk Wood*.

One of the inhabitants is the **Reverend Eli Jenkins**, an elderly,
unthreatening, poetry-writing minister who steps out of his front door twice a
day to recite verses he has composed himself. His morning poem begins "Dear
Gwalia! I know there are / Towns lovelier than ours…" — a hymn of praise to
Wales. The poem you are studying is his **sunset poem**, spoken near the end of
the play as the light goes.

::: note Morning poem or evening poem?
The poem begins "Every morning when I wake", so it is natural to call it a
morning prayer, and your textbook does. But in the play it is Eli Jenkins's
**evening** recitation — his sunset poem. That is not a contradiction. The poem
*describes* what he does every morning and every evening; he *speaks* it at
dusk. If an examiner asks when the speaker prays, the honest answer is: twice a
day, morning and at sun-down, and the poem itself is his evening piece.
:::

The poem has no title of its own in the play. "Every Morning I Wake" is simply
its first line, used as a title when the passage is anthologised.

## 9.3 The poem, stanza by stanza

The poem is in copyright, so it is quoted here in short pieces; keep your
textbook open beside this page.

### Stanza 1 — the morning prayer

> Every morning when I wake,
> Dear Lord, a little prayer I make,

The first thing to notice is the word **little**. Eli Jenkins does not claim to
be a great intercessor. He makes *a little prayer* — modest in length, modest in
ambition, and offered daily rather than in an emergency. The habit matters more
than the eloquence.

**"Dear Lord"** is the second thing. It is how you begin a letter to somebody
you know. There is no "Almighty", no "O Thou", no trembling. The relationship is
affectionate and everyday.

The request, when it comes, is startlingly small: that God should *keep Thy
loving eye* on **"all poor creatures born to die"**. He is not asking God to
*do* anything. He is asking God to *look*, and to look lovingly. And the object
of that look is not the good, or the faithful, or the people of Llareggub — it is
every living thing, on the sole qualification that it is mortal. "Poor" here
means *pitiable*, not *penniless*; "creatures" means everything that has been
created, animals included. In two lines the poem has replaced the idea of a God
who rewards and punishes with the idea of a God who watches with pity.

::: note A textual variant worth knowing
Your textbook prints "O please **do** keep Thy **loving** eye". The published
text of *Under Milk Wood* reads "O please to keep Thy lovely eye". Quote your
textbook's version in the exam — it is the version being examined — but if you
meet the other wording in a library copy, you now know why.
:::

### Stanza 2 — the evening blessing

> And every evening at sun-down
> I ask a blessing on the town,

The stanza is built as a deliberate mirror of the first: *Every morning* becomes
*And every evening*, and the private prayer for all creatures becomes a public
blessing on one small place. The poem's shape is therefore the shape of a day,
and of the minister's own routine, and — because the play is about one day in
one town — of *Under Milk Wood* itself in miniature.

Then comes the poem's frankest couplet. Whether the townspeople survive the
night, he says, is always **"touch-and-go"**. The idiom means *highly uncertain,
could go either way*, and it is a shockingly plain thing for a clergyman to say.
He is not promising his congregation protection. He is telling them, and
himself, that every night is a risk and that faith does not remove the risk.
There is no bargaining in this prayer and no guarantee attached to it.

### Stanza 3 — the plea for a fair hearing

> We are not wholly bad or good,
> Who live our lives under Milk Wood,

This is the most quoted line Thomas ever wrote outside "Do not go gentle", and
the whole play stands behind it. The people of Llareggub include a man who
dreams of poisoning his wife, a woman who scrubs her house all day, a blind sea
captain in love with the drowned, and a girl who loves everybody. None of them
is a saint; none is a villain. Thomas's judgement on humanity is the one his
minister states here: **"not wholly bad or good"**.

The construction is a **litotes** — an understatement made by denying the
opposite. Saying "not wholly bad" is warmer than saying "quite good", because it
admits the badness first. And the rhyme is perfect and quiet: *good* / *Wood*.
The place and the moral verdict chime, as if the town and its imperfection were
the same fact.

The second couplet turns to God and, remarkably, tells Him what He will do:
*And Thou, I know, wilt be the first / To see our best side, not our worst.*
Three points are worth making here.

The verb is **"I know"**, not "I hope" or "I pray" — the one moment of complete
confidence in the poem. What he is confident about is not his own salvation but
God's **generosity**.

**"The first"** is a small, sharp word. Others will see the worst side first —
neighbours, gossips, the town itself. God will be the first to look for the best.
The line contains a quiet criticism of human judgement.

And **"Thou … wilt"** is the archaic second person singular of the old English
Bible and the chapel prayer book. In Welsh nonconformist chapels, as in the
Book of Common Prayer, God is addressed as *Thou* — the intimate, singular form,
the form you would use to a member of your family. Thomas keeps it because Eli
Jenkins would keep it, and because it is affectionate rather than grand.

### Stanza 4 — good night, and good morning

> O let us see another day!
> Bless us all this night, I pray,

The last stanza is the only one that asks for something concrete, and what it
asks for is simply **more time** — one more day, for everybody, without
distinction. The exclamation mark is the loudest sound in the poem.

Then the ending that makes the poem memorable:

> And say, good-bye – but just for now!

The community bows to the setting sun. That is not Christian practice — it is
older than Christianity, the instinctive courtesy of people who live by daylight
— and Thomas lets the minister do it without embarrassment. Then the dash, then
the correction: **"but just for now!"**

That phrase is doing two jobs at once. On the surface it is a fact of astronomy:
the sun will come back in the morning, so the good-bye is temporary. Underneath,
it is the poem's answer to "all poor creatures born to die". If saying good night
to the sun is only a short parting, then perhaps dying is too. The poem never
says so. It lets a cheerful, almost throwaway idiom carry the whole weight of a
hope it is too modest to state.

## 9.4 Paraphrase in plain English

**Stanza 1.** Every morning when I wake up I say a short prayer: dear God,
please keep watching kindly over every living thing, since everything that is
born must die.

**Stanza 2.** And every evening as the sun goes down I ask for a blessing on the
village. Whether or not we get through the night is always, I am sure, a very
close thing.

**Stanza 3.** We who live our lives under Milk Wood are neither completely bad
nor completely good. And You, I am sure, will be the first to look for the best
in us rather than the worst.

**Stanza 4.** Oh, let us live to see another day! Bless us all tonight, I pray.
And all of us will bow to the sun and say goodbye to it — but only for the time
being.

## 9.5 Central idea and themes

**Central idea.** A village minister's daily prayer asks God not for protection
or prosperity but for *mercy in judgement*: that He should look with love on
mortal creatures, notice that ordinary people are mixed rather than wicked, and
grant them one more day. The poem argues that humility and kindness are the
right religious attitude, and that the proper response to death is courtesy
rather than terror.

**Prayer as habit rather than emergency.** *Every* morning, *every* evening. The
poem's authority comes from repetition. This is not a cry from a sickbed; it is
what this man says on ordinary days when nothing is wrong, which is why it can
be trusted.

**Human imperfection.** "We are not wholly bad or good" is the moral centre of
both the poem and the play. Thomas refuses both the sentimental view (village
people are innocent) and the puritanical one (people are sinners). Human beings
are mixed, and a religion worth having must begin there.

**Mortality.** "All poor creatures born to die"; "whether we last the night or
no"; "let us see another day". Death is mentioned or implied in every stanza,
and never with fear. The poem's achievement is to be fully aware of death and
still end on an exclamation mark.

**A merciful God, imagined humbly.** God in this poem has a *loving eye*, is
addressed as *Dear Lord* and *Thou*, and is expected to see the best side. There
is no hell, no judgement seat, no list of sins. The magnificence of God, which
the textbook question asks about, is shown entirely through generosity: He is
great because He is kind, and because the whole of creation is small enough to
fit under one loving glance.

**Community.** Almost every pronoun in the second half is plural: *we*, *our*,
*us*, *us all*. The individual speaker dissolves into the town. He asks a
blessing "on the town", not on himself, and the final bow is made by everybody
together.

**Nature and the daily cycle.** Sunrise, sun-down, night, another day. The poem
is organised by the turning of the earth, and its last gesture is directed at
the sun.

## 9.6 Form, structure, metre and rhyme

**Form.** Sixteen lines, printed as **four quatrains**. It is a **lyric** and,
more precisely, a **prayer poem** in the tradition of the Welsh chapel hymn and
the English metrical psalm.

**Rhyme scheme: aabb ccdd eeff gghh.** Every quatrain is built from **two
rhyming couplets**, and every one of the eight rhymes is **perfect**:

| Couplet | Rhyme words | Letter |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | wake / make | aa |
| 2 | eye / die | bb |
| 3 | sun-down / town | cc |
| 4 | no / touch-and-go | dd |
| 5 | good / Wood | ee |
| 6 | first / worst | ff |
| 7 | day / pray | gg |
| 8 | bow / now | hh |

No rhyme is carried from one quatrain to the next, and no rhyme is slant or
forced. This matters for the meaning: a couplet is the most *settled* rhyme
arrangement in English, because the ear gets what it is waiting for immediately.
Nothing is left hanging. In a poem about uncertainty and death, the form is
continuously reassuring — the sound keeps promising what the sense cannot.

**Metre.** Predominantly **iambic tetrameter** (four beats to the line), the
metre of children's rhymes, ballads and hymns. It is not perfectly regular, and
the departures are audible:

| Line | Syllables | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 7 | opens on a stress: *EV-ery MORN-ing WHEN I WAKE* |
| 2–6 | 8 | steady iambic tetrameter |
| 7 | 9 | "For whether we last the night or no" — the line stretches at exactly the point where the sense becomes least certain |
| 8–13 | 8 | steady |
| 14 | 7 | "Bless us all this night, I pray" — clipped, urgent |
| 15–16 | 8 | steady |

Two of the three irregular lines are the two shortest, and both of them begin on
a strong stress, which makes them sound spoken rather than sung.

**Structure of thought.** The four stanzas form a clean progression:

1. morning — a prayer for *all creatures* (the widest circle)
2. evening — a blessing on *the town* (narrower) and an admission of risk
3. a plea for *fair judgement* of "we … under Milk Wood"
4. a request for *one more day*, and a farewell to the sun

The circle narrows from all creation to one village and then to one night, and
the last line opens it out again to the sun. The whole poem is one turn of the
earth.

## 9.7 Imagery, figures of speech and sound devices

| Device | Where it occurs | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Apostrophe / invocation | "Dear Lord", "O please…", "And Thou, I know…" | the poem is spoken *to* God; it is an address, not a description |
| Archaic diction | "Thy", "Thou", "wilt" | the intimate singular of the old Bible and the chapel prayer book; affectionate, not grand |
| Metonymy / synecdoche | "Thy loving eye" | God reduced to a single kindly glance — no throne, no thunder |
| Periphrasis | "all poor creatures born to die" | a phrase used instead of the word *mortals*; it makes us pity what it names |
| Parallelism | "Every morning …" / "And every evening …" | the two halves of the day balanced against each other |
| Idiom / colloquialism | "touch-and-go" | ordinary conversational English admitted into a prayer |
| Litotes (understatement) | "We are not wholly bad or good" | denies the extreme instead of asserting the middle; warmer and more honest |
| Antithesis | "bad or good"; "our best side, not our worst"; "last the night or no" | the poem thinks in balanced opposites |
| Personification | "we all will bow / And say, good-bye" to the sun | the sun treated as a departing person owed a courtesy |
| Anticlimax / bathos (used deliberately) | "good-bye – but just for now!" | the grand farewell is immediately made cheerful and small |
| Symbol | Milk Wood; the sun | the wood is the whole community's world; the sun is life and its daily withdrawal |
| Alliteration | "**l**ittle prayer"; "**l**ive our **l**ives"; "**b**less … **b**orn … **b**ow"; "**s**ure … **s**un" | a soft, sung surface, close to Welsh chapel singing |
| Assonance | "b**o**rn to d**ie**"; "t**ou**ch-and-g**o**"; "b**ow** … n**ow**" | binds the couplets aurally beyond the rhyme |
| Rhyming couplet | throughout | see §9.6: every line is immediately answered — formal reassurance |
| Exclamation | "O let us see another day!"; "but just for now!" | the two moments when feeling breaks through the calm |
| Dash | "good-bye – but just for now" | a pause, then a correction: the poem's hope arrives as an afterthought |

## 9.8 Tone

The tone is **humble, affectionate, conversational and quietly brave**.

It is humble because of the scale of the requests: a little prayer, a loving
eye, a fair hearing, one more day. It is affectionate because of the diction:
*Dear Lord*, *Thou*, *us all*. It is conversational because of "touch-and-go",
which no formal liturgy would allow. And it is brave because the poem states
twice that everyone in it is going to die and never once flinches.

There is also a thread of **gentle humour**, which comes from the play. Eli
Jenkins is a slightly comic figure — the village poet who recites to nobody in
particular — and the last line's bounce keeps him human rather than saintly.
Do not mistake the humour for irony: Thomas is not mocking him. The poem asks
you to find him funny and to agree with him at the same time.

## 9.9 Critical appreciation

The strength of "Every Morning I Wake" is that it earns an enormous claim with
tiny means.

The claim is that the right relationship between a human being and God is
neither fear nor bargaining but **mutual kindness** — we look at each other
generously. The means are sixteen lines of eight-syllable verse, eight nursery
rhymes' worth of couplet, and a vocabulary a ten-year-old could read. Thomas,
whose own poetry is usually dense with compound words and half-buried images,
writes here in a voice that is not his own. It is Eli Jenkins's voice, and it is
plainer than anything Thomas wrote under his own name. This is the discipline of
**dramatic writing**: the poem is in character.

The most interesting decision in the poem is what it leaves out. There is no
mention of sin, of hell, of the Cross, of church or chapel, of scripture, or of
any doctrine whatever. A Welsh minister of the 1950s could have been expected to
supply all of these. Instead Thomas gives him an eye, a town, a night and the
sun. The result is a prayer that a non-believer can say without dishonesty,
which may be why it has outlived the play it came from and is read at funerals
and school assemblies by people who have never heard of Llareggub.

Set against that, a critic could object that the poem is **too comfortable**.
"We are not wholly bad or good" is a generous verdict, but it is also a
convenient one; it excuses everybody. *Under Milk Wood* has been accused of
exactly this — of turning a real, poor, narrow Welsh town into a charming place
where nothing serious is at stake. The counter-argument is line 7: "whether we
last the night or no / I'm sure is always touch-and-go". A poem that says that
is not comfortable. It has simply decided that the correct response to danger is
courtesy.

Finally, the poem's **form is its theology**. Perfect couplets in a poem about
uncertainty say, at the level of sound, what the speaker cannot say at the level
of statement: that things come out even, that nothing is left unpaired, that the
morning follows the night. He says good-bye; the rhyme says *just for now*.

## Chapter summary

"Every Morning I Wake" is the sunset poem of the Reverend Eli Jenkins in Dylan
Thomas's radio play *Under Milk Wood*, a play for voices covering one spring day
in the invented Welsh village of Llareggub, first read publicly in New York in
May 1953 and first broadcast by the BBC in January 1954, after Thomas's death at
thirty-nine. Its sixteen lines are four quatrains made of eight perfect rhyming
couplets — aabb ccdd eeff gghh — in loose iambic tetrameter. Stanza by stanza it
moves through a day: a morning prayer that God should keep a loving eye on "all
poor creatures born to die"; an evening blessing on the town, with the frank
admission that survival is "touch-and-go"; a plea to be judged fairly, since "We
are not wholly bad or good / Who live our lives under Milk Wood"; and a final
request for "another day", ending with the whole community bowing to the setting
sun and saying "good-bye – but just for now!" The poem asks for mercy rather
than miracles, speaks for a community rather than a self, faces death without
fear, and lets its unbroken couplets supply the reassurance its words are too
honest to promise.

## Practice questions

### Understanding the text

**a. When does the speaker pray to the Lord?** <span class="marks">[2]</span>

Twice every day, as a settled habit. Every morning when he wakes he makes "a
little prayer", and every evening at sun-down he asks a blessing on the town. In
the last stanza he also prays at nightfall for the night ahead. The repetition
of "every" is the point: this is not an emergency prayer but the ordinary rhythm
of his life.

**b. What does the speaker pray for?** <span class="marks">[2]</span>

For remarkably little, and never for himself alone. He asks God to keep a loving
eye on all mortal creatures; to bless the town; to judge the people of Milk Wood
by their best side rather than their worst; and to let them all live to see
another day, and be kept safe tonight. He asks for mercy, attention and time —
not for wealth, success, or the punishment of enemies.

**c. Who are the 'poor creatures'? Why does the speaker call them 'poor
creatures'?** <span class="marks">[2]</span>

The "poor creatures" are all living things — the people of Llareggub and, since
"creatures" means everything created, the animals and birds too. He calls them
*poor* not because they lack money but because they are pitiable: the phrase is
completed by "born to die", so their poverty is their mortality. Everything that
is alive is under sentence from the moment of its birth, and that shared
helplessness is what makes them deserve compassion — the speaker's and God's.

**d. What does Milk Wood sound like? A type of wood or a place? Why?**
<span class="marks">[2]</span>

It sounds like both, and Thomas intends the ambiguity. Because it is capitalised
and preceded by "under", it works as a **place name** — the wood on the hill
above the village, and by extension the little world the villagers live in ("who
live our lives under Milk Wood" means, in effect, "who live here"). But the
words themselves, *milk* and *wood*, are the names of a substance and a
material, so on first hearing it also sounds like a **kind of timber**, soft,
pale and gentle. The milkiness colours the whole poem: it suggests mildness,
nourishment, and something childlike about the people being prayed for.

**e. Why do the inhabitants of Milk Wood bow to the setting sun 'but just for
now'?** <span class="marks">[2]</span>

Because the parting is temporary. They bow as a mark of respect and farewell at
sun-down, but they know the sun will rise again in the morning, so the goodbye
is only "for now" rather than forever. The phrase also carries a second,
unstated meaning: since the poem has just prayed for "poor creatures born to
die", saying that a nightly goodbye is only temporary quietly suggests that
death, too, may be a parting rather than an ending. The speaker never claims
this outright; he lets a cheerful everyday phrase imply it.

### Reference to the context

**a. Discuss "Every Morning When I Wake" as a prayer to the God.**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

The poem is a prayer in the strict formal sense and in a very unusual spirit.

**Formally**, it has everything a prayer needs. It is *addressed*: "Dear Lord",
"O please", "And Thou". It uses the traditional language of devotion — the
archaic *Thy*, *Thou*, *wilt* of the old Bible and the chapel prayer book. It
contains petitions: keep Thy loving eye on all creatures; bless the town; bless
us all this night; let us see another day. It is spoken at the two canonical
hours of private devotion, morning and evening. And it is uttered by a
clergyman, the Reverend Eli Jenkins of *Under Milk Wood*, as part of his daily
practice.

**In spirit**, however, it is unlike most prayers you will have met. Three
things mark it out.

*It asks for almost nothing.* The main request of stanza 1 is not that God should
*act* but that He should *look* — "keep Thy loving eye". A prayer for attention
rather than intervention is a very humble prayer.

*It is honest about its own limits.* "For whether we last the night or no / I'm
sure is always touch-and-go." The speaker prays and simultaneously admits that
praying may not save anybody. There is no bargain here, no promise of good
behaviour in return for protection.

*It speaks for a community, not a self.* After the first two lines the pronouns
become plural and stay plural: *the town*, *we*, *our*, *us all*. Eli Jenkins is
praying as a representative, and the final bow is made by everyone together.

What the poem finally asks for is **mercy in judgement**. "We are not wholly bad
or good" is a confession and a defence in one line, and the answer to it is the
poem's one moment of certainty: "And Thou, I know, wilt be the first / To see
our best side, not our worst." The prayer's argument, in short, is that God is
kinder than people are — and it ends not with amen but with an entire village
bowing to the sunset and saying good night.

**b. Why does the speaker make a prayer to the God, but not to a king, a
billionaire or a scientist?** <span class="marks">[5]</span>

Because of *what* he is asking for. Look carefully at the four requests and ask
who could grant them.

He asks that somebody should keep **"Thy loving eye"** on "all poor creatures
born to die". No king rules all creatures, and no king can watch them all at
once; the request needs a being who is everywhere and who loves what He sees. A
monarch's attention is partial by definition — it falls on his own subjects, and
usually on the important ones.

He asks to be seen at his **"best side, not our worst"**. That is a plea for a
just and generous *judge*. A billionaire has no standing to judge anybody's
moral worth, and money is precisely the thing this poem never mentions: nothing
in the prayer can be bought. Eli Jenkins does not ask for a better harvest, a
new chapel roof or relief from poverty, all of which a rich man could supply.

He asks whether **"we last the night or no"**, which he calls touch-and-go, and
he asks for **"another day"**. A scientist can lengthen a life but cannot
guarantee a night, and cannot do anything at all about the fact that creatures
are "born to die". Medicine postpones; it does not answer. The question the poem
is really asking — *is our mortality watched over by anything that cares?* — is
not a question science is built to take.

There is a further, human reason. Kings, billionaires and scientists are all
**powerful people**, and the whole temper of this prayer is a rejection of
power-relations. The speaker approaches God as "Dear Lord", the way you would
open a letter to a relative, and expects kindness rather than patronage. You
cannot speak to a king like that. Thomas's minister wants a listener who is
great enough to see everything and intimate enough to be addressed as *Thou* —
and only God, as this poem imagines Him, is both.

**c. How does the poet highlight the magnificence of the God?**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

Not by describing Him. There is no throne in this poem, no light, no angels, no
thunder and no judgement seat. Thomas's method is the opposite of the usual one:
he makes God magnificent by making Him **generous, attentive and near**.

*By scale of attention.* "All poor creatures born to die" is the largest phrase
in the poem — it takes in every living thing that has ever existed — and it is
governed by a single organ, "Thy loving eye". One glance covers the whole of
mortal creation. The magnificence lies in the ratio between the enormous object
and the effortless means.

*By moral generosity.* "Thou … wilt be the first / To see our best side, not our
worst." God is great because He is the most charitable reader of human beings in
existence — better than neighbours, better than the villagers are to each other,
better than they are to themselves. Notice that this is the only statement in
the poem the speaker introduces with "I know". Of everything in heaven and
earth, God's kindness is the one thing he is sure of.

*By the reverence of the language.* The archaic *Thy*, *Thou*, *wilt* and the
capitalised pronoun place God in the register of the Bible; "O please" and "Dear
Lord" place Him within speaking distance. Holding both at once is itself a way
of saying that He is both above us and beside us.

*By what is left for Him to do.* Every other authority in the poem is absent.
There is no mention of church, doctrine, sin or hell — only a man at a door, a
town, a night and the sun. Into that emptiness God is invited simply to *look
kindly*, and that turns out to be the largest thing anyone in the poem can
imagine.

*By contrast with human smallness.* "A little prayer", "poor creatures", "we are
not wholly bad or good", "just for now" — everything on the human side of the
poem is diminutive and provisional. Against that steady smallness, a being who
can hold the whole of it in one loving eye is made to seem immense without a
single grand adjective being used.

**d. How does the rhyme scheme of the poem reinforce its message?**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

The poem is written in **rhyming couplets** — aabb ccdd eeff gghh — arranged in
four quatrains, and every one of the eight rhymes is perfect: *wake / make*,
*eye / die*, *sun-down / town*, *no / touch-and-go*, *good / Wood*, *first /
worst*, *day / pray*, *bow / now*. Four features of that scheme do real work.

**Nothing is left unpaired.** In a couplet the ear gets its answer at once, in
the very next line. The poem's subject is a community — "we", "our", "us all" —
and its central anxiety is being left alone in the dark. A form in which every
line immediately finds its partner enacts the togetherness the prayer asks for.

**The form is reassuring where the sense is not.** The content is frankly
frightening: creatures are "born to die" and survival is "touch-and-go". But the
sound never falters. There are no half rhymes, no failed expectations, no line
left hanging. The rhyme keeps promising order underneath a statement of
disorder, which is exactly what faith does. The scheme *is* the speaker's
confidence.

**It is the sound of a hymn, and of a nursery rhyme.** Couplets in eight
syllables are the metre of the chapel, of children's verse and of folk song —
the poetry of ordinary people rather than of scholars. That is the right music
for a village minister speaking on behalf of shopkeepers and fishermen, and it
keeps the prayer from sounding superior to the people it is about.

**Each quatrain closes cleanly, and so does each idea.** Because the rhymes do
not run on between stanzas, every four lines form a complete, self-contained
thought — morning, evening, judgement, night. The poem advances in four calm
steps, like the four stages of a day, and comes to rest at the end of each.
When the very last couplet closes on *bow / now*, the sound of finality arrives
on the word "now", which means *not final at all*. The scheme has one last joke
in it, and it is a consoling one.

### Reference beyond the text

**a. Does the God exist? Give your opinion.** <span class="marks">[5]</span>

*This question asks for your own view. What earns marks is not which side you
take but whether you take one clearly, support it with reasons, and treat the
other side fairly. Below is one fully worked answer; write yours in your own
voice.*

I do not think the question can be settled by evidence, and I think that is the
most honest place to begin. Nothing we can measure would count decisively for or
against the existence of a being who is, by definition, outside the system we
measure. So the real question becomes a different one: is belief in God
reasonable, and is it good?

The strongest argument for God's existence is the one from **order and
existence itself** — that the universe is here at all, that it is intelligible,
and that human beings possess a moral sense that is difficult to reduce entirely
to self-interest. The strongest argument against is the **problem of suffering**:
if God is both all-powerful and all-loving, the earthquake that killed nine
thousand people in Nepal in 2015 is very hard to account for. Neither argument
defeats the other. Believers answer the second with free will and with the
limits of human understanding; unbelievers answer the first by pointing out that
a universe which produced us would look designed to us whether it was or not.

What Eli Jenkins's prayer suggests to me is that this may be the wrong argument
to spend a life on. His God is not a hypothesis about the origin of matter; He
is a way of holding two facts together — that we are "born to die", and that we
still deserve to be looked at with love. Whether or not such a being exists, a
person who prays that prayer every morning will treat his neighbours better than
a person who does not.

My own position is that I cannot know, and that I choose to live as though the
loving eye is there. It costs me nothing I value, it makes me less willing to
see the worst side of people, and it makes death easier to look at. If I am
wrong, I will have been kind for no reason, which is not a serious loss.

**b. In his Epistle to the author of the book *The Three Impostors* (1768),
Voltaire says, "Even if the God didn't exist, it would be necessary to invent
him." Write an essay highlighting the importance of the God in the society.**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

*Plan first. This essay needs an introduction that explains Voltaire's sentence,
three or four body paragraphs each with one function of religion in society and
a concrete example, a paragraph acknowledging the other side, and a conclusion
that takes a position. Aim for 400–450 words. The model below is compressed;
expand each paragraph with examples from Nepal.*

**Introduction.** Voltaire was not a believer in any conventional sense — he
attacked the Church all his life — which is what makes his famous line so
interesting. *Si Dieu n'existait pas, il faudrait l'inventer.* He is not arguing
that God is real. He is arguing that the *idea* of God performs work in a
society that nothing else has yet performed as well, and that a society which
abolished it would have to build a replacement.

**God as the guarantee of morality when nobody is watching.** Laws restrain
people who fear being caught. The idea of a being who sees everything restrains
people who know they will not be caught. Voltaire's own version of the argument
was blunt: he wanted his lawyers and his servants to believe in God. In Eli
Jenkins's poem the same idea appears in its gentle form — the assumption that we
are being watched, lovingly, and will one day be judged by our best side.

**God as the source of social solidarity.** Religion assembles people who
otherwise have no reason to meet. A temple, a mosque, a gumba or a chapel is a
place where the rich and the poor of a locality stand in the same room. In Nepal,
festivals such as Dashain and Tihar, or the community feasts attached to a
*guthi*, do more to hold a neighbourhood together than any civic institution.
Sociologists from Émile Durkheim onward have argued that what such rituals
worship is partly the community itself.

**God as consolation in the face of death and injustice.** This is the function
the poem is most interested in. Human beings are "poor creatures born to die",
and every society must give its members some way of bearing that. The belief
that suffering is seen, and that the accounts are settled somewhere, is what
allows grief to be survivable and allows the powerless to keep their dignity.

**God as the source of meaning and of art.** Much of the world's greatest
architecture, music, painting and poetry exists because somebody was trying to
praise God — the Pashupatinath temple and the Boudhanath stupa as much as the
cathedrals of Europe. The idea has been extraordinarily productive.

**The other side, stated fairly.** Religion has also justified caste, the
subjugation of women, the burning of heretics and, in our own region, communal
violence. Voltaire knew this better than anyone; he spent his life fighting
religious persecution. And modern secular societies such as Denmark and Japan
manage high levels of honesty and social trust with very low levels of belief,
which suggests the "necessary invention" can be replaced by education, law and
prosperity.

**Conclusion.** The honest conclusion is the one Voltaire's sentence itself
implies: what societies need is not necessarily God, but the things belief in
God supplies — a shared morality, a reason to be good unobserved, a way of
meeting our neighbours, and a bearable answer to death. Where religion supplies
those things kindly, as it does in Eli Jenkins's prayer, it remains one of the
most valuable institutions we have. Where it supplies them cruelly, the
invention has failed, and Voltaire would have been the first to say so.
