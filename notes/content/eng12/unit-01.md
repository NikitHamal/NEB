"Neighbours" is a very short story by the Australian writer Tim Winton. A young,
newly married couple move into an inner-city street full of European migrants,
find everything about it strange, and then — over the course of one year and one
pregnancy — stop being strangers themselves. Almost nothing dramatic happens: a
vegetable patch is dug, a henhouse falls down, ducks are killed, a baby is born.
The whole story is about the distance between people and how it quietly closes,
and it opens the Grade 12 course because it teaches you to read a text in which
the meaning is carried by small actions rather than by speeches.

::: key The central idea
Language is not the same thing as understanding. The couple never learn a word
of Macedonian or Polish, and the neighbours never learn to explain themselves;
yet within a year they are one community, because they have dug, cooked,
butchered, waited and rejoiced together. Winton's point is that human warmth
crosses barriers that words cannot.
:::

## 1.1 About the author

Timothy John Winton (born 1960) is an Australian novelist and short-story writer,
born in Perth and brought up in Perth and in the small coastal town of Albany in
Western Australia. He began publishing extraordinarily young. He wrote his first
novel, *An Open Swimmer*, while still an undergraduate; it won The Australian /
Vogel Literary Award for an unpublished manuscript and appeared in print in 1982,
when he was twenty-two. The prize made him a professional writer at an age when
most writers are still students, and he has written full time ever since.

```

Winton has won the **Miles Franklin Literary Award**, Australia's leading prize
for fiction, four times: for *Shallows* (1984), *Cloudstreet* (1991), *Dirt
Music* (2001) and *Breath* (2008). *Cloudstreet*, a big, warm novel about two
poor families sharing one rambling house in Perth, is often voted Australia's
best-loved novel. Two of his books, *The Riders* (1994) and *Dirt Music*, were
shortlisted for the Booker Prize. He has also written a great deal for younger
readers — *That Eye, the Sky* (1986), *Lockie Leonard, Human Torpedo* (1990),
*The Bugalugs Bum Thief* (1991) and *The Deep* (1998). Away from his desk he is
a well-known environmental campaigner, closely associated with the successful
campaign to protect Western Australia's Ningaloo Reef.

Three things in Winton's style matter for this story. First, he writes about
**ordinary, unliterary people** — the kind of Australians who rarely appeared in
serious fiction before him. Second, his prose is **plain, short and physical**:
he gives you smells, sounds and actions, and almost never tells you what a
character feels. Third, **place is a character** in his work; the street, the
weather and the season do much of the emotional work that dialogue does in other
writers. All three are visible in "Neighbours", a story of barely twelve hundred
words in which nobody makes a speech and nobody has a name.

::: caution Where the story comes from
The Nepali textbook's headnote says that "Neighbours" is taken from *Migrants of
Australia*, edited by Harwood Lawler. That book and editor could not be verified
in any library catalogue while these notes were being written. What is certain is
that "Neighbours" is one of the stories in Winton's first collection of short
fiction, ***Scission*** (1985), and that is the safer thing to say in an
examination. Either way, the date places the story in the 1980s, when Australia
had officially adopted **multiculturalism** and post-war migrant families from
southern and eastern Europe were long settled in the inner suburbs of its cities.
:::

## 1.2 The story

**Arrival and unease.** When they first move in, the young couple are "wary of
the neighbourhood". The street is full of European migrants and the newly-weds
feel "like sojourners in a foreign land". On the left lives a Macedonian family;
on the right, a widower from Poland. Their own house is small, but its high
ceilings and paned windows give it the feel of an elegant cottage, and from his
study window the young man can see out over the rooftops and used car yards to
the Moreton Bay figs in the park where they walk their dog — a docile, moulting
collie the neighbours regard with caution.

**A year of small offences.** The couple have grown up in the "expansive outer
suburbs where good neighbours were seldom seen and never heard", so an
inner-city street is a shock: spitting, washing, watering at daybreak. The
Macedonians "shouted, ranted, screamed", and it takes the newcomers six months to
realise that the family are not murdering each other but merely talking. The
Polish widower spends his days hammering nails into wood and pulling them out
again; his yard fills with salvaged lumber which he adds to but never builds
with. The disapproval runs both ways. The Macedonians raise eyebrows at the late
hour at which the newcomers get up, and the young man senses their judgement of a
household in which the wife goes out to work while the husband stays at home to
write a thesis. He for his part watches with disgust as the little boy next door
urinates in the street and sprays the cat from the back step, and is made nervous
by the shaved-headed child standing at the fence with "only his cobalt eyes
showing".

**Autumn: the garden.** The thaw begins over vegetables. The couple clear the
rubbish from their backyard and turn and manure the soil "under the open and
measured gaze of the neighbours", planting leeks, onions, cabbage, Brussels
sprouts and broad beans. The garden brings the neighbours to the fence with
advice about spacing, hilling and mulching. The young man resents the
interference — but, tellingly, "he took careful note of what was said". His wife
is bolder: she runs a hand over the child's stubble, and "the big woman with
black eyes and butcher's arms" gives her a bagful of garlic cloves to plant.

**The henhouse.** The couple build a henhouse and the neighbours watch it fall
down. The Polish widower then slides through the fence uninvited and rebuilds it
for them. Winton adds one flat sentence which is the heart of the whole story:
"They could not understand a word he said."

**Winter: exchange.** As autumn merges into winter, with vermilion sunsets and
sudden dark dusks smelling of wood smoke, the couple find themselves smiling
back. They give away heads of cabbage and accept gifts of grappa and firewood.
The young man works at his thesis on the development of the twentieth century
novel, cooks the dinners and listens to his wife's stories of eccentric patients
and hospital incompetence. In the street they no longer walk with their eyes
lowered — and they feel "superior and proud" when their own parents visit and
cast shocked glances across the fence.

**Spring: the ducks.** They keep big, silent muscovy ducks through the winter,
and in the spring the Macedonian family teach them to slaughter, pluck and dress
the birds. Everyone sits round on blocks and upturned buckets telling "barely
understood stories", the men butchering and the women plucking as custom demands.
"In the haze of down and steam and fractured dialogue", the couple feel
intoxicated. The cat toys with severed heads, the child pulls the cat's tail, and
the newcomers find themselves shouting — that is, behaving exactly like the
neighbours whose noise had appalled them six months before.

**The pregnancy.** The pregnancy is unplanned and stuns them; their friends do
not have children until years after marriage, if at all. Tension returns for a
while. The Polish widower at last begins to build with his hoarded wood, sinking
posts and pouring cement at dawn; the couple curse him behind his back and the
husband suspects him of deliberate antagonism. His wife is sick in the mornings;
his hay fever wears him down. But the street takes the pregnancy over. The deli
man presses chocolates on her and cigarettes on him, which he stores at home, not
being a smoker. Italian women offer names. Greek women stop her in the street,
pull her skirt up and feel her belly and tell her it is bound to be a boy. The
woman next door knits a suit complete with booties and beanie. The young woman
feels, in Winton's beautifully unsorted list, "flattered, claustrophobic,
grateful, peeved". And when the young man is on the point of complaining about
the noise of the almost-finished two-car garage — built by a man with no car —
the Polish widower comes over with a barrowful of wood scraps for their fire.

**The birth.** Labour comes abruptly. The young man abandons the twentieth
century novel for the telephone; the midwife arrives; he runs about "making
statements that sounded like queries" while his wife blacks the stove and
supervises him. Going outside for more wood in the last light of the day, he sees
faces at every fence — he counts twelve — and the Macedonians wave and call out
what sounds like their best wishes. The night passes in contractions, ice,
liverwurst and the midwife's crooning. Then the pushing: his wife bears down in a
squat until the floor trembles, and he feels "the power of her, the
sophistication of her". A boy is born and goes straight to the breast, trailing
cord and vernix, with one eye on his father.

**The last line.** The baby loses the nipple and cries. The young man hears
shouting outside, goes to the back door, and sees on the Macedonian side of the
fence "a small queue of bleary faces" looking up and cheering. He begins to weep.
"The twentieth century novel had not prepared him for this."

## 1.3 Characters

| Character | Who they are | Role in the story |
|---|---|---|
| The young man | A postgraduate student writing a thesis on the twentieth century novel; stays at home, cooks, keeps the garden | Protagonist and centre of consciousness; the one whose prejudice we watch dissolve |
| The young woman | His wife; works at a hospital and tells stories of eccentric patients; later pregnant | Braver and quicker than her husband; makes the first physical gesture of friendship |
| The Macedonian family | The household on the left — loud, large, watchful; includes the shaven-headed little boy and the big woman with "butcher's arms" | The street's warmth and its noise; they teach the couple gardening and butchery and cheer the birth |
| The Polish widower | The old man on the right, alone, endlessly hammering and unhammering nails | Gives most and receives least; his wordless help is the story's purest act of neighbourliness |
| The little boy | The Macedonians' son; urinates in the street, sprays the cat, watches through the fence | A test of the young man's tolerance; the first neighbour the young woman touches |
| The midwife | Attends the birth at home | Marks the birth as a domestic, communal event rather than a medical one |
| The couple's parents | Visit and "cast shocked glances across the fence" | Show how far the couple have already travelled; the prejudice they have left behind |

**The young man.** He is intelligent, well-meaning and quietly snobbish, and
Winton is merciless about it without ever saying so. Look at his verbs in the
first half: he *senses* disapproval, *watches in disgust*, *resents* the
interference, *assumes* why the child's head is shaved, *suspects* the widower of
antagonising him on purpose. He is a reader of novels, and he treats the street
as a text to be interpreted at a distance. Yet the same paragraph that records
his resentment records that "he took careful note of what was said" — the crack
through which the street gets in. His pride is exposed in one devastating
sentence: he and his wife feel "superior and proud" when their parents are
shocked by the neighbours, which means that even his tolerance is, at that stage,
a way of feeling better than somebody. Only at the very end, weeping at the back
door, is he stripped of commentary altogether.

**The young woman.** She has less page-space and more courage. She is the one who
runs a hand over the child's stubble — a small, physical, unasked-for act of
affection that instantly earns a bag of garlic cloves. She is also the one who
does the hardest work in the story, and Winton's account of the birth deliberately
transfers all the heroic vocabulary to her: she is "assaulting some unseen
barrier" until "it was smashed and she was through". Her own feelings about the
neighbourhood's attention are honestly mixed — "flattered, claustrophobic,
grateful, peeved" — which keeps her human rather than saintly.

**The Polish widower.** The most moving figure in the story and the one we know
least. He is old, alone, and occupies himself with work that produces nothing:
nails in, nails out; a pile of lumber added to but never used. When the couple's
henhouse collapses he does not ask permission — he "slid through the fence
uninvited" and simply rebuilds it, and they cannot understand a single word he
says. Note the arc of his hoarded wood. He finally begins to build with it in the
spring, and the noise makes the couple curse him; what he is building turns out
to be a two-car garage for a man with no car, which is absurd and rather sad; and
the offcuts of that absurd project arrive at their door as firewood at the exact
moment the young man is composing a complaint. He is Winton's argument in one
person: neighbourliness is not a feeling you have, it is something you carry
through a fence.

**The Macedonian family.** They begin as noise and end as a choir. Everything the
couple first dislike about them — loudness, bluntness, bodily frankness, no
respect for privacy — turns out to be the same quality as their generosity. The
duck-killing scene is the turning point, because there the couple do not merely
receive help but *join in*, sitting on upturned buckets, telling half-understood
stories and finally shouting themselves. By the last page the Macedonians are the
people standing at the fence at dawn, cheering a baby that is not theirs.

## 1.4 Themes

**Community across language.** The story's central claim is that people can
belong to each other without a shared language. Not one line of dialogue is
exchanged across the fences in the whole story; what crosses is garlic, cabbage,
advice, grappa, firewood, labour, a knitted suit and finally a cheer. Winton
underlines this by making the two most generous acts completely wordless — the
rebuilt henhouse ("they could not understand a word he said") and the barrowful
of wood scraps.

::: definition Multiculturalism
The presence, and the deliberate public acceptance, of several distinct cultures
within one society, in place of an expectation that minorities should abandon
their culture and become indistinguishable from the majority. Australia adopted
multiculturalism as official policy in the 1970s, and Winton's street — with
Macedonian, Polish, Italian and Greek families in it — is what that policy looked
like on the ground.
:::

**Prejudice and its slow correction.** The couple's prejudice is not hatred; it
is the mild, educated, condescending kind, which is much harder to see in oneself.
Winton corrects it not with an argument but with time and proximity. Notice that
he is even-handed: the migrants judge the couple too, for rising late and for
letting the wife earn the money.

**Book learning against lived experience.** The young man is writing a thesis on
the twentieth century novel, a form famous for irony, alienation and the lonely
inner life. The story keeps setting that project against what is happening
outside his study window, and the last line settles the contest: the novel "had
not prepared him for this."

**Birth, the body and the shared event.** The final third of the story is
insistently physical — vomiting, ice, liverwurst, sweat, a squat, cord and vernix.
Winton's point is that the great human events are of the body and cannot be
experienced privately. The birth of one baby is attended by a midwife, a father
and, across two fences, a whole street.

**Belonging and the outsider.** Everyone in the street was once a newcomer. The
couple's journey from "sojourners in a foreign land" to weeping members of the
neighbourhood is, in miniature, the migrant experience itself — which is why the
story can be read as being as much about the Macedonians and the Pole as about
the Australians.

## 1.5 Setting, structure and point of view

**Setting.** An unnamed inner suburb of an Australian city — the Moreton Bay figs
and the street of post-war European migrants suggest Perth or Sydney — some time
in the late twentieth century. The setting is not background: the smallness of the
houses, the shared fences and the backyards big enough for a vegetable patch, a
henhouse and ducks are the physical conditions that force these people into each
other's lives. Winton contrasts it explicitly with the "expansive outer suburbs
where good neighbours were seldom seen and never heard" — the space that buys
privacy and sells loneliness.

**Structure.** The story has no chapters and no scene-breaks, but it is very
tightly organised by the **seasons**: the couple arrive, then autumn brings the
garden, winter the exchange of gifts, spring the ducks and the pregnancy, summer
the neighbourhood's celebration, and the year closes with a birth. The vegetable
cycle and the human one run in parallel; things planted in autumn and a child
conceived in autumn come to fruition together. This is why the story feels
complete despite having almost no plot.

**Point of view.** Third person, past tense, but limited almost entirely to the
young man — a technique called **free indirect style**, in which the narrator's
words take on the character's attitudes without quotation marks. When we read
that the little boy's head "was shaved regularly, he assumed, in order to make his
hair grow thick", the guess is the young man's, not the narrator's, and the small
phrase "he assumed" quietly warns us that he is inventing explanations for people
he does not know. Because we see the street through his eyes, our prejudice is
corrected at the same speed as his.

**Namelessness.** Nobody in the story is named. There is "the young man", "the
young woman", "the Macedonian family", "the Polish widower", "the little boy". A
name is something you learn from somebody who can tell it to you; across these
fences no names can be exchanged, so the narration honestly withholds them. The
effect is to make the story representative — this is any young couple, in any
migrant street, anywhere — and it gives the closing scene its force, because these
strangers behave like family without ever having been introduced.

## 1.6 Style and literary devices

| Device | Example from the story | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Understatement | "They could not understand a word he said." | Placed straight after the widower rebuilds their henhouse; the flatness makes the kindness enormous |
| Irony | The last line: "The twentieth century novel had not prepared him for this." | The man who studies modern literature is educated by a street he once looked down on |
| Free indirect style | "The child's head was shaved regularly, he assumed, in order to make his hair grow thick." | Lets us hear the young man's prejudice in the narrator's voice, and to judge it |
| Sensory imagery | "vermilion sunsets", "the smell of wood smoke", "the haze of down and steam" | Builds the street physically instead of describing emotion |
| Catalogue / list | "leeks, onions, cabbage, Brussels sprouts and broad beans"; "flattered, claustrophobic, grateful, peeved" | Lists of things suggest abundance; the list of feelings admits that emotion is mixed |
| Symbolism | The fence; the Polish widower's hoarded lumber; the vegetable garden | The barrier that everything must cross; a life of unused effort; growth through shared labour |
| Metonymy | "Italian women", "Greek women", "twelve faces" | People are known by group and by body part before they are known as individuals |
| Motif of sound | Shouting mistaken for murder; roosters; the crooning midwife; the final cheering | The story is organised by noise — the thing the couple first hate and finally join |
| Structural parallel | The garden planted in autumn; the child conceived in autumn | Both come to harvest at the same moment |
| Short simple sentences | "Labour came abruptly." "A boy, she said." | Speed and plainness at the emotional peak; Winton refuses to decorate |

::: tip Reading the story in the exam
If you are asked what happens in "Neighbours", resist the urge to say "nothing".
Say instead that the plot is a change of *relationship*, and prove it with the
sequence of objects that cross the fence: advice, garlic, a rebuilt henhouse,
cabbage, grappa, a knitted suit, a barrow of firewood, a cheer. Examiners reward
the candidate who can show the movement with evidence rather than the one who
only asserts that the couple "became friendly".

## 1.8 Textbook exercises

**Understanding the text**

*a. Describe how the young couple's house looked like.*

The house was small, but it did not feel mean: its high ceilings and paned
windows gave it "the feel of an elegant cottage". It stood in a crowded inner
street between a Macedonian family on the left and a Polish widower on the right.
From the young man's study window one could look out over the rooftops and the
used car yards to the Moreton Bay figs in the park where the couple walked their
collie. Behind it lay a backyard which was full of rubbish when they arrived and
which they later cleared, manured and planted as a vegetable garden, and in which
they built a henhouse and kept muscovy ducks.

*b. How did the young couple identify their neighbours in the beginning of their
arrival?*

Not by name — nobody in the story has one — but by **nationality and by noise**.
They knew only that "next door on the left lived a Macedonian family" and "on the
right, a widower from Poland". Beyond that they identified them by the sounds and
habits that reached them over the fence: spitting, washing and watering at
daybreak; a family that "shouted, ranted, screamed" so alarmingly that it took six
months to understand they were merely talking; and an old man who spent his days
hammering nails into wood and pulling them out again beside a growing stack of
salvaged lumber. In other words they identified their neighbours as **types and
noises**, which is exactly how strangers are identified everywhere.

*c. How did the neighbours help the young couple in the kitchen garden?*

When the couple cleared the backyard in the autumn and turned and manured the
soil, the neighbours watched with an "open and measured gaze" and then came to the
fence to offer practical advice about spacing, hilling and mulching. The young man
resented this interference, but "he took careful note of what was said". The big
Macedonian woman with black eyes and butcher's arms went further and gave the
young wife a bagful of garlic cloves to plant, after the wife had run a hand over
her son's shaved head. Their help with the garden was quickly followed by the
Polish widower's help with the henhouse: when the couple's own attempt collapsed,
he slid through the fence uninvited and rebuilt it.

*d. Why were the people in the neighbourhood surprised at the role of the young
man and his wife in their family?*

Because the couple's arrangement reversed what the migrant families took to be
the natural division of work between husband and wife. The young woman went out
to a job at a hospital and earned the household's money, while the young man
stayed at home writing a thesis on the twentieth century novel, cooking the
dinners and looking after the garden. To neighbours brought up in conservative
European villages a man who stayed indoors while his wife worked looked idle, and
the impression was made worse by the late hour at which the couple got out of
bed, at which the Macedonians "raised eyebrows". The young man could feel their
disapproval without a word being said.

*e. How did the neighbours respond to the woman's pregnancy?*

With an enthusiasm that was collective, physical and quite without reserve.
Before long "the whole neighbourhood knew", and people "smiled tirelessly" at the
couple. The man in the deli gave her chocolates and gave him packets of cigarettes
which he kept at home, not being a smoker. Italian women offered names for the
baby. Greek women stopped the young woman in the street, pulled up her skirt,
felt her belly and assured her it was bound to be a boy. The woman next door
knitted the baby a complete suit with booties and beanie. On the night of the
labour twelve faces watched from the fences and the Macedonians called out what
sounded like their best wishes, and when the child was born a small queue of
bleary faces was still there, cheering. The young woman's reaction to all this is
honestly recorded as mixed: she felt "flattered, claustrophobic, grateful,
peeved".

*f. Why did the young man begin to weep at the end of the story?*

Because several overwhelming things arrive at once and he has no words for any of
them. He has just watched his wife give birth — an act of strength he calls "the
power of her, the sophistication of her" — and seen his newborn son look at him
with one eye. Then, going to the back door, he finds that the neighbours he once
watched with disgust and suspicion have sat up the whole night at the fence and
are cheering for his child. His tears are relief and joy, but they are also
gratitude and a kind of shame, because he has understood in that moment that he
belongs to these people and that his learning gave him no way of expecting such a
thing. Winton says exactly this in the next sentence: "The twentieth century novel
had not prepared him for this."

*g. Why do you think the author did not characterize the persons in the story with
proper names?*

Several reasons work together. First, it is **realistic**: names are exchanged in
conversation, and these households cannot converse, so the young man through whose
eyes we see never learns them. Second, it makes the characters **representative**
— "the young man", "the young woman", "the Macedonian family", "the Polish
widower" could be any such people in any such street, so the story becomes a
statement about migration and neighbourliness in general rather than an anecdote
about particular individuals. Third, it quietly reproduces **the way prejudice
sees**: strangers are first perceived as categories — a nationality, an age, a
pair of cobalt eyes at the fence — and only later as persons. Finally, it
sharpens the ending: people who never learned each other's names nonetheless
behave like family, which is precisely Winton's point.

**Reference to the context**

*a. The story shows that linguistic and cultural barriers do not create any
obstacle in human relationship. Cite some examples from the story where the
neighbours have transcended such barriers.*

The story is built out of such examples, and almost all of them are actions rather
than words.

- The neighbours come to the fence to teach the couple about **spacing, hilling
  and mulching** their vegetables; the young man understands the gardening even
  while resenting the advice.
- The young wife runs a hand over the little boy's **stubble**, and is immediately
  answered with **a bagful of garlic cloves** — a gesture repaid with a gift, with
  no sentence spoken.
- The Polish widower **slides through the fence uninvited and rebuilds the
  collapsed henhouse**, and Winton adds the decisive sentence: "They could not
  understand a word he said." Help needs no grammar.
- The couple give away **heads of cabbage** and receive **grappa and firewood** —
  a working economy of gifts across two fences.
- The Macedonian family teach them to **slaughter, pluck and dress** the muscovy
  ducks, and the whole group sits on blocks and upturned buckets telling "barely
  understood stories"; in that "haze of down and steam and fractured dialogue" the
  couple feel intoxicated and find themselves shouting like everybody else.
- Greek women **feel the young woman's belly** in the street and predict a boy;
  Italian women **offer names**; the woman next door **knits a suit** with booties
  and beanie.
- The Polish widower arrives with a **barrowful of wood scraps** for their fire at
  the very moment the young man is preparing to complain about his noise.
- On the night of the birth **twelve faces** wait at the fences, the Macedonians
  call out "what sounded like their best wishes", and at dawn "a small queue of
  bleary faces" cheers the baby.

Taken together these show that the barrier was never language. What the couple
lacked at first was not vocabulary but willingness; once they were willing to dig,
kill ducks and accept firewood, they were understood perfectly.

*b. The last sentence of the story reads "The twentieth-century novel had not
prepared him for this." In your view, what differences did the young man find
between twentieth-century novels and human relations?*

The twentieth-century novel — the thing he has been writing about for a year — is
famously a literature of the **inner life**: of irony, detachment, alienation,
the isolated consciousness observing a world it cannot join. It taught him to
watch, to interpret and to judge, and the first half of the story shows him doing
exactly that from behind his study window: sensing disapproval, feeling disgust,
resenting interference, suspecting motives. It is a private art, read alone and
written alone.

What he meets at the end is the opposite of all this in four ways. It is
**physical** rather than mental — sweat, ice, a squat, a cord, vernix — and no
amount of reading prepares a man for the body. It is **communal** rather than
private: the most intimate night of his life is attended by a midwife and watched
over by twelve strangers at a fence. It is **wordless**, whereas his whole
training is in words; the people who share the moment with him cannot even tell
him what they are shouting. And it is **unironic** — pure, unqualified joy, of a
kind the sceptical modern novel is rather embarrassed by. So the sentence is not
a rejection of literature. It is an admission that his particular literary
education had trained him for solitude and irony, and life has just handed him
company and joy.

*c. A Nepali proverb says "Neighbors are companions for wedding procession as well
as for funeral procession." Does this proverb apply in the story? Justify.*

Yes, the proverb applies closely, though the story dramatises only the joyful half
of it.

The proverb's real claim is that in the great events of life it is your
**neighbours**, not your distant relatives or chosen friends, who are actually
present, because they are the ones near enough to come. Winton's story proves this
exactly. The couple's own friends belong to a class that postpones children "until
several years after being married — if at all", and their parents appear only to
"cast shocked glances across the fence". The people who are physically there when
the birth comes are the Macedonians and the Pole: twelve faces at the fences in
the last light of the day, and a queue of bleary faces still cheering at dawn.
That is the *janti*, the wedding party, of the proverb — the neighbours who turn
out for a celebration that is not theirs.

The funeral half is not shown, since nobody dies in the story, but the story gives
us its equivalent in the form of **trouble willingly shared**: the henhouse
rebuilt without being asked, firewood carried over in a barrow, garlic given for
nothing, the long patient tolerance of a noisy, sick, sleepless household next
door. These are the small burdens the proverb has in mind. One might add that the
proverb also implies a duty, and by that measure the couple are at first poor
neighbours — they curse the widower behind his back and give him nothing in
return — which is part of what makes the ending a moral resolution and not merely
a happy one.

*d. The author has dealt with an issue of multiculturalism in the story. Why do you
think multiculturalism has become a major issue in the present world?*

Multiculturalism has become unavoidable because people have become mobile on a
scale that has no precedent. Work, education, war, persecution and climate
disaster move millions of people across borders every year, and modern transport
and communication make it possible to live in one country while remaining attached
to another. Cities everywhere are therefore now made of many languages, religions
and diets at once, exactly like Winton's street with its Macedonian, Polish,
Italian and Greek households.

This raises real questions that every society must answer. Should newcomers be
expected to give up their language, dress and religion in order to be accepted, or
should the state protect their right to keep them? Who gets the jobs and the
housing? Whose festivals become public holidays, and whose history is taught in
school? Where these questions are handled badly the results are racism,
segregation, riots and the rise of politicians who win votes by blaming migrants.
Where they are handled well, societies gain enormously — in food, music,
languages, skills and trade.

Nepal is not an outside observer of this debate. It is itself a multicultural
state of well over a hundred languages and many ethnic groups and religions, and
it is also a country from which a very large number of young people migrate for
work to the Gulf, Malaysia, Korea, India and the West, where they become "the
Macedonians" of somebody else's street. Winton's story is therefore doubly useful
to a Nepali reader: it shows how a majority may learn to accept its migrant
neighbours, and it shows how a migrant family may be seen before it is known.

**Reference beyond the text**

*a. Write an essay on Celebration of Childbirth in my Community.*

**Celebration of Childbirth in My Community**

In my community a birth is never a private matter. From the moment it is known
that a woman is expecting, the household begins to be visited, and by the time the
child arrives an entire neighbourhood has taken an interest in it. Like the street
in Tim Winton's "Neighbours", we do not think a baby belongs only to its parents.

The care begins before the birth. Elder women advise the expectant mother on what
to eat and what to avoid, and she is excused from heavy work. After the delivery
the new mother — the *sutkeri* — is looked after for several weeks. She is kept
warm, given soups flavoured with *jwano* (ajwain), ghee and warming spices, and
usually a female relative moves in to cook and wash so that she can rest and feed
the baby. Neighbours arrive with fruit, eggs, milk and money, and nobody comes
empty-handed.

The first formal ceremony is the **nwaran**, the name-giving, held on the eleventh
day in most Hindu families. A priest reads the horoscope, purification is
performed, and a name is chosen from the syllable indicated by the child's
birth-star, although the name the family actually uses every day is often a quite
different pet name. Many communities also keep a sixth-night observance for the
goddess who is believed to write the infant's fate. Months later comes the
**pasni**, the rice-feeding, at around six months for a boy and five for a girl,
when the baby is dressed in new clothes and fed its first grain of rice by the
maternal uncle, and a tray of objects — a book, a pen, coins, a little soil — is
placed before the child so that everyone may guess at its future from whatever it
grabs. Songs, a large meal, and a photograph of a startled infant in unfamiliar
finery complete the day. Customs differ from community to community, and a Newar,
Tharu, Gurung, Madhesi or Muslim household in Nepal will each mark the birth
differently.

What all of these share is a conviction that a child is welcomed by a group and
not merely registered by a family. That is also the discovery made by Winton's
young man, who goes to his back door expecting silence and finds his neighbours
cheering in the dark.

*b. Do the people in your community respond with similar reactions upon the
pregnancy and childbirth as depicted in the story? Give a couple of examples.*

Yes — in several respects the resemblance is striking, and in one or two it is
not.

The first similarity is **the loss of privacy**. In the story the whole
neighbourhood knows about the pregnancy before it has been announced, people smile
"tirelessly", and Greek women stop the young woman in the street, pull up her
skirt and feel her belly. Anyone who has been pregnant in a Nepali town or village
will recognise this instantly. Elderly neighbours ask direct questions about the
pregnancy that would be unthinkable in a Western city; hands are laid on the
stomach without permission; and complete strangers offer confident predictions
about the sex of the child, exactly as the Greek women do.

The second similarity is **the flood of practical gifts and advice**. The deli man
presses chocolates on the young woman, the woman next door knits a suit with
booties and beanie, Italian women propose names. In my community the equivalents
are bags of fruit, eggs and *ghee*, knitted caps, and firm instructions about what
the *sutkeri* must eat; and the naming of the child is treated as everybody's
business, with the priest, the grandparents and any number of aunts all having
opinions.

The third similarity is the one the story ends on: **the community turns out**.
The twelve faces at the fences and the queue of bleary faces cheering at dawn are
matched by the crowd that fills a Nepali house at the *nwaran* and the *pasni*.

There are differences too. Winton's birth takes place at home with a midwife and
the husband present throughout, rubbing his wife's back and feeding her ice; in
most Nepali families today the delivery happens at a hospital or birthing centre,
and the husband is more often outside the door than inside the room, with the
mother's own mother or mother-in-law in the supporting role. And the young woman's
mixed feelings — "flattered, claustrophobic, grateful, peeved" — describe very
accurately something a Nepali daughter-in-law may feel but is rarely free to say.

## Chapter summary

- "Neighbours" is a very short story by the Australian writer Tim Winton (b.
  1960), collected in *Scission* (1985); the Nepali textbook's attribution to
  *Migrants of Australia* could not be verified.
- A young, newly married Australian couple move into an inner-city street of
  European migrants and at first feel "like sojourners in a foreign land".
- The neighbours are a loud Macedonian family on one side and a solitary Polish
  widower on the other; the couple find their noise, habits and watchfulness
  offensive, and the neighbours in turn disapprove of a household where the wife
  earns and the husband stays at home.
- The barrier begins to fall over a vegetable garden in autumn, and falls
  completely when the Polish widower rebuilds their collapsed henhouse although
  "they could not understand a word he said".
- The story is organised by the seasons: garden in autumn, gifts in winter,
  duck-killing and the pregnancy in spring, the neighbourhood's celebration in
  summer, and the birth at the close.
- Nobody in the story is given a name, which makes the characters representative
  and reflects the fact that names cannot be exchanged across a language barrier.
- It is told in third person limited point of view through the young man, in free
  indirect style, so that the reader's prejudices are corrected at the same pace
  as his.
- The central themes are community across language, the slow correction of mild
  prejudice, the inadequacy of book learning before lived experience, and birth as
  a shared, physical event.
- The climax is wordless: twelve faces at the fences, a cheer at dawn, and a man
  weeping at his back door because "the twentieth century novel had not prepared
  him for this."

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** Because the street was full of European migrants whose language, habits and
noise were completely unfamiliar to them. They had grown up in the "expansive
outer suburbs where good neighbours were seldom seen and never heard", so the
daybreak watering, the spitting and the shouting of the Macedonian family made
them feel that they, and not the migrants, were the outsiders.

**2.** For most of the story he did nothing with it: he hammered nails into wood
only to pull them out again, and added to the stack of salvaged timber in his yard
without ever building with it. In the late spring he finally began to sink posts
and pour cement, and by late summer he had almost completed a two-car garage —
although he owned no car.

**3.** The parents visited and "cast shocked glances across the fence" at the
migrant neighbours. The couple, who had themselves been wary only months before,
felt "superior and proud" — which shows both how far they had moved and that their
tolerance was at that stage still a way of feeling better than somebody else.

**4.** They taught them to slaughter, pluck and dress the muscovy ducks the couple
had kept through the winter. Everyone sat on blocks and upturned buckets, the men
butchering and the women plucking, telling "barely understood stories"; the couple
felt intoxicated in the haze of down and steam, and "found themselves shouting" —
behaving at last exactly like the neighbours whose noise had once appalled them.

**5.** Because the boy behaved in ways the young man found crude and could not
interpret — he urinated in the street and sprayed the cat from the back step — and
because of the way he watched. He would stand at the fence with "only his cobalt

properly nor speak to.

**6.** In the last light of the day he saw faces at each fence, watching the house

sounded like their best wishes. It was the first sign that the whole street was

**Long answer questions (5 marks each)**

1. "Neighbours" describes a friendship built almost entirely without words. Trace
   the stages by which Winton builds it, and explain what he is arguing about
   human relationships. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Discuss the importance of the seasonal cycle in the structure and meaning of
   "Neighbours". <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Examine the fence as the central image of the story, and show how Winton uses
   the young man's point of view to expose and then correct his prejudice.
   <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** Winton builds the friendship in stages, and every stage is an action rather
than a conversation. It begins in mutual suspicion: the couple are "wary", the
street feels like "a foreign land", and it takes six months for them to realise
that the Macedonians are talking and not murdering each other. The first contact
is made over work — the couple dig and manure their backyard under "the open and
measured gaze" of the neighbours, who come to the fence with advice about spacing,
hilling and mulching, and the young man, while resenting the interference, "took
careful note of what was said". The first physical contact follows: the young wife
strokes the little boy's stubble and is given a bagful of garlic cloves. The
decisive moment is the henhouse. It falls down, the Polish widower slides through
the fence uninvited and rebuilds it, and Winton lets the silence speak: "They could
not understand a word he said." After that the exchange becomes routine — cabbages
out, grappa and firewood in — and reaches its high point when the couple join the
duck-killing, sitting on upturned buckets in "fractured dialogue" and ending by
shouting themselves. The pregnancy makes the street a single household, and the
birth completes the process with a cheer from people who cannot say what they
mean. Winton's argument is that community is not made of shared opinions, shared
language or even shared culture; it is made of shared work, shared food and shared
waiting. Understanding follows intimacy rather than producing it.

**2.** The story has scarcely any plot, and the seasons supply the shape that plot
would otherwise give. The couple arrive and endure a period of unmarked, unhappy
months; then autumn arrives with the digging and planting of the vegetable patch,
which is the first occasion for contact. Winter, with its "vermilion sunsets" and
the smell of wood smoke, is the season of exchange — cabbages for grappa and
firewood — and the season in which the ducks are kept. Spring brings the killing
of the ducks, the full entry of the couple into the life of the street, and the
beginning of the pregnancy; the Polish widower's hoarded lumber also finally
begins to be used. Summer is the season of the neighbourhood's public celebration
of the coming child, and the year ends with the birth. The seasonal frame does
three jobs. It makes the story's slowness meaningful, since the change in these
people is organic and cannot be hurried any more than a crop can. It produces a
sustained parallel between the vegetable garden and the human one, so that things
planted in autumn and a child conceived in autumn come to fruition together. And
it lends the ending a sense of natural completion: a full turn of the year has
been made, and the arrival of new life is presented as the season's own work
rather than as a plot twist.

**3.** The fence is the story's governing image. It is a literal wooden boundary
between three backyards, but everything that matters in the story is defined by
its relation to that boundary. At first the fence is where misunderstanding is
manufactured: it is where the shaven-headed boy stands with "only his cobalt eyes
showing", where eyebrows are raised at the newcomers' habits, and where the young
man watches his neighbours as if they were specimens. Then it becomes a counter
over which goods pass — advice, garlic cloves, cabbages, grappa, firewood, a
barrowful of scraps. The Polish widower's great act is precisely a violation of
it: he "slid through the fence uninvited". And at the close the fence is lined
with twelve faces, and then with a cheering queue, so that the barrier has become
the place where the community gathers. Winton exposes the young man's prejudice by
telling the story through his eyes in free indirect style, letting us hear his
assumptions in the narrator's own voice: the boy's head "was shaved regularly, he
assumed, in order to make his hair grow thick". That small phrase "he assumed"
lets us catch him inventing explanations for people he has never spoken to. The
same technique records his disgust, his resentment and his suspicion that the
widower is antagonising him on purpose — and then, by giving us no commentary at
all in the final paragraph, it drops him into an experience he cannot analyse. He
simply weeps. The correction is complete because the instrument of judgement has
been taken away from him.
:::

**Reference to the context**

1. "It took six months for the newcomers to comprehend the fact that their
   neighbours were not murdering each other, merely talking."

   (a) Who are the "newcomers" and who are the "neighbours" referred to here?
   (b) What had led the newcomers to such a conclusion? (c) What does the detail
   of "six months" suggest? (d) How does the couple's relationship with this noise
   change by the end of the story? <span class="marks">[5]</span>

2. "The twentieth century novel had not prepared him for this."

   (a) Who is "him" and what is "this"? (b) What is his connection with the
   twentieth century novel? (c) Explain the irony of the sentence. (d) What does
   the sentence contribute to the theme of the story?
   <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — reference to the context
**1.** (a) The "newcomers" are the young, newly married Australian couple who have
just moved into the street; the "neighbours" are the Macedonian family living
immediately on their left. (b) The Macedonians conducted ordinary family
conversation at a volume the couple had never encountered: they "shouted, ranted,
screamed". Having grown up in quiet outer suburbs "where good neighbours were
seldom seen and never heard", the couple could only interpret such noise as
violence. (c) Six months is a long time to be wrong about something so simple, and
Winton chooses the figure deliberately. It measures the depth of the couple's
isolation — in half a year they had not asked a single question or crossed the
fence once — and it shows that prejudice is not corrected by proximity alone but
only by contact. It also makes the reader's own first judgement suspect. (d) The
change is complete and is deliberately marked in the same terms. At the duck
killing in spring, in "the haze of down and steam and fractured dialogue", the
couple "found themselves shouting" — they have joined the noise instead of
decoding it. At the end, the noise that wakes the young man is the neighbours
cheering his son, and it makes him weep.

**2.** (a) "Him" is the young man, the husband and new father; "this" is
everything he has just lived through — his wife's labour and the birth of his son,
and then the discovery that his migrant neighbours have waited all night at the
fence and are cheering at dawn. (b) He is a postgraduate student who has spent the
whole story writing a thesis on the development of the twentieth century novel; it
is mentioned at every stage, and it is what he abandons "for the telephone" when
labour begins. (c) The irony is double. First, a man whose profession is the study
of modern human experience is completely unprepared for the most ordinary human
experience there is. Second, the education he thought made him superior to his
neighbours — he and his wife had felt "superior and proud" — turns out to be
exactly what left him unequipped, while the "uneducated" migrants knew without
being told how to garden, how to build, how to deliver kindness and how to welcome
a child. (d) It states the story's theme in a single line. Winton is contrasting a
private, ironic, verbal, intellectual way of meeting the world with a communal,
physical, wordless one, and declaring the second the more real. It also completes
the young man's character arc: having spent the story interpreting people from
behind a window, he ends it unable to interpret anything and simply weeping, which
in this story is a sign not of failure but of arrival.
:::
