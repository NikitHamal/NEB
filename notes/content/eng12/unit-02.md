"A Respectable Woman" is a very short story by the American writer Kate Chopin.
A wife on a Louisiana sugar plantation is annoyed to hear that her husband has
invited an old college friend to stay; she then finds the quiet, unimpressive
guest disturbing in a way she had not expected, and spends one night on a garden
bench discovering something about herself. Nothing is confessed and nothing is
done. Everything in the story happens inside Mrs. Baroda's mind, and the last
three sentences leave the reader with a question that has been argued over for a
hundred and thirty years.

::: key The central idea
A woman discovers a desire that her position as "a respectable woman" does not
permit her to name, let alone act upon. Chopin neither condemns her nor excuses
her; she simply shows the struggle, and then ends the story on a line that can be
read two opposite ways. The subject is not adultery but **self-knowledge** — and
the cost of the roles society hands to women.
:::

## 2.1 About the author

Kate Chopin (1850–1904) was an American novelist and short-story writer. She was
born Katherine O'Flaherty in St. Louis, Missouri, to an Irish immigrant father
and a mother from an old French-Creole family. Her father was killed in a railway
bridge collapse when she was five, and she grew up in a household run entirely by
women — her mother, grandmother and great-grandmother — which is worth
remembering when you read her fiction about female independence.

```photo id="eng12/kate-chopin" caption="Kate Chopin (1850–1904). Her Louisiana stories were admired in her lifetime as charming regional sketches; a later generation of readers found in them one of the sharpest studies of women's inner lives in nineteenth-century American fiction." width="44"
```

In 1870 she married Oscar Chopin and moved to New Orleans, and later to the
village of Cloutierville in Natchitoches Parish, deep in the Louisiana cotton and
sugar country. She had six children. When Oscar died of malaria in 1882 she was
left with his debts, ran the plantation store herself for a time, and then
returned to St. Louis, where she began to write in her late thirties. Her first
novel, *At Fault*, appeared in 1890. Two collections of stories, *Bayou Folk*
(1894) and *A Night in Acadie* (1897), made her reputation as a **local colour**
writer — a recorder of the speech, dress and customs of Louisiana's Creole and
Cajun communities. She wrote more than a hundred stories in all, among them
"Désirée's Baby", "The Story of an Hour" and "A Respectable Woman".

Her second novel, ***The Awakening*** (1899), is the book she is now famous for.
Its heroine, Edna Pontellier, leaves her husband's house, takes a lover and
finally walks into the sea, and reviewers in 1899 attacked the book as morbid and
indecent. Chopin's writing career effectively ended; a third collection was
dropped by her publisher, and she published almost nothing more before her death
of a brain haemorrhage in 1904. From the 1950s onward critics rediscovered her,
and *The Awakening* is now a standard text in American literature.

::: caution Three details the textbook gets wrong
The Nepali textbook's headnote needs three corrections, and examiners will accept
the accurate version.

1. Chopin was born in **1850**, not 1851.
2. *The Awakening* **was** published, by Herbert S. Stone of Chicago, in 1899.
   It was savaged by reviewers, not suppressed by its publisher. What did happen
   is that her publisher then cancelled her planned third story collection, *A
   Vocation and a Voice* — which is probably the source of the confusion.
3. "A Respectable Woman" was first published in the magazine **Vogue on 15
   February 1894** and collected in ***A Night in Acadie*** (1897). *The Awakening
   and Other Short Stories* (2005) is a modern reprint anthology, not the book
   Chopin wrote it for.
:::

One further fact is useful. **Gouvernail is a recurring Chopin character.** He
appears again in the story "Athénaïse" (1896) and once more, briefly, in *The
Awakening* (1899) — always the same quiet, kindly, slightly melancholy
journalist, and always a man who awakens something in a married woman without
ever pursuing her.

## 2.2 The story

**The unwelcome guest.** Mrs. Baroda is "a little provoked" to learn that her
husband Gaston expects his friend Gouvernail to spend a week or two on the
plantation. The couple have entertained a great deal through the winter and have
spent much of it in New Orleans in "various forms of mild dissipation"; she is
looking forward to rest and to an "undisturbed tete-a-tete with her husband". She
has never met Gouvernail, who was Gaston's college friend and is now a
journalist, but she has "unconsciously formed an image of him": tall, slim,
cynical, in eyeglasses, hands in his pockets. And on that evidence — which is no
evidence at all — "she did not like him".

**The man who arrives.** The real Gouvernail is slim enough, but not very tall
and not at all cynical; he wears no eyeglasses and does not keep his hands in his
pockets. To her own surprise, "she rather liked him when he first presented
himself" — and cannot explain why. He is nothing like the brilliant man Gaston
had described. He sits "mute and receptive" before her chatty eagerness and her
husband's "frank and wordy hospitality". His courtesy is perfect, yet he makes
"no direct appeal to her approval or even esteem" — he simply does not need her
good opinion, which is what unsettles her.

**Days on the portico.** Settled in, Gouvernail likes to sit on the wide portico
in the shade of a big Corinthian pillar, smoking lazily and listening to Gaston
talk about sugar planting. "This is what I call living," he says, with deep
satisfaction, as the warm scented air comes across the cane field. He makes
friends with the dogs. He does not care to fish and shows no eagerness to shoot
grosbecs. In other words he wants nothing, and a hostess whose whole social skill
lies in supplying what guests want is left with nothing to do.

**Piqued.** Puzzled at first, Mrs. Baroda soon becomes **piqued** — irritated in
her vanity. She tries leaving the two men alone; Gouvernail "took no manner of
exception to her action", which is worse. So she does the opposite and "imposed
her society upon him", walking with him to the mill and along the batture,
"persistently" trying "to penetrate the reserve in which he had unconsciously
enveloped himself". That one word *unconsciously* is important: he is not
withholding himself from her; he simply has no idea she is trying.

**The quarrel with Gaston.** "When is he going — your friend?" she asks. "For my
part, he tires me frightfully." Gaston cannot understand it: the man gives her no
trouble. "No. I should like him better if he did," she answers — a reply that
gives away more than she knows. Making a bit of toilet together in her
dressing-room, Gaston takes her pretty face between his hands and tells her,
laughing, that she is "full of surprises, ma belle", and that she is making "a
commotion" over poor Gouvernail, "the last thing he would desire or expect". She
resents the word hotly, and retorts, "unconciliated", that Gaston had called his
friend clever and a man of ideas. Gaston explains that he is clever but "run down
by overwork". She announces that she will go to the city in the morning to have
her spring gowns fitted, and asks to be told when Mr. Gouvernail has gone.

**The bench.** That night she sits alone on a bench beneath a live oak at the
edge of the gravel walk. She has never known her thoughts "to be so confused",
and can gather from them only "the feeling of a distinct necessity to quit her
home in the morning". She hears footsteps on the gravel and sees in the darkness
only "the approaching red point of a lighted cigar" — she knows it is Gouvernail,
because her husband does not smoke. She hopes to stay hidden, but her white gown
gives her away. He throws away his cigar and sits down beside her, "without a
suspicion that she might object", and hands her the white scarf her husband has
sent out. She takes it with a murmur of thanks and lets it lie in her lap.

**The voice in the dark.** Gouvernail says something ordinary about the night air,
and then, half to himself, quotes Walt Whitman: "'Night of south winds — night of
the large few stars! Still nodding night —'". She makes no reply to this
"apostrophe to the night", which "was not addressed to her". Then his reserve
melts, and he talks freely and intimately "in a low, hesitating drawl that was not
unpleasant to hear" — about the old college days with Gaston, about "keen and
blind ambitions and large intentions", and about what is left to him now: "a
philosophic acquiescence to the existing order — only a desire to be permitted to
exist, with now and then a little whiff of genuine life, such as he was breathing
now."

**The temptation.** Chopin then writes the sentences on which the whole story
turns. Mrs. Baroda's mind "only vaguely grasped what he was saying. Her physical
being was for the moment predominant." She is not listening to his words but
"drinking in the tones of his voice". She wants to reach out in the darkness and
touch his face or his lips with her fingertips; she wants to draw close and
whisper against his cheek — "she did not care what — as she might have done if she
had not been a respectable woman." And then the beautiful, exact reversal: "The
stronger the impulse grew to bring herself near him, the further, in fact, did she
draw away from him." As soon as she can do it without obvious rudeness she gets
up and leaves. Gouvernail, entirely unaware, lights a fresh cigar and finishes his
apostrophe to the night.

**The battle fought alone.** She is greatly tempted to tell her husband — "who was
also her friend" — about "this folly that had seized her", but does not: "Besides
being a respectable woman she was a very sensible one; and she knew there are some
battles in life which a human being must fight alone." She takes an early train to
the city the next morning and does not come back until Gouvernail has left the
house.

**The last three sentences.** Gaston wants the friend back in the summer, and
gives up the idea against his wife's "strenuous opposition". Then, before the year
ends, she proposes "wholly from herself" that Gouvernail visit them again. Her
husband is surprised and delighted: "I am glad, chere amie, to know that you have
finally overcome your dislike for him; truly he did not deserve it." And she
answers, laughing, after pressing a long tender kiss on his lips: "Oh, I have
overcome everything! You will see. This time I shall be very nice to him."

## 2.3 Characters

| Character | Who they are | Role in the story |
|---|---|---|
| Mrs. Baroda | Mistress of a Louisiana sugar plantation; young, attractive, sociable, used to being the centre of a room | Protagonist; the entire story is her inner conflict |
| Gouvernail | Gaston's old college friend; a journalist, "run down by overwork", quiet and self-contained | The catalyst — he does nothing, and that is exactly the point |
| Gaston Baroda | Her husband; a sugar planter, frank, affectionate, talkative, trusting | Represents the comfortable, secure marriage that the temptation threatens; also its blindness |
| Aunt Octavie | Mrs. Baroda's aunt in the city; mentioned only | Her respectable escape route |
| The dogs, the cigar, the white scarf | Objects that carry meaning | Gouvernail's ease, his presence in the dark, and the husband's unwitting part in the scene |

**Mrs. Baroda.** She is not a wicked woman and not a weak one; she is a woman who
has never before met a situation her social training could not handle. Notice that
her difficulty begins in **vanity**, not in love. She dislikes Gouvernail before
she has seen him, on the strength of an imagined portrait. She likes him on sight
and cannot say why. She is annoyed that he does not need her attention, and the
word Chopin chooses is precise: she is "piqued". Only on the bench does the
annoyance reveal what it really was. Chopin's most daring sentence is not about
love at all but about the body — "Her physical being was for the moment
predominant" — and the desire she records is specific and physical: to touch his
face, to whisper against his cheek. What stops her is a single phrase from the
story's title: she would have done it "if she had not been a respectable woman".
And then she behaves with real strength. She does not confess, does not flirt,
does not run into anybody's arms; she removes herself from the house and fights it
alone, because "she knew there are some battles in life which a human being must
fight alone." Whether she wins it is precisely what the last line refuses to say.

**Gouvernail.** He is the stillest character in the story and the most disturbing.
He wants nothing. He will not fish, will not shoot, makes no effort to charm, and
"made no direct appeal to her approval or even esteem". Chopin is careful to tell
us that he is not shy — "in no sense a diffident man, for he was not a
self-conscious one" — his silences are moods, not disabilities. When he does talk
on the bench, what he says is quietly sad: youthful ambition has come to nothing
and all he asks now is "to be permitted to exist, with now and then a little whiff
of genuine life". He is entirely innocent. He has no idea that Mrs. Baroda is
struggling beside him, throws away his cigar without a thought, quotes Whitman to
the night rather than to her, and lights a fresh cigar when she goes. That
innocence is what makes the story so uncomfortable — the crisis is one person's,
and the other party never knows it happened.

**Gaston Baroda.** Generous, cheerful and completely trusting. He teases his wife
affectionately, takes her face in his hands, calls her *ma belle* and *chere
amie*, and reads her irritation as a charming unpredictability: "Even I can never
count upon how you are going to act under given conditions." He is also the
unconscious author of the bench scene — it is he who sends the scarf out into the
garden with Gouvernail. His trust is admirable, but Chopin lets us see its other
face: he does not know his wife at all, and at the end he is delighted by a
sentence whose meaning he has completely misread.

## 2.4 Themes

**Desire and respectability.** The title is the theme. "Respectable" is a social
word, not a moral one: it describes how a woman is seen. Mrs. Baroda's impulse is
not checked by love for her husband or by conscience but by an identity — she is
*a respectable woman*, and respectable women do not do this. Chopin's quiet
radicalism is to present the impulse itself as natural and the restraint as
external.

**The inner life is the real life.** Almost nothing happens outwardly: a guest
visits, a wife goes shopping, a guest leaves. The story insists that the important
events of a life may leave no trace at all in its history.

**Self-knowledge.** Mrs. Baroda learns three things she did not know: that she can
be attracted to a man who makes no attempt to attract her; that her body can
overrule her mind; and that some things cannot be shared even with a loving
husband. This is the "awakening" that Chopin would enlarge into a whole novel five
years later.

**Marriage, trust and its limits.** The Barodas' marriage is happy, affectionate
and, at its centre, uninformed. Chopin does not suggest that Mrs. Baroda should
have confessed; she suggests that a marriage can be warm and still leave a woman
completely alone with the thing that matters most to her.

**The freedom the guest represents.** Gouvernail is a man with no plantation, no
wife and no ambition, who asks only for "a little whiff of genuine life". To a
woman whose days are made of gowns, guests and hospitality, that unowned life is
itself an attraction.

::: definition Local colour (regionalism)
A kind of nineteenth-century American fiction devoted to the speech, manners,
dress and landscape of one particular region. Chopin's Louisiana details — the
portico with Corinthian pillars, the sugar field, the batture, the grosbecs, the
French phrases *ma belle*, *chere amie*, *tete-a-tete* — are local colour; but in
her best work the region is only the surface, and the subject underneath is the
inner life of women.
:::

## 2.5 Setting, structure and point of view

**Setting.** A sugar plantation in Creole Louisiana in the 1890s: a large house
with a wide portico and Corinthian pillars, a mill, a gravel walk, live oaks, a
batture along the river, and New Orleans a train ride away. The setting does real
work. It is **enclosed** — a household in which the same three people meet at
every meal — and it is **hot, scented and languid**, which is why the air
"caressed him with its warm and scented velvety touch". The climax could only
happen where it does: outdoors, in the dark, out of sight of the house.

**Structure.** The textbook headnote is right that the story follows the classical
pattern exactly — exposition (the invitation and her prejudice), rising action (her
puzzlement, her pique, the quarrel with Gaston), climax (the bench), falling
action (her silence and her flight to the city), resolution (her proposal that he
return). What is unusual is that every stage is internal, and that the resolution
resolves nothing.

**Point of view.** Third person limited, tied to Mrs. Baroda, and much of it in
**free indirect style**, so that her judgements reach us in the narrator's voice:
"she did not like him", "he tires me frightfully". Because we are locked inside
her head we know what Gouvernail cannot know and what Gaston never learns — and
because we are locked inside her head, we are denied the one piece of information
we want at the end, since she does not tell us what she means.

**The ending.** "I have overcome everything!" is the most discussed sentence
Chopin ever wrote after the last line of *The Awakening*. It is a genuine
ambiguity — Chopin has arranged the words so that both readings are fully
available — and in an examination you should present the possibilities rather than
pretending to certainty.

## 2.6 Style and literary devices

| Device | Example from the story | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| Irony (situational) | She dislikes the man she has never met and is drawn to the man who ignores her | Overturns her confidence in her own judgement — and the reader's |
| Free indirect style | "he tires me frightfully"; "she did not like him" | Her opinions arrive in the narrator's voice, so we share them before we question them |
| Antithesis | "The stronger the impulse grew to bring herself near him, the further, in fact, did she draw away" | One sentence containing the whole conflict; desire and restraint measured against each other |
| Symbolism | The lighted cigar; the white gown and white scarf; the darkness | His unconscious presence; her visible "respectability"; the concealment that makes feeling possible |
| Literary allusion | Gouvernail's quotation from Whitman's "Song of Myself" | Whitman was the great American poet of the body and of frank desire; the allusion says what the characters cannot |
| Euphemism / omission | "she did not care what"; "this folly that had seized her" | Her own language refuses to name the desire, enacting her repression |
| Sensory imagery | Air that "caressed him with its warm and scented velvety touch"; "the approaching red point of a lighted cigar" | The setting itself is made sensuous, so the climax feels inevitable |
| Foreshadowing | "I should like him better if he did [give me trouble]" | She has already, without knowing it, confessed what she wants |
| Understatement | "There was some talk of having him back during the summer" | A whole year of resistance compressed into one flat sentence |
| Open ending | "I have overcome everything!" | Transfers the moral judgement from the author to the reader |

::: tip A warning about this story in the exam
Do not write that Mrs. Baroda "falls in love with Gouvernail" or that she "has an
affair". Neither is in the text. What the text gives you is a physical impulse,
lasting a few minutes on a bench, which she resists and never mentions. Similarly,
do not state flatly what the last line means. The strongest answer sets out both
readings, gives the evidence for each, and says why Chopin left it open.
:::

## 2.8 Textbook exercises

**Understanding the text**

*a. Why was Mrs. Baroda unhappy with the information about Gouvernail's visit to
their farm?*

For two reasons, one practical and one entirely imaginary. Practically, she was
tired of company. She and Gaston had "entertained a good deal during the winter",
with much of the season passed in New Orleans "in various forms of mild
dissipation", and she was now looking forward to "a period of unbroken rest" and
an "undisturbed tete-a-tete with her husband". A guest staying a week or two
would destroy exactly the quiet she wanted. But she was also prejudiced against a
man she had never seen. He was Gaston's college friend, now a journalist, and "in
no sense a society man or 'a man about town'" — so she had "unconsciously formed
an image of him in her mind", picturing him tall, slim and cynical, with
eyeglasses and his hands in his pockets, "and she did not like him". Chopin makes
the point gently: the dislike had no object, only a portrait she had painted
herself.

*b. How was Gouvernail different from Mrs. Baroda's expectation?*

At every point. He was slim, but "he wasn't very tall nor very cynical; neither
did he wear eyeglasses nor carry his hands in his pockets" — and, contrary to
everything she had decided in advance, "she rather liked him when he first
presented himself". Nor was he the brilliant, clever, idea-filled man Gaston had
described. He sat "rather mute and receptive" before her chatty eagerness and her
husband's wordy hospitality; his manners were faultless, yet "he made no direct
appeal to her approval or even esteem". He was happy to sit on the portico
smoking and listening to Gaston talk about sugar, saying with deep satisfaction
"This is what I call living"; he made friends with the dogs; he did not care to
fish and showed no eagerness to shoot grosbecs. She had expected a performer and
found a man who wanted nothing from her — which was far harder to deal with.

*c. How does Mrs. Baroda compare Gouvernail with her husband?*

Chopin never sets the two men side by side in a formal comparison; the contrast
emerges from Mrs. Baroda's reactions. Gaston is frank, "wordy", hospitable and
energetic — a working sugar planter who proposes fishing and shooting, teases his
wife, takes her face in his hands and calls her *ma belle*. Gouvernail is quiet,
passive and physically idle, content to sit in the shade of a pillar. Gaston is,
as Chopin puts it at the crucial moment, her husband "who was also her friend":
she knows him completely. Gouvernail is a reserve she cannot "penetrate". One
small detail measures the distance between them: she can identify the approaching
figure in the dark by the red point of a cigar, "for her husband did not smoke".
Intellectually she takes her husband's side against Gouvernail — Gaston says he
is clever and a man of ideas, and she cannot see it. Emotionally, on the bench,
the comparison reverses without her ever stating it: it is the stranger's voice,
not her husband's, that makes her "physical being" predominant.

*d. Why and how did Mrs. Baroda try to change Gouvernail's solitary habits?*

She tried because his self-sufficiency puzzled her and then wounded her vanity.
After a few days she "gave over being puzzled and remained piqued", because a man
who needed nothing from his hostess gave her no role to play. Her method had two
stages. First she withdrew, leaving her husband and her guest "for the most part,
alone together" — a hint that a polite guest was expected to notice and protest.
When she found that "Gouvernail took no manner of exception to her action", she
reversed the tactic completely and "imposed her society upon him", going with him
on his idle strolls to the mill and his walks along the batture. In Chopin's
phrase, she "persistently sought to penetrate the reserve in which he had
unconsciously enveloped himself". The adverb is the sting: he was not shutting
her out, and never noticed the campaign.

*e. How does Gaston disagree with his wife on Gouvernail's character?*

When she complains that the guest "tires me frightfully", Gaston is baffled,
because by any ordinary standard Gouvernail is the perfect visitor: "I can't
understand; he gives you no trouble." Her answer — "No. I should like him better
if he did" — strikes him as merely one of her charming inconsistencies, and he
tells her, laughing, that she is "full of surprises, ma belle" and that "even I
can never count upon how you are going to act under given conditions". He accuses
her, in a word she hotly resents, of "taking poor Gouvernail seriously and making
a commotion over him, the last thing he would desire or expect". Where she finds
Gouvernail dull and disappointing, Gaston insists he is genuinely clever and a
man of ideas, and explains the quietness away entirely: "the poor fellow is run
down by overwork now. That's why I asked him here to take a rest." Gaston's
reading of his friend is, in fact, accurate; his reading of his wife is not.

*f. Why is Gaston surprised with his wife's expression towards the end of the
story?*

Because her behaviour reverses itself twice and he can account for neither
reversal. Having disliked Gouvernail from before his arrival and left home rather
than stay in the house with him, she then opposes so "strenuously" a second visit
in the summer that Gaston gives up the idea. Yet before the year is out she
proposes, "wholly from herself", that Gouvernail should come again. Her husband is
"surprised and delighted" — surprised because nothing has happened, so far as he
knows, to change her mind, and delighted because he is fond of his friend. He
explains it to himself as the natural end of a groundless prejudice: "I am glad,
chere amie, to know that you have finally overcome your dislike for him; truly he
did not deserve it." Her reply, given laughingly after a long, tender kiss — "Oh,
I have overcome everything! You will see. This time I shall be very nice to him"
— surprises him again by its warmth and its odd emphasis. The reader is surprised
for a quite different reason: Gaston does not know about the bench, and we do.

**Reference to the context**

*a. What is the cause of conflict in Mrs. Baroda's mind? What role does Mrs.
Baroda 'being a respectable woman' play in the story?*

The conflict is between a sudden, physical desire for her husband's guest and the
identity she has been brought up to inhabit. It builds in three stages. It begins
as injured **vanity** — Gouvernail neither needs nor courts her good opinion, and
she is "piqued". It becomes **curiosity** — she cannot understand him, and pursues
him along the batture trying to "penetrate the reserve". It becomes, on the bench
in the darkness, **desire**: "Her physical being was for the moment predominant."
She wants to touch his face and lips with her fingertips and to whisper against
his cheek, and Chopin states the obstacle in the same sentence — she might have
done it "as she might have done if she had not been a respectable woman". The
result is the story's finest line, in which the conflict is measured: "The
stronger the impulse grew to bring herself near him, the further, in fact, did she
draw away from him."

The phrase "a respectable woman" functions in three ways.

First, it is the **brake**. What stops her is not love of her husband, not
religion and not conscience, but a social identity; Chopin is careful to locate
the restraint outside her feelings.

Second, it is an **isolation**. Precisely because she is respectable she cannot
speak of the episode, even to the husband "who was also her friend"; respectability
requires that the struggle be invisible, and so she must fight "one of those
battles in life which a human being must fight alone".

Third, it is **ironic**, and the irony deepens at the close. If respectability is
only a matter of appearance, then a woman who feels everything and shows nothing
remains, publicly, entirely respectable — and the final line lets us wonder
whether the title describes a virtue or merely a reputation.

*b. Sketch the character of Gouvernail and contrast it with Gaston.*

**Gouvernail** is a journalist, Gaston's old college friend, "run down by
overwork" and invited to the plantation to rest. Physically he is slim and
unremarkable. Temperamentally he is quiet, passive and entirely without vanity: he
sits mute before his hostess's chatter, makes "no direct appeal to her approval or
even esteem", and is perfectly content on the portico with a cigar and somebody
else's conversation. Chopin is careful to distinguish his quietness from shyness —
he is "in no sense a diffident man, for he was not a self-conscious one", and his
silences are "not constitutional, but the result of moods", which is why they melt
on the bench. Underneath he is a disappointed man. Of the "keen and blind
ambitions and large intentions" of his college days nothing is left but "a
philosophic acquiescence to the existing order — only a desire to be permitted to
exist, with now and then a little whiff of genuine life". He is also completely
innocent in the story's crisis: he sits down beside Mrs. Baroda "without a
suspicion that she might object", quotes Whitman to the night rather than to her,
and lights a fresh cigar when she leaves.

**Gaston Baroda** is his opposite in almost every particular. He is a landowner,
not a wage-earner; active, not idle; frank and "wordy" where his friend is silent;
sociable, hospitable and affectionate, taking his wife's face between his hands,
teasing her and calling her *ma belle* and *chere amie*. Where Gouvernail wants
nothing, Gaston enjoys everything — his plantation, his guest, his marriage. He
does not smoke; his friend's cigar is the very thing that identifies the stranger
in the dark.

The contrast has a purpose. Gaston represents the known, warm, secure world Mrs.
Baroda already possesses; Gouvernail represents an unowned life she has never
imagined. Yet they share one quality, and it is the saddest note in the story:
**neither man has any idea what is happening to her.** Gaston misreads her as
charmingly unpredictable; Gouvernail does not read her at all.

*c. Why does Mrs. Baroda not disclose her feelings towards Gouvernail to her
husband?*

Chopin gives the reason directly, and it is worth quoting because it is the
story's moral centre: "Besides being a respectable woman she was a very sensible
one; and she knew there are some battles in life which a human being must fight
alone."

Several things are folded into that. Practically, she judges that telling him
would do harm and no good: the impulse lasted minutes, nothing happened, and a
confession would poison her husband's oldest friendship and her own marriage for
the sake of relieving her feelings. Psychologically, she understands that sharing
a temptation is a way of handing it to someone else to solve, and that this one is
hers. Socially, respectability forbids even the naming of such a feeling; note
that she cannot name it in her own thoughts either, calling it only "this folly
that had seized her". And there is something more troubling underneath. She was
"greatly tempted" to tell Gaston — the same verb Chopin has just used of the
desire itself — which suggests that confession would have been a second kind of
self-indulgence. Her silence is therefore genuine strength, and at the same time
the exact price of her respectability: it makes her, on the most important night
of her inner life, completely alone in a happy marriage.

*d. The last three sentences of the story bring a kind of twist. After reading
these three sentences, how do you analyze Mrs. Baroda's attitude towards
Gouvernail?*

The three sentences are Gaston's pleasure at her change of mind, his assumption
that she has "finally overcome your dislike for him", and her laughing reply: "Oh,
I have overcome everything! You will see. This time I shall be very nice to him."
They are built to be read in at least two opposite ways, and a good answer
presents both.

**The optimistic reading — she has mastered herself.** A year has passed. She has
removed herself from the house, refused a summer visit, and fought the battle
alone; now she judges that she is safe, and is generous enough to restore her
husband's friendship with his oldest friend. On this reading "everything" means
the attraction itself, "very nice to him" means no more than ordinary courtesy,
and the long tender kiss she gives Gaston first is evidence that her marriage has
won. The word "overcome" in its plainest sense means *conquered*.

**The disturbing reading — she has mastered her scruples.** She emphasises
"everything", not "my dislike", correcting her husband's guess; the year of
"strenuous opposition" suggests she was not safe at all; and the proposal comes
"wholly from herself". On this reading what she has overcome is her resistance,
"You will see" is a promise, and "I shall be very nice to him" is a quiet
declaration of intent — delivered, with real irony, immediately after kissing her
husband.

**Why it matters.** Chopin has arranged the words so that both are fully available
and neither can be proved, and this is deliberate rather than careless. By 1894 an
American magazine story was expected to reward virtue plainly. Chopin instead
hands the verdict to the reader, so that whichever reading you choose reveals your
own assumptions about what a respectable woman is likely to do. That is the real
twist: the story tests us rather than Mrs. Baroda. It is also consistent with the
rest of her work — five years later, in *The Awakening*, she would refuse in the
same way to tell the reader how to judge Edna Pontellier.

**Reference beyond the text**

*a. The entry of an outsider into a family has been a recurring subject in both
literature and films. Narrate a story, real or imaginative, where an outsider's
arrival destroys the intimate relationship between the husband and the wife and
causes a break-up in the marital relationship without direct fault of anyone.*

**The Lodger**

Ramesh and Sabina had been married eleven years and had, as everyone in the
neighbourhood agreed, nothing to complain of. He taught mathematics at the
campus; she ran a small tailoring business from the front room of their house in
Dharan. They had a daughter of nine. When the upstairs flat fell vacant, it was
Ramesh who suggested letting it to Nabin, a photographer newly transferred from
Kathmandu, and Ramesh who carried his boxes up the stairs.

Nabin was an easy tenant and a pleasant man. He paid on time, mended the gate
without being asked, and in the evenings sat on the steps developing his pictures
and answering the daughter's endless questions. He was not handsome and he did not
flirt. What he had — and what nobody in that house had had for years — was time.
He noticed things. He noticed that the woman downstairs had an eye for colour that
was wasted on school uniforms, and he said so, once, in the ordinary way, and went
back to his photographs.

Sabina thought about the remark for three days.

Nothing happened. That is the whole point of the story. She did not fall in love
with Nabin; she fell in love with the version of herself that a stranger had
accidentally described. She began to take on design work she had once refused. She
began to talk at dinner about things other than the shop, and to notice that her
husband, tired after six hours of teaching, answered in single words. Ramesh, for
his part, noticed only that his wife had become restless, and that she laughed
more readily on the stairs than at the table. He said nothing, because there was
nothing to accuse anybody of, and a man who complains of a lodger who has done no
wrong looks small.

So the silence grew instead of the quarrel. When Nabin was transferred back to
Kathmandu after fourteen months, the two of them stood at the gate and waved, and
then went inside to a house that was suddenly very quiet. It was three years
before they separated, and when people asked why, neither of them could give a
reason. There was none to give. An outsider had walked through their lives without
touching anything, and by the light of his passing each of them had seen how long
they had stopped looking at the other.

*(Chekhov's "About Love" turns on the same subject: Alyokhin and Anna
Alekseyevna, the wife of a friend, love each other for years, say nothing, and
part — and the story's sadness lies in the fact that nobody behaves badly.)*

*b. Mrs. Baroda makes an expectation about Gouvernail even before meeting him.
Suppose you are a mature girl/boy and your family members are giving you pressure
for getting married. Write in about 200 words describing what qualities you would
like to get in your future husband/wife.*

**What I Would Look For**

My family has begun to ask when I intend to marry, and to describe candidates to
me the way Mrs. Baroda described Gouvernail before she had met him — height,
complexion, salary, family. Her mistake is the one I would most like to avoid: she
built a portrait and decided to dislike it, and the real man turned out to be
nothing like the picture.

So I would begin with temperament rather than a list. I would want someone
**kind** — not merely polite in company, but considerate when tired and when
nobody is watching. I would want someone **honest**, because a marriage in which
difficult things cannot be said becomes, as Chopin's story shows, a very lonely
place. I would want **respect between equals**: my partner's work should matter as
much as mine, and household duties and money should be shared rather than
assigned by custom.

Beyond that I would hope for a sense of humour, patience with my family and mine
with theirs, some curiosity about the world, and the ordinary ability to apologise.

I am less concerned with caste, complexion or salary. Those can be listed in
advance; character cannot. It can only be discovered by meeting a person with an
open mind — which is exactly what Mrs. Baroda failed to do.

## Chapter summary

- "A Respectable Woman" is a short story by the American writer Kate Chopin
  (1850–1904), first published in *Vogue* on 15 February 1894 and collected in
  *A Night in Acadie* (1897).
- Mrs. Baroda is annoyed that her husband Gaston has invited his old college
  friend Gouvernail, a journalist, to stay on their Louisiana sugar plantation,
  and has already decided to dislike a man she has never met.
- The real Gouvernail is quiet, undemanding and entirely uninterested in
  impressing her, which first puzzles her, then leaves her "piqued".
- The climax comes at night on a bench beneath a live oak: Gouvernail sits beside
  her, quotes Whitman to the darkness and talks about his lost ambitions, and she
  finds that "her physical being was for the moment predominant" and longs to
  touch him.
- She resists — "the stronger the impulse grew... the further, in fact, did she
  draw away" — tells nobody, and leaves for the city on the early train.
- She does not confess to her husband because "there are some battles in life
  which a human being must fight alone".
- A year later she herself proposes that Gouvernail visit again, and tells Gaston,
  "I have overcome everything! ... This time I shall be very nice to him" — a line
  that can mean either that she has conquered the attraction or that she has
  conquered her resistance to it.
- The themes are desire against respectability, the reality of the inner life,
  self-knowledge, and the limits of even a loving marriage.
- The technique is third person limited point of view with free indirect style, a
  classical plot shape applied to an entirely internal action, and a deliberately
  open ending.

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** She had "unconsciously formed an image of him in her mind", picturing him
tall, slim and cynical, wearing eyeglasses and carrying his hands in his pockets —
and on that basis she decided that she did not like him. The picture was almost
entirely wrong: he was slim, but not very tall and not at all cynical, and he wore
no eyeglasses and did not keep his hands in his pockets.

**2.** Because he asked nothing of her. She could not understand him, and when she
deliberately left him alone with her husband he "took no manner of exception" to
it. A hostess whose importance depends on being needed found herself unnecessary,
and the injury to her vanity turned her puzzlement into irritation.

**3.** She could see nothing in the dark but "the approaching red point of a
lighted cigar", and she knew from that alone that it must be Gouvernail, "for her
husband did not smoke". Her own white gown then revealed her to him.

**4.** He spoke of his college days with Gaston and of "the days of keen and blind
ambitions and large intentions", and said that all that was left to him now was "a
philosophic acquiescence to the existing order — only a desire to be permitted to
exist, with now and then a little whiff of genuine life, such as he was breathing
now."

**5.** That she was sensible as well as respectable, and "she knew there are some
battles in life which a human being must fight alone." Telling Gaston would have
relieved her own feelings at the cost of his peace and his oldest friendship.

**6.** It is Gaston who invites Gouvernail in the first place, and it is Gaston who
sends Gouvernail out into the dark garden with his wife's white scarf. The husband
thus arranges, in perfect innocence and out of simple kindness, the one encounter
that threatens his marriage.

**Long answer questions (5 marks each)**

1. Discuss "A Respectable Woman" as a study of the conflict between natural desire
   and social respectability. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Examine Chopin's use of point of view and of the open ending, and explain what
   she gains by them. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. "Gouvernail does nothing at all, and that is why the story works." Discuss his
   role. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** The story sets a natural impulse against an acquired identity and shows the
identity winning — at a price. Chopin prepares the ground carefully. Mrs. Baroda's
interest in Gouvernail is not presented as love; it grows out of vanity, because
he does not court her good opinion, and then out of curiosity, as she tries "to
penetrate the reserve in which he had unconsciously enveloped himself". On the
bench, in darkness, with a voice speaking Whitman's lines about the night, the
feeling becomes unmistakably physical: "Her physical being was for the moment
predominant," and she wants to touch his face and lips and to whisper against his
cheek — she "did not care what". Chopin then names the restraint precisely: she
would have done it "if she had not been a respectable woman". This is a social
category, not a moral one, and that is the story's quiet daring. The conflict is
then compressed into a single antithesis: "The stronger the impulse grew to bring
herself near him, the further, in fact, did she draw away from him." The cost of
the victory is measured in the next paragraph. Because respectable women may not
even name such feelings, she cannot tell the husband "who was also her friend",
and must fight "one of those battles in life which a human being must fight
alone". Chopin neither condemns the desire nor celebrates the restraint. She shows
a woman locked out of her own marriage by a word — and then, with the last line,
declines to tell us whether the lock held.

**2.** Chopin tells the story in the third person but stays entirely inside Mrs.
Baroda, and much of it is written in free indirect style, so that the character's
judgements reach us as if they were the narrator's: "she did not like him", "he
tires me frightfully". This has three consequences. First, we adopt her prejudice
before we examine it, and are therefore surprised along with her when Gouvernail
turns out to be likeable — the reader is caught out in exactly the way she is.
Second, it makes the crisis visible. Nothing happens on the bench that an observer
would notice; the whole event is a movement of feeling, and only an interior
viewpoint could record it. Third, and most cunningly, it controls what we are
allowed to know. We know what Gouvernail cannot know and what Gaston never learns,
which gives the final scene its dramatic irony — a delighted husband misreading
his wife in front of a reader who has more information than he does. But the same
device creates the famous ambiguity of the ending. Because the narration reports
Mrs. Baroda's speech without entering her mind at that moment, "I have overcome
everything! ... This time I shall be very nice to him" can mean that she has
conquered the attraction or that she has conquered her resistance to it. Chopin
had spent the whole story telling us what her heroine felt; by withholding it at
the last moment she forces the reader to supply the verdict, and whichever verdict
we supply tells us more about our own assumptions than about Mrs. Baroda. In a
magazine world that expected virtue to be rewarded visibly, that refusal was itself
a bold act.

**3.** Gouvernail's passivity is the mechanism of the entire story. He does not
flirt, does not flatter and does not pursue; Chopin states flatly that he "made no
direct appeal to her approval or even esteem". He will not fish or shoot, he is
happy to sit on the portico with a cigar listening to somebody else, and when his
hostess withdraws he "took no manner of exception". Had he made the smallest
advance, the story would have become a conventional tale of a predatory guest, and
Mrs. Baroda's struggle would have been merely a struggle against him. Because he
does nothing, everything that happens must come from inside her, and the story
becomes what Chopin wanted: a study of a woman discovering herself. His
indifference is also the precise cause of the trouble, since it is his
self-sufficiency that wounds her vanity and sets her pursuing him along the
batture. His innocence is maintained to the end — he sits down beside her "without
a suspicion that she might object", quotes Whitman "half to himself" in an
apostrophe that "was not addressed to her", and lights a fresh cigar as she
escapes. He never learns that anything occurred. At the same time Chopin makes him
more than a device: the disappointed man who asks only "to be permitted to exist,
with now and then a little whiff of genuine life" is a real and rather touching
figure, and it is his weariness, not any charm, that moves her. He is the still
centre of the story, and all its motion is measured against him.
:::

**Reference to the context**

1. "The stronger the impulse grew to bring herself near him, the further, in fact,
   did she draw away from him."

   (a) Who is "she" and who is "him"? (b) Where and when does this happen?
   (c) What is the impulse referred to, and what restrains it? (d) Comment on the
   construction of the sentence and its importance in the story.
   <span class="marks">[5]</span>

2. "Oh, I have overcome everything! You will see. This time I shall be very nice
   to him."

   (a) Who speaks these words, to whom, and on what occasion? (b) What had the
   listener just said? (c) Give two possible meanings of the word "everything".
   (d) Why do you think the author ends the story here?
   <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — reference to the context
**1.** (a) "She" is Mrs. Baroda, mistress of the plantation; "him" is Gouvernail,
her husband's old college friend, who is staying with them as a guest. (b) It
happens at night in the garden, on a bench beneath a live oak at the edge of the
gravel walk, where she had gone to sit alone with her confused thoughts and where
Gouvernail has joined her, bringing a scarf sent by her husband. (c) The impulse
is physical desire: she has stopped listening to his words and is "drinking in the
tones of his voice", and she wants to reach out in the darkness and touch his face
or lips with her fingertips and to whisper against his cheek. What restrains her is
stated in the previous sentence — she would have done it "if she had not been a
respectable woman", that is, her social identity rather than her conscience.
(d) The sentence is built on **antithesis** and balanced comparatives — "the
stronger... the further" — so that the two opposing forces are measured against
each other inside a single clause, and the interpolated "in fact" gives it the
tone of a discovery rather than a judgement. It matters because it states the
story's whole conflict in one line and explains all her subsequent behaviour: her
flight to the city, her year of "strenuous opposition", and the impossibility of
telling even her husband. It also prepares the ending, since a woman who retreats
hardest when she is most tempted may still be retreating when she says she has
"overcome everything".

**2.** (a) Mrs. Baroda speaks them to her husband Gaston, laughing and after
pressing "a long, tender kiss" upon his lips, about a year after Gouvernail's
visit, on the occasion of her own unexpected proposal that he should be invited
again. (b) Gaston had just said that he was glad she had "finally overcome your
dislike for him", adding that "truly he did not deserve it" — assuming, as he has
throughout, that her objection was a groundless prejudice. (c) "Everything" may
mean (i) the attraction itself, so that she is announcing that she has mastered her
feelings and is now safe enough to receive him as an ordinary guest; or (ii) her
own resistance — the scruples, the respectability and the year of opposition — so
that the invitation is issued with a very different intention, and "I shall be very
nice to him" becomes a promise rather than a courtesy. Notice that she corrects
her husband's word "dislike" with the much larger word "everything", which is what
makes the second reading possible. (d) Chopin ends here because the story's subject
is the reader's judgement as much as Mrs. Baroda's conduct. To continue would be to
settle the question, and settling it would turn a study of a woman's inner life
into a simple moral tale of either virtue rewarded or virtue lost. By stopping at
the ambiguity she leaves the heroine her privacy, keeps the dramatic irony intact —
Gaston is delighted by a sentence he has misunderstood — and obliges each reader to
decide what a "respectable woman" is likely to do, thereby testing the very
assumption the title raises.
:::
