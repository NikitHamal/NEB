Modal verbs are the nine little helpers — *can, could, may, might, will, would,
shall, should, must* — that do not describe an action but **comment on** it: how
possible it is, how necessary it is, how polite you are being. NEB Q7 sets them
almost every year, usually as a gap to be filled with a suitable modal, or as a
sentence to be corrected. Because each modal carries several meanings, the safe
method is to read the whole sentence, name the meaning you need — ability,
permission, obligation, deduction, advice — and only then pick the word.

::: key The five grammatical rules that never change
1. A modal takes **no -s** in the third person: *He **can** swim*, not ~~cans~~.
2. A modal is followed by the **bare infinitive** (V1, no *to*): *She **must
   go***, not ~~must to go~~. Only *ought* keeps *to*.
3. Questions and negatives need **no *do***: *Can he drive?* / *He cannot drive.*
4. **Two modals cannot stand together**: ~~will can~~ → *will **be able to***;
   ~~must have to~~ → *must* **or** *have to*, never both.
5. Modals have **no infinitive, no -ing and no participle**; for the missing
   forms use a semi-modal: *to be able to, having had to, will have to*.
:::

## 22.1 The nine modals and what each one means

| Modal | Meanings it carries | Example | Past / substitute form |
|---|---|---|---|
| **can** | ability; permission (informal); general possibility; request | *She **can** speak Maithili.* | could; was/were able to; be allowed to |
| **could** | past ability; polite request; present possibility; past permission | ***Could** you lend me your notes?* | could have + V3 |
| **may** | permission (formal); possibility (about 50 per cent); wish | *You **may** leave after the bell.* | might; was/were allowed to |
| **might** | weaker possibility; very polite request; past of *may* in reporting | *It **might** rain this evening.* | might have + V3 |
| **will** | future; willingness; promise; strong prediction; characteristic habit | *I **will** carry your bag.* | would |
| **would** | polite request/offer; past habit; unreal situations; past of *will* | ***Would** you like some tea?* | would have + V3 |
| **shall** | offers and suggestions with *I / we*; formal legal obligation | ***Shall** I open the window?* | should |
| **should** | advice; expectation; obligation (weaker than *must*) | *You **should** see a doctor.* | should have + V3 |
| **must** | strong obligation; prohibition (*mustn't*); confident deduction | *He **must** be over sixty.* | had to (obligation); must have + V3 (deduction) |

## 22.2 The meanings, one by one

### Ability

*Can* for the present, *could* for general ability in the past, and *be able to*
wherever a modal cannot go.

- *Bishal **can** solve any sum in his head.*
- *At fourteen I **could** cycle from Butwal to Bhairahawa.* (general past ability)
- *After three attempts the team **was able to** reach the summit.* (one specific
  success — here *could* is wrong)
- *By next year she **will be able to** drive.* (no two modals, so *be able to*)

::: caution Ability: the two errors that are everywhere
✗ *He can able to speak Chinese.* → ✓ *He **can speak** Chinese.* / ✓ *He **is
able to speak** Chinese.* You may use **one** of them, never both.
✗ *We could not able to finish the paper.* → ✓ *We **could not finish** the
paper.* / ✓ *We **were not able to finish** the paper.*
✗ *I will can help you.* → ✓ *I **will be able to** help you.*
:::

### Permission and prohibition

| Function | Words used | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Asking permission | *Can I …?* (informal), *Could I …?* (polite), *May I …?* (formal), *Might I …?* (very formal) | ***May I** come in, sir?* |
| Giving permission | *can, may, be allowed to* | *You **may** use the library after four.* |
| Refusing permission | *cannot, may not* | *You **may not** take the question paper out.* |
| Prohibition (a rule, an order) | *must not* | *Candidates **mustn't** carry mobile phones.* |
| **No** obligation (free to choose) | *needn't, don't have to* | *You **needn't** come on Saturday.* |

::: key *Mustn't* and *needn't* are opposites, not twins
*You **mustn't** park here* = it is forbidden.
*You **needn't** park here* = you are free not to; there is no need.
Every year a candidate writes *needn't* where the sentence means "forbidden".
:::

### Obligation and necessity

- **must** — the obligation comes from the speaker, or is felt inside: *I **must**
  stop wasting time.*
- **have to / has to** — the obligation comes from outside: rules, the timetable,
  other people: *We **have to** wear the college uniform.*
- **had to** — the past of both. *Must* has no past form: *Yesterday I **had to**
  work late* (never ~~I must worked~~).
- **should / ought to** — a duty that is weaker than *must*: *A citizen **ought
  to** pay tax.*
- **be to** — a formal instruction or arrangement: *You **are to** report to the
  ward office at ten.*

### Advice and suggestion

| Strength | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| gentle | *could*, *might* | *You **could** try the evening class.* |
| normal | *should*, *ought to* | *You **should** revise the whole unit.* |
| strong, about now | *had better* (+ bare infinitive) | *You**'d better** leave now or you'll miss the bus.* |
| suggestion, including the speaker | *shall we, let's, why don't we* | ***Shall we** start?* |
| preference | *would rather* (+ bare infinitive) | *I**'d rather** walk than wait.* |

*Had better* is about **one particular occasion** and hints at a bad result if the
advice is ignored; *should* is a general opinion. The negative is ***had better
not***: *You'd better not tell him.*

- ***must*** — the only possible explanation: *The lights are off; they **must**
  have gone out.*
- ***can't / cannot*** — the impossible explanation. It is the negative of
  deductive *must*, **not** *mustn't*: *He **can't** be in Jumla; I saw him in
  Kathmandu an hour ago.*
- ***may / might / could*** — one of several possibilities: *She **may** be in the
  library.* *It **might** snow tonight.*
- ***can*** for **general** possibility (what sometimes happens): *Jumla **can**
  be bitterly cold in Magh.* Compare the **particular** possibility: *It **may**
  be cold in Jumla tomorrow.*

### Habit, willingness and characteristic behaviour

- **used to + V1** — a past habit or state that has stopped: *There **used to** be
  a wooden bridge here.*
- **would + V1** — a repeated past *action* in a description (not for states):
  *Every Dashain grandmother **would** make selroti for the whole village.*
- **will** for a present characteristic habit: *He **will** sit reading for hours.*
- **be used to + V-ing** — accustomed to (a different structure altogether): *She
  **is used to walking** three kilometres to school.*

## 22.3 Semi-modals

Semi-modals supply the forms the nine modals lack, and add meanings of their own.

| Semi-modal | Meaning | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **have to / has to / had to** | external obligation | *I **had to** renew my licence.* | supplies the past and future of *must* |
| **be able to** | ability | *He **has been able to** walk since March.* | supplies the missing forms of *can* |
| **be allowed to** | permission | *We **were allowed to** leave early.* | past of permission *can/may* |
| **ought to** | duty, expectation | *You **ought to** thank her.* | the only modal with *to* |
| **had better** | urgent advice | *We**'d better** hurry.* | bare infinitive; negative *had better not* |
| **would rather** | preference | *I**'d rather** not say.* | bare infinitive |
| **used to** | discontinued past habit | *He **used to** smoke.* | question/negative with *did*: *Did he use to smoke?* |
| **be supposed to** | what is expected or arranged | *You **are supposed to** wear a helmet.* | often implies it is not being done |
| **need** | necessity | *You **needn't** shout.* (modal) / *You **don't need to** shout.* (main verb) | modal *need* has no -s and takes V1 |
| **dare** | courage | *He **dare not** complain.* (modal) / *He **didn't dare to** complain.* (main verb) | rare except in fixed phrases |
| **be going to** | intention, prediction from evidence | *She **is going to** apply for the scholarship.* | not a modal, but tested with them |

## 22.4 Past and perfect forms — modal + have + V3

A modal followed by *have* + past participle talks about the **past**. Each
combination says something precise about whether the action actually happened.

| Perfect modal | Meaning | Did it happen? |
|---|---|---|
| **must have + V3** | I am sure it happened | yes |
| **can't / couldn't have + V3** | I am sure it did not happen | no |
| **may / might have + V3** | possibly it happened | unknown |
| **could have + V3** | it was possible, but it did not happen (or: unused ability) | usually no |
| **should / ought to have + V3** | it was the right thing, and it was not done | no — and that was a mistake |
| **shouldn't have + V3** | it was done, and it was wrong | yes |
| **needn't have + V3** | it was done, but it was unnecessary | yes |
| **didn't need to + V1** | it was unnecessary, so normally it was not done | usually no |
| **would have + V3** | an unreal past result (see Unit 28) | no |

Compare the pair the board loves:

- *I **needn't have taken** an umbrella — it did not rain.* (I took it; it was a
  waste)

  did not take it)

## 22.5 The mistakes Nepali speakers make most

| ✗ Wrong | ✓ Right | Rule broken |
|---|---|---|
| I must have to go now. | I **must** go now. / I **have to** go now. | never two obligation words together |
| You should have to submit it today. | You **should** submit it today. | *should* is complete by itself |
| He can able to drive. | He **can drive.** / He **is able to drive.** | *can* + bare infinitive |
| She could not able to attend. | She **could not attend.** / She **was not able to attend.** | same rule in the negative |
| I will can meet you. | I **will be able to** meet you. | two modals are impossible |
| He cans play the madal. | He **can** play the madal. | a modal takes no -s |
| We should to respect our teachers. | We **should respect** our teachers. | no *to* after *should* |
| You had better to consult a doctor. | You **had better consult** a doctor. | *had better* + bare infinitive |
| Does he can swim? | **Can** he swim? | modals need no *do* |
| May be he is at home. | **Maybe** he is at home. / He **may be** at home. | *maybe* (one word) = perhaps |
| Yesterday I must submit the form. | Yesterday I **had to** submit the form. | *must* has no past |
| I am used to go there on foot. | I **used to go** there on foot. / I **am used to going** there on foot. | two different structures |
| Would you mind to open the door? | Would you mind **opening** the door? | *mind* + V-ing |
| He mustn't worry, there is no hurry. | He **needn't** worry, there is no hurry. | *mustn't* forbids; *needn't* releases |

::: example Worked item 22.1 — choosing between *could* and *was able to*
**Problem.** Fill in with the correct form: *The house was burning fiercely, but
the fire brigade ____ rescue everyone inside.*

**Solution — step by step.**

1. **Name the meaning.** This is about ability in the past.
2. **General ability or one particular success?** The sentence describes **one**
   difficult occasion on which the rescue actually succeeded.
3. **Apply the rule.** *Could* is used for a general past ability ("I could swim
   at five"). For a single achievement — managing to do something on one occasion
   — English uses ***was / were able to*** or *managed to*.
4. **Fit the subject.** *The fire brigade* is singular → *was*.

**Answer.** *… but the fire brigade **was able to** rescue everyone inside.*

(In the **negative**, *couldn't* is perfectly correct even for a single occasion:
*They couldn't rescue the cat.*)
:::

::: example Worked item 22.2 — a deduction about the past
**Problem.** Rewrite using a modal: *I am sure Rita did not take my umbrella,
because she left before the rain started.*

**Solution — step by step.**

1. **Name the meaning.** The speaker is **sure** about something in the **past**,
   and the certainty is **negative**.
2. **Choose the modal.** Certainty that something is false is *can't* (or
   *couldn't*), never *mustn't*. *Mustn't* means "it is forbidden".
3. **Make it past.** A modal is made past by adding *have* + past participle.

**Answer.** *Rita **can't have taken** my umbrella; she left before the rain
started.*

The positive twin would be: *Rita **must have taken** my umbrella.*
:::

::: example Worked item 22.3 — correcting a sentence with several modal faults
**Problem.** Correct: *Since I must have to reach the airport by six, I think I
will can book a taxi, but you should to come with me, isn't it?*

**Solution — one fault at a time.**

1. *must have to* stacks two obligation forms. Keep one: *have to* (the deadline
   is external).
2. *will can* stacks two modals. Replace the second with its semi-modal: *will be
   able to* — but here the plain future is simpler: *I will book a taxi.*
3. *should to come* — *should* takes the bare infinitive: *should come*.
4. The tag must match the statement. The main clause is *you should come*, so the
   tag is *shouldn't you?*

**Answer.** *Since I **have to** reach the airport by six, I think I **will book**
a taxi, but you **should come** with me, **shouldn't you?***
:::

## Chapter summary

- The nine modals are *can, could, may, might, will, would, shall, should, must*;
  they take no -s, no *do*, and a **bare infinitive**.
- Two modals never stand together: use a semi-modal (*will be able to*, *have
  to*).
- Ability: *can / could* for general ability, *was/were able to* for a single past
  success, *be able to* for the missing forms.
- Permission: *can < could < may < might* in formality. Prohibition is *mustn't*;
  absence of obligation is *needn't / don't have to*.
- Obligation: *must* (from the speaker) vs *have to* (from outside); the past of
  both is *had to*.
- Advice: *should / ought to*, and *had better* + bare infinitive for urgent
  advice on one occasion.
- Certainty scale: **must** (sure it is true) > *will / should* > *may* > *might /
  could* > **can't** (sure it is false).
- Past habit: *used to* + V1 (habits and states), *would* + V1 (repeated actions
  only); *be used to* + V-ing means "accustomed to".
- Perfect modals: *must have* (it happened), *can't have* (it did not), *may/might
  have* (unknown), *should have* (it did not, and should have), *needn't have* (it
  did, unnecessarily).

::: note Answers
**1.** can — present ability.
**2.** May (or Could) — asking formal permission.
**3.** must — *must not* states a prohibition.
**4.** needn't — there is no necessity; *mustn't* would wrongly forbid hurrying.
**5.** must — the only likely explanation, so a confident deduction.
**6.** can't — certainty that something is false is *can't*, not *mustn't*.
**7.** had to — *must* has no past form.
**8.** used — *used to be* is a discontinued past state.
**9.** had — *had better* + bare infinitive gives urgent advice.
**10.** were able — one particular success in the past, so *were able to*.
:::
