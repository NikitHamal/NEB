Question 5 is worth eight marks, and it is the most predictable question on the paper.
The examiner is not asking you to think of something interesting to say. He is asking
whether you know the shape of a document that real people send to real offices: where
the date goes, how a stranger is addressed, what a subject line is for, and how a
letter ends when you do not know the reader's name. Those conventions are fixed, they
can be learnt in an evening, and they carry half the marks.

::: key Format first, then content
A brilliant argument in the wrong layout loses the format marks and cannot get them
back. Before you write a word, decide which of the four forms is being asked for —
letter to the editor, email, application with CV, or letter of complaint or request —
and lay the page out for that form.
:::

## 32.1 What Question 5 asks, and how the eight marks fall

The question names the form and the reader:

- *Write a letter to the editor of a national daily about the condition of …*
- *Write an email to your head teacher requesting …*
- *Write an application for the post of … advertised in … Attach your CV.*
- *Write a letter to the manager of … complaining about …*

| What is credited | Marks | What that means |
|---|---|---|
| **Format** | 2 | Addresses, date, salutation, subject, close, signature — each in its place |
| **Content** | 3 | Every point the question names, plus what the reader needs in order to act |
| **Organisation** | 1 | One idea to a paragraph, in a sensible order |
| **Language** | 2 | Formal register held throughout; accurate sentences |

**Length 150–200 words of body. Time about eighteen minutes**, of which two go on
laying out the page before you begin.

## 32.2 Choosing the register

Register is the level of formality. Question 5 is almost always formal, and the
commonest Grade 12 fault is a body that slides from formal into friendly halfway
through.

| Friendly | Neutral | Formal — use this |
|---|---|---|
| I'm writing about | I am writing about | I am writing to draw your attention to |
| sorry about | apologies for | I regret any inconvenience caused |
| Can you fix it? | Please fix it. | I should be grateful if the matter could be looked into |
| get in touch | contact me | I may be contacted on the number given above |
| Thanks a lot | Thank you | Thank you for your time and consideration |
| a big problem | a serious problem | a matter of considerable concern to residents |
| ASAP | soon | at your earliest convenience |

::: caution The seven phrases to strike out of your English
- ***Myself Ramesh Thapa.*** Write *I am Ramesh Thapa* or, better, say who you are at
  the end and open with the issue.
- ***Kindly do the needful.*** Say what the needful is.
- ***Your's faithfully / Yours' sincerely.*** There is no apostrophe: ***Yours***.
- ***Respected Sir.*** Use *Dear Sir* or *Dear Sir/Madam*.
- ***I beg to state that…*** A hundred years out of date.
- ***Please reply back.*** *Reply* already means back.
- ***I have no words to express…*** in an official letter. You are asking for a street
  light, not writing a poem.
:::

## 32.3 The letter to the editor

This is the form the NEB paper asks for most often, and the one candidates lay out
least well. Remember what it actually is: a letter addressed to *one* person, the
editor, but written to be read by *thousands*. So it must be short, it must be about a
matter of public concern, and it must ask for something.

Four rules that separate a printed letter from a rejected one:

1. **One issue only.** A letter that complains about roads, load-shedding and the
   examination system at once will be cut by the sub-editor.
2. **Say when and where.** *The lights on the Milanchowk stretch have been dark since
   Baisakh* is evidence; *there are many problems* is not.
3. **Name the person who can act** — the ward office, the municipality, the department —
   and say exactly what they should do.
4. **Sign with your full name and place.** Newspapers do not print anonymous letters,
   and NEB examiners do not give format marks for an unsigned one.

::: example Model answer 32.1 — a letter to the editor
**Question.** Write a letter to the editor of a national daily drawing attention to the
danger caused by unlit streets in your ward. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Model answer.**

> Ward No. 7, Milanchowk
> Butwal Sub-Metropolitan City
> 12 Asar 2082
>
> The Editor
> The Himalayan Times
> Kathmandu
>
> Dear Sir/Madam,
>
> **Subject: Unlit streets in Ward No. 7, Butwal**
>
> Through the columns of your esteemed daily, I wish to draw the attention of the
> concerned authorities to the state of street lighting in Ward No. 7 of Butwal.
>
> The four hundred metres of road between Milanchowk and the health post have been in
> darkness since the middle of Baisakh, when a transformer was replaced. Of the
> fourteen poles on that stretch, only three are now lit. The road is used every
> evening by students returning from tuition and by patients walking to the health
> post, and two motorcycle accidents have already been reported at the unlit bend near
> the pipal tree. Residents have complained twice at the ward office and have received
> no reply.
>
> I should therefore be grateful if the municipality would restore the lights on this
> stretch before the monsoon, when the road becomes more dangerous still. A single
> day's work by the electricity office would remove a risk that the whole ward now
> lives with.
>
> Yours sincerely,
>
> Sabin Gurung
> Milanchowk, Butwal

**Why it scores.** Both addresses and the date are in place; the subject line is one
line; there is one issue and no other; the middle paragraph carries dates, distances
and numbers instead of adjectives; the request names who must act and by when; and
*esteemed daily*, *I should be grateful*, *the concerned authorities* hold the register
from first line to last.
:::

## 32.4 The formal email

An email is a letter with the envelope printed at the top. The header does the work
the addresses used to do, so you do not repeat them — but everything below the header
stays as formal as a letter.

```figure caption="A formal email. The header fields are labels, not sentences: no full stops, no greeting inside the Subject box."
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
import matplotlib.patches as mpatches
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(5.0, 3.3))
ax.axis("off")
ax.add_patch(mpatches.FancyBboxPatch((0.35, 0.4), 8.1, 7.6,
             boxstyle="round,pad=0.0,rounding_size=0.12",
             linewidth=1.0, edgecolor=GRID, facecolor="#ffffff"))
rows = [("To:", "principal@jsss.edu.np", 7.35),
        ("Cc:", "classteacher12a@jsss.edu.np", 6.80),
        ("Subject:", "Permission for blood donation camp", 6.25)]
for k, v, y in rows:
    ax.plot([0.35, 8.45], [y - 0.26, y - 0.26], lw=0.7, color=GRID)
    ax.text(0.70, y, k, fontsize=6.3, color=MUTED)
    ax.text(2.05, y, v, fontsize=6.3, color=INK)
ax.text(0.70, 5.55, "Dear Sir,", fontsize=6.4, color=INK)
for y in [5.00, 4.68, 4.36]:
    ax.plot([0.70, 8.10 if y > 4.4 else 6.30], [y, y], lw=2.4,
            color=GRID, solid_capstyle="butt")

ax.text(0.70, 1.80, "Anisha Karki", fontsize=6.4, color=INK)
ax.text(0.70, 1.35, "Class 12 'A', Roll No. 14", fontsize=6.0, color=MUTED)
ax.text(0.70, 0.90, "98########", fontsize=6.0, color=MUTED)
ax.annotate("say what you want\nin the subject line",
            xy=(7.45, 6.25), xytext=(9.10, 5.05), fontsize=5.9, color=MUTED,

```

| Header field | Rule |
|---|---|
| **To** | one reader; the person who can actually decide |
| **Cc** | people who need to know but need not act — a class teacher, a supervisor |
| **Subject** | six to ten words naming the action: *Request for two days' leave*, not *Leave* and not *Hello sir* |
| **Attachment** | mention it in the body: *I have attached my CV and transcript.* |

::: example Model answer 32.2 — a formal email
**Question.** You are the secretary of your school's social service club. Write an email
to your principal asking permission to hold a blood donation camp in the school hall.
<span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Model answer.**

> **To:** principal@janajyoti.edu.np
> **Cc:** socialservice@janajyoti.edu.np
> **Subject:** Request for permission to hold a blood donation camp on 5 Shrawan
>
> Dear Sir,
>
> I am writing on behalf of the Social Service Club to seek your permission to hold a
> one-day blood donation camp in the school hall on Friday, 5 Shrawan 2082, between
> 10 a.m. and 3 p.m.
>
> The Nepal Red Cross Society, Butwal branch, has agreed to send two technicians and
> all the necessary equipment at no cost to the school. Twenty-six students of Grades
> 11 and 12 have already registered as donors, and the club will arrange the tables,
> the registration desk and the refreshments. We should need the hall from 8 a.m. for
> setting up, and the permission of the class teachers for the six volunteers who will
> be on duty during the fifth and sixth periods.
>
> I have attached the Red Cross letter of consent and a copy of the programme schedule
> for your kind approval. I should be grateful for a reply by Sunday so that the
> society can confirm its team.
>
> Thank you for your time and consideration.
>
> Yours sincerely,
>
> Anisha Karki
> Secretary, Social Service Club
> Class 12 'A', Roll No. 14

**Why it scores.** The subject line contains the request and the date; the first
sentence states purpose and date together; the second paragraph answers the four
questions a principal will ask (who is coming, what it costs, who is involved, what is
needed from the school); the attachment is named; and a deadline for the reply is given
politely.
:::

## 32.5 The application and the CV

An application is a covering letter. It does not repeat the CV; it tells the reader
*which two facts in the CV matter for this post* and where you saw the advertisement.

**The covering letter, in four paragraphs:**

1. Post applied for, and where and when you saw it advertised.
2. Your qualification — the single most relevant one, with the year and the board.
3. What you have actually done: the internship, the tuition you taught, the shop you
   helped run, the software you can use.
4. A closing offering yourself for interview, and naming the enclosures.

```photo id="eng11/cv" caption="A professionally set CV. A school leaver's CV is shorter than this one, but the architecture is identical: name and contact details at the top, a two-line profile, then skills, then experience — each under a heading, each in reverse date order." width="88"
crop 0.02 0.58 0.98 0.99
```

**The CV, in seven headings.** Never in paragraphs — a CV is read in thirty seconds.

| Heading | What goes under it |
|---|---|
| **Personal details** | full name, address, telephone, email (a plain one: *sabin.gurung@gmail.com*) |
| **Career objective** | one sentence, aimed at *this* post |
| **Academic qualifications** | a small table: examination, board, year, division — newest first |
| **Work experience** | post, organisation, dates, one line on what you did |
| **Skills** | languages, computer packages, licences — only what you can prove |
| **Extra-curricular activities** | posts held, prizes, volunteering |
| **References** | two people, with designation and telephone, who have agreed |

Sir/Madam*; the advertisement is identified by paper and date; the experience paragraph
gives duties rather than adjectives; the CV is in headings and reverse date order; and
nothing in the CV is repeated word for word in the letter.
:::

::: tip Sincerely or faithfully?
*Dear Mr Sharma* → **Yours sincerely.** *Dear Sir / Dear Sir or Madam* (you do not
know the name) → **Yours faithfully.** A letter to the editor may use either, because
the editor is a named office; *Yours sincerely* is the safer choice. Capital **Y**,
small **s** or **f**, and never an apostrophe.
:::

## 32.6 The letter of complaint or request

A complaint that sounds angry gets a polite reply and no action. A complaint that
sounds like a file note gets acted on, because the reader can see exactly what has to
be put right.

**The five facts a complaint must contain:** what you bought or used, when, where,
proof (bill number, ticket number, receipt date), what went wrong, and what you want —
repair, replacement, refund or apology. Name one of those four; do not leave the remedy
to the reader's imagination.

::: example Model answer 32.4 — a letter of complaint
**Question.** Write a letter to the manager of an online store complaining about a
damaged article and asking for a replacement. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Model answer.**

> Milanchowk, Butwal-7
> 20 Shrawan 2082
>
> The Customer Service Manager
> Daraj Nepal Pvt. Ltd.
> Naxal, Kathmandu
>
> Dear Sir/Madam,
>
> **Subject: Damaged goods delivered against Order No. DN-4471902**
>
> I am writing about a scientific calculator delivered to me on 18 Shrawan 2082 against
> Order No. DN-4471902, placed on your website on 14 Shrawan.
>
> The parcel arrived in a torn carton with no inner packing. The calculator inside has
> a cracked display, and six of the keys do not respond at all, so the article is of no
> use to me. I photographed the parcel before opening it, and the photographs and the
> delivery slip are attached to this letter. The invoice amount was Rs 1,850, paid in
> advance by mobile banking.
>
> As the item is within the seven-day replacement period stated on your website, I
> request you to replace it with a new piece of the same model, or to refund the amount
> in full if the model is out of stock. I should be grateful if the matter could be
> settled within a week, as I need the calculator for my examination on 3 Bhadra.
>
> Yours faithfully,
>
> Sabin Gurung
> 98########

**Why it scores.** The order number is in the subject line, where a clerk will look for
it; dates, amount and payment method are all given; the evidence is named and attached;
the remedy offers the company a choice; and the deadline is justified by a reason
rather than by irritation.
:::

## 32.7 Marking checklist for Question 5

| | Check | Mark at risk |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Is it the **form** the question asked for — letter, email, application? | Format |
| 2 | Sender's address top right; receiver's address left, below the date? | Format |
| 3 | Is the date written out (*12 Asar 2082*), not *12/3/82*? | Format |
| 4 | Is there a one-line **subject**? | Format |
| 5 | Does the close match the salutation — *Sir* → *faithfully*, name → *sincerely*? | Format |
| 6 | Is the full name written below the signature? | Format |
| 7 | Has every point named in the question been answered? | Content |
| 8 | Are there dates, numbers or reference numbers, not just adjectives? | Content |
| 9 | Is the remedy or request stated in one clear sentence? | Content |
| 10 | One idea to a paragraph; three or four paragraphs in all? | Organisation |
| 11 | Any *myself*, *do the needful*, *respected sir*, *reply back*? Delete. | Language |
| 12 | Same level of formality in the last line as in the first? | Language |

## Chapter summary

- Question 5 is 8 marks: format 2, content 3, organisation 1, language 2. Write 150–200
  words in about eighteen minutes.
- Decide the form before you write; lay the page out first.
- Letter to the editor: your address and date top right, the editor's address left,
  *Dear Sir/Madam*, a subject line, one issue, evidence with dates and numbers, a named
  remedy, full name and place.
- Email: the header replaces the addresses. The subject line names the action; Cc is for
  people who need to know but not to act; attachments are mentioned in the body.
- Application: four paragraphs — the post and where it was advertised, the
  qualification, what you have done, availability and enclosures. The CV goes under
  seven headings in reverse date order.
- Complaint: what, when, proof, what went wrong, what you want. Choose one of repair,
  replacement, refund, apology.
- *Dear Sir* → *Yours faithfully*; *Dear Mr Sharma* → *Yours sincerely*. No apostrophe
  in *Yours*.
- Formal register all the way down: *I should be grateful if…*, *at your earliest
  convenience*, *Thank you for your time and consideration*.
