A child comes running in with news. *I'll tell you how the sun rose* — and out it
comes, in a rush, in pictures, in the wrong grammar, with a shout at the end.
Then the child stops. *But how he set, I know not.* The second half of the poem
is spoken more slowly, in a lowered voice, and by the last line something has
happened that the speaker does not quite have words for. Emily Dickinson's "A
Day" is sixteen short lines long, uses almost no word of more than two
syllables, and is one of the most quietly frightening poems in your course. Read
it once for the colours. Read it again for what happens to the voice.

::: key What the exam wants from this poem
The two halves of the poem — sunrise **told** and sunset **not known** — and why
the difference matters. The four picture-making lines: the *ribbon*, the
*steeples swam in amethyst*, the *news like squirrels ran*, the *hills untied
their bonnets*. The four quatrains, the **abcb** rhyme, and the hymn-book metre
Dickinson grew up singing. The sunset allegory: stile, yellow children, dominie,
bars, flock. And the fact that "A Day" is not Dickinson's title.
:::

## 8.1 About the poet

Emily Elizabeth Dickinson (1830–1886) was born, lived and died in the same house
in Amherst, a small college town in Massachusetts, in the United States. Her
grandfather had helped to found Amherst College; her father was its treasurer
and a member of Congress. She was well educated for a woman of her time — Amherst
Academy, then a year at Mount Holyoke Female Seminary — and then she came home,
and gradually stopped going out. By her forties she rarely left the house and
spoke to some visitors from behind a door. She wore white. Her world shrank to a
garden, a bedroom, a family and an enormous correspondence.

Inside that small world she wrote about **1,800 poems**. She published perhaps
ten of them in her lifetime, most of them anonymously and all of them altered by
editors who thought her punctuation was a mistake. She stitched the rest into
little hand-sewn booklets, now called *fascicles*, and put them in a drawer. Her
sister Lavinia found them after her death. The first selection, *Poems by Emily
Dickinson*, appeared in **1890**, edited by **Mabel Loomis Todd** and **Thomas
Wentworth Higginson**.

Your textbook's note is right about the ingredients of her mind: the
**Metaphysical poets** of seventeenth-century England (Donne, Herbert), with
their habit of yoking a huge idea to a homely object; the **Book of
Revelation**, with its purple, its jewels and its last things; and the
**Calvinist Puritan** religion of New England, which she never comfortably
accepted and never escaped. She and **Walt Whitman**, working at the same time
and in complete ignorance of each other, are the two poets who broke American
poetry away from its English models — he by making the line enormous, she by
making it tiny.

::: note A note on how her poems are numbered
Because Dickinson did not publish, her poems have no titles and no fixed order.
Scholars refer to them by number. "A Day" is **J318** in Thomas H. Johnson's
1955 edition and **F204** in R. W. Franklin's 1998 edition. If you meet
"I'll tell you how the sun rose" as a first line anywhere, it is this poem.
:::

## 8.2 The poem: text, title and date

Three facts about the text in front of you, all of them examinable.

**The title is not Dickinson's.** She left the poem untitled, as she left almost
all her poems untitled. "A Day" was supplied by Todd and Higginson in 1890, who
also grouped it under the heading *Nature*. The title is therefore an editor's
reading of the poem — a helpful one, but not evidence of what the poet intended.

**The text is the 1890 editors' text.** The version in your textbook descends
from the 1890 printing, which smoothed Dickinson's spelling, her capitals and
above all her dashes. In her manuscript line 11 begins *That* little yellow boys
and girls; the printed tradition gives *Which*. Do not lose marks over this — the
textbook's word is the one to quote — but know that it happened.

**That mark in line one is a dash.** The textbook prints:

> I'll tell you how the sun rose, ___

Dickinson wrote a comma and then one of her long dashes. Your textbook's
Reference-to-the-context question calls it "the hyphen"; the safe thing in the
exam is to answer the question as asked while describing what the mark actually
does, which is what §8.11 below models. A dash is Dickinson's fingerprint. It is
a held breath, not a stop.

The poem was written about **1860** and first printed in **1890**, four years
after her death. It is in the public domain, which is why it can be given here
in full.

## 8.3 The poem, stanza by stanza

### Stanza 1 — the sunrise, in four pictures

> I'll tell you how the sun rose, ___
> A ribbon at a time.
> The steeples swam in amethyst,
> The news like squirrels ran.

The poem opens on a **promise to a listener**: *I'll tell you*. That is the voice
of a child who has seen something and cannot keep it. It is also, quietly, the
voice of a teacher — and by the end of the poem we shall meet a real teacher.

*A ribbon at a time* is the first metaphor and the best one. Dawn does not
arrive; it is issued, in narrow horizontal bands, one after another, like ribbon
being unrolled off a bolt in a draper's shop. The phrase makes the sunrise both
slow and domestic.

*The steeples swam in amethyst* takes the eye upward. Church spires are the
tallest things in a New England town, so they catch the light first. **Amethyst**
is a violet quartz used in jewellery — but it is also, in the Book of
Revelation, one of the twelve stones of the wall of the heavenly city. So the
line is doing two things at once: it is reporting a purple sky, and it is
hinting that the town has been briefly built into heaven. And *swam* turns the
air into water; the steeples are not standing in the light, they are moving
through it.

*The news like squirrels ran* is the only simile in the poem, and the liveliest
line in it. The "news" is the light spreading downward from the steeples to the
streets and gardens. It runs the way a squirrel runs — not in a straight line,
but in bursts, up and along and round, stopping and starting.

### Stanza 2 — the hills, the birds, and a child's conclusion

> The hills untied their bonnets,
> The bobolinks begun.
> Then I said softly to myself,
> "That must have been the sun!"

*The hills untied their bonnets* is a personification so exact that it is worth
slowing down over. A **bonnet** is a woman's or a baby's hat tied with strings
under the chin. Before dawn the hills are wrapped in mist; as the light comes
the mist loosens and lifts off the tops. The hills are therefore women taking off
their hats — which is what New England women did when they came indoors, or when
the service ended. The world is waking up and getting comfortable.

*The bobolinks begun* is the famous piece of bad grammar. A **bobolink** is a
North American songbird with a bubbling, tumbling song. "Begun" for "began" is
the past tense a child uses, and also the form you find in hymns and folk songs.
Dickinson uses it deliberately: it keeps the speaker young. The line is also an
**ellipsis** — begun *what*? Singing. The verb is missing because the speaker is
too excited to supply it.

Then the voice drops. *Then I said softly to myself* — softly, and to myself:
this is a private conclusion, not an announcement. And the conclusion is
childlike to the point of comedy: **"That must have been the sun!"** The speaker
has watched the whole performance and only worked out afterwards, by deduction,
what caused it. It is charming. It is also the first warning that this speaker
understands less than she is describing.

### Stanza 3 — the turn

> But how he set, I know not.
> There seemed a purple stile
> Which little yellow boys and girls
> Were climbing all the while

*But* is the hinge of the poem. Eight lines of confident telling; now, **I know
not**. Notice what has changed grammatically: the sun, an "it" by implication in
stanza 1, is now **he** — personified, given a will, and gone somewhere the
speaker cannot follow.

Everything in the second half is hedged. *There **seemed** a purple stile* — she
will not swear to it. A **stile** is a set of steps built into a field wall or
fence so that people can climb over where animals cannot. So the horizon at
evening becomes a boundary with a way over it: you can cross, but only on foot,
one at a time, and you cannot bring the flock.

*Little yellow boys and girls* are the last rays of the sun, or the small
gold-lit evening clouds, seen as children going home over the wall. Once you
have accepted that image the rest of the poem follows inevitably: if the lights
are children, then something must come and fetch them.

### Stanza 4 — the dominie

> Till when they reached the other side,
> A dominie in gray
> Put gently up the evening bars,
> And led the flock away.

A **dominie** is a schoolmaster in Scots usage and a minister of religion in
American usage — and Dickinson, who knew both senses, chose the word because it
holds both. He is *in gray*: the colour of dusk, of a Puritan minister's coat,
and of nothing in particular. He has no face.

*Put gently up the evening bars* is a farm image. The **bars** are the movable
poles that close a gap in a fence once the animals are inside for the night. To
"put up the bars" is to shut the field. It is done **gently** — the poem insists
on this — and it is done from the far side, after the children have crossed.

*And led the flock away.* "Flock" completes the transformation: the children
became sheep, the schoolmaster became a shepherd, and the shepherd leading his
flock is the oldest image in Christian writing for God and the dead. The last
line is four plain words and a full stop, and it is where the poem is going the
whole time.

## 8.4 Paraphrase in plain English

**Stanza 1.** Let me describe the sunrise to you. The light came in narrow strips,
one strip after another. The church spires seemed to float in a violet-coloured
glow, and the brightness spread quickly and jerkily over the town, the way
squirrels dart about.

**Stanza 2.** The morning mist lifted off the hilltops, the way a woman takes off
her bonnet. The bobolinks started singing. Only then did I say quietly to myself,
"So that was the sun coming up!"

**Stanza 3.** But I cannot tell you how the sun went down. I thought I could see
a purple set of steps over the horizon, and small golden figures — like children —
were climbing over them all the time.

**Stanza 4.** When at last they had all got across to the other side, a grey
figure like a schoolmaster (or a pastor) quietly closed the gate behind them and
took them away, as a shepherd takes his sheep home for the night.

## 8.5 Central idea and themes

**Central idea.** A whole day is described by a speaker who can explain the
morning perfectly and cannot explain the evening at all. Behind the day lies a
life: birth and childhood can be told, but death can only be imagined, and the
best the poem can do is to imagine it as a kind and orderly ending — a gate shut
gently, children led home.

**Nature as theatre.** Dickinson does not describe the sun; she describes what
the sun does to a town. Everything in the poem is a *reaction* — steeples,
squirrels, hills, birds. The cause is the one thing she cannot look at.

**Innocence and experience.** The speaker of stanzas 1–2 is a child with a story.
By stanza 4 she has watched something she cannot narrate. The poem enacts the
loss of confidence that comes with understanding. Your textbook puts it as a
movement "from innocence to experience", and that is a defensible summary
provided you can point to *But how he set, I know not* as the moment it happens.

**Death, handled gently.** The word "death" never occurs. Instead: a boundary
(stile), a crossing (climbing), an arrival (the other side), an authority in
grey, a closing (bars) and a departure (led the flock away). Dickinson wrote
dozens of poems about dying; this is one of the few that is not frightened.
Whether the dominie is God, a minister, night itself or simply Death is left
open, and the openness is the point.

**Faith and doubt.** The amethyst steeples put a church at the centre of the
morning, and a shepherd at the centre of the evening — the whole apparatus of
Christian consolation is present. But it is present as something *seen*, not
something known: "There **seemed** a purple stile." That gap between the
comforting picture and the honest verb is the most Dickinsonian thing in the
poem.

## 8.6 Form, metre, rhyme and structure

**Stanzas.** Four **quatrains**, sixteen lines, printed without a break in
sense between stanzas 3 and 4 (the sentence runs on across the gap).

**Rhyme scheme: abcb** in every stanza — that is, only the second and fourth
lines of each quatrain rhyme, and the rhyme does not carry from one stanza to
the next.

| Stanza | Line 2 | Line 4 | Rhyme |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | time | ran | **slant** (only the nasal consonance of *m* / *n*) |
| 2 | begun | sun | perfect |
| 3 | stile | while | perfect |
| 4 | gray | away | perfect |

The one imperfect rhyme is in the first stanza. Dickinson uses such **slant
rhymes** (also called half rhymes, near rhymes or, in her case, *partial
rhymes*) constantly, and here it has a job: the opening stanza is deliberately
breathless and slightly out of control, and the rhyme does not quite land.
Be careful of a common error in study notes: *ribbon* and *amethyst* are **not**
a rhyming pair — they sit at the ends of lines 2 and 3 in some printings but
carry no rhyme at all.

**Metre.** The poem is written in the **common metre** of the hymn book — the
metre of "Amazing Grace" — which alternates lines of eight and six syllables,
four iambic feet then three (**iambic tetrameter / iambic trimeter**). Dickinson
sang this metre every Sunday of her childhood, and most of her poems are in it or
close to it.

But she does not keep it exactly. Count:

| Stanza | L1 | L2 | L3 | L4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 7 | 6 | 8 | 6 |
| 2 | 7 | 6 | 8 | 6 |
| 3 | 7 | 6 | 8 | 6 |
| 4 | **8** | 6 | 8 | 6 |

In stanzas 1–3 the *first* line is a syllable short. Such a line is called
**headless** (or *acephalous*): the light opening syllable has been chopped off,
so the line starts on a stress — *I'LL tell you HOW the SUN rose*. The effect is
a small forward push, as though the speaker has begun before you were ready.
Only in the last stanza does the line fill out to its regulation eight
syllables, and the poem settles into perfect hymn metre exactly where its
subject becomes religious. That is not an accident.

**Punctuation and capitals.** The printed text uses ordinary punctuation, but

naming in an answer: the poem's two halves end on two opposite noises.

## 8.7 Imagery, figures of speech and sound devices

| Device | Where it occurs (exact words) | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| Metaphor | "A ribbon at a time" | makes dawn a slow domestic unrolling, not a sudden event |
| Metaphor / colour imagery | "The steeples swam in amethyst" | jewel-purple light; *swam* turns air to water; the stone is one of Revelation's twelve |
| Simile | "The news like squirrels ran" | the only *like* in the poem; darting, restless spread of light |
| Personification | "The hills untied their bonnets" | mist lifting becomes women removing hats; the landscape is at ease |
| Personification | "how **he** set" | the sun becomes a person with a will and a destination |
| Ellipsis / dialect | "The bobolinks begun" | the missing verb and the child's past tense keep the speaker young |
| Symbol | "a purple stile" | the horizon as a crossing place between two fields |
| Extended metaphor (allegory) | "little yellow boys and girls … the flock" | last rays as children, then as sheep: sunset becomes the end of a life |
| Biblical allusion | "A dominie in gray … led the flock away" | the Good Shepherd of Psalm 23 and John 10, unnamed |
| Rural metaphor | "Put gently up the evening bars" | the farm gate closed for the night; the adverb *gently* is the poem's whole attitude to death |
| Antithesis | "I'll tell you how the sun rose" / "But how he set, I know not" | the structural opposition on which the poem is built |
| Enjambment | "Were climbing all the while / Till when they reached …" | the sentence steps over the stanza break, as the children step over the stile |
| Alliteration | "**s**un **r**ose", "**s**teeples **s**wam", "**s**aid **s**oftly", "**l**ittle **y**ellow" | soft *s* sounds through the sunrise; the hush of stanza 2 |
| Assonance | "h**o**w the s**u**n r**o**se"; "l**i**ttle … cl**i**mbing … wh**i**le" | binds the lines aloud |
| Consonance | "ti**m**e" / "ra**n**" | the nasal near-rhyme that makes stanza 1 feel unfinished |
| Colour imagery | amethyst, yellow, gray | purple morning, gold evening, grey close: the poem fades as it ends |
| Auditory imagery | "The bobolinks begun" | the single sound in a poem otherwise entirely visual |

::: tip Naming the device correctly
*The hills untied their bonnets* is both a **metaphor** (mist = bonnet) and a
**personification** (hills = women). Either name earns the mark; naming both and
explaining the picture earns the whole mark. Never stop at the label — the
examiner wants "what it makes you see", not the word "personification" on its
own.
:::

## 8.8 Tone

The poem has two tones and the change between them is the poem.

Stanzas 1–2 are **chatty, excited and faintly comic**. The speaker buttonholes
you, talks fast, gets the grammar wrong and ends on an exclamation mark after a
conclusion anybody could have reached.

Stanzas 3–4 are **hushed, tentative and reverent**. The verbs go soft
(*seemed*), the adverb *gently* appears, and the last line is a statement of
fact with nothing added. There is no fear in it and no protest. The nearest word
for the closing tone is **awe** — the feeling of watching something both
beautiful and final that you are not being allowed to understand.

## 8.9 Critical appreciation

"A Day" is a miniature that behaves like an epic. In sixteen lines Dickinson
moves from a draper's ribbon to the Book of Revelation, from a squirrel on a
fence to the Good Shepherd, and she does it without one abstract noun.

The technique is her most characteristic one: she takes an enormous and
unspeakable subject and refuses to name it, describing instead a series of small
household or farmyard things that stand in its place. Death is never mentioned;
what we get is a gate, a grey coat, and the word *gently*. This is why the poem
survives re-reading. A poem that announced "the sun's setting is like dying"
would be exhausted in one reading. This one lets you find the meaning yourself,
and then makes you wonder whether you have put it there.

The choice of a **child's voice** is the second stroke. It does two jobs at
once. It justifies the freshness of the metaphors — only a child would see the
light as ribbon and the hills as hatted women — and it makes the ignorance of
the second half innocent rather than stupid. An adult who did not know how the
sun set would be a fool. A child who does not know is simply telling the truth,
and the truth is that none of us knows what the other side of the stile looks
like.

There is also a quiet argument with religion going on. Dickinson gives us the
whole comforting Christian picture of death — the shepherd, the flock, the far
side, the gentle keeper — and then sets it inside a verb she will not commit to:
*there **seemed** a purple stile*. She neither believes nor denies. She reports
what the evening looked like, and stops.

The poem's limitation, if you want to argue one, is the same as its strength:
the allegory of stanzas 3–4 is so complete and so orderly that it risks becoming
pretty. Some readers find the ending sentimental — a Victorian child's picture
of dying. The defence is *I know not*. The poem never claims the picture is
true; it claims only that this is what the speaker saw, softly, to herself.

## Chapter summary

Emily Dickinson (1830–1886) of Amherst, Massachusetts, wrote about 1,800 poems
and published almost none; "A Day" is J318 / F204, written about 1860, first
printed in 1890 and given its title by her editors Todd and Higginson, not by
her. Its sixteen lines are four abcb quatrains in something very close to the
hymn book's common metre, with the opening line of stanzas 1–3 a syllable short.
Stanzas 1–2 **tell** the sunrise in four bright metaphors — ribbon, amethyst
steeples, squirrel-news, hills untying bonnets — in the excited, ungrammatical
voice of a child, and end in an exclamation mark. At *But how he set, I know
not* the poem turns. Stanzas 3–4 do not tell the sunset; they guess at it, in a
hushed allegory of a purple stile, little yellow boys and girls climbing over,
and a grey dominie who gently puts up the evening bars and leads the flock away.
The day is a life, the stile is the boundary of death, and the shepherd is a
consolation the poem offers without quite promising. The central movement is
from knowledge to mystery, from innocence to experience, from noise to silence.

## Practice questions

### Understanding the text

**a. How does the poet describe the morning sun in the first stanza?**
<span class="marks">[2]</span>

She never describes the sun itself — only its effects. The light arrives "a
ribbon at a time", in narrow bands unrolled slowly one after another. It strikes
the church spires first, so that "the steeples swam in amethyst", floating in a
violet glow. Then it spreads down over the town quickly and unevenly, "like
squirrels", as though the news of morning were running from house to house.

**b. What does the line 'The news like squirrels ran' mean?**
<span class="marks">[2]</span>

The "news" is the news of daybreak — the light itself, and with it the knowledge
that morning has come. The simile compares its spread to squirrels running:
quick, darting, in short bursts with pauses, scattering in several directions at
once rather than advancing in a straight line. It captures exactly the way
sunlight jumps unevenly from roof to roof and tree to tree, and it fills the
stanza with small animal life.

**c. What do you understand by the line 'The hills untied their bonnets'?**
<span class="marks">[2]</span>

A bonnet is a hat tied with strings under the chin. Before sunrise the hilltops
are wrapped in mist as though wearing bonnets; as the sun warms the ground the
mist loosens and lifts away. Dickinson personifies the hills as women untying
and taking off their hats — a homely, domestic gesture that makes the landscape
seem to relax and become sociable now that the day has begun.

**d. Is the speaker watching the morning sun? Why? Why not?**
<span class="marks">[2]</span>

Not directly. Every image in stanzas 1 and 2 is of something the sun is doing to
*other* things — steeples, hills, birds, the town. The speaker's own conclusion,
"That must have been the sun!", is a **deduction** made afterwards, not a
report of something seen: the word *must* proves she inferred the cause from the
effects. She is watching the sunrise, not the sun. (This is also physically
true: one cannot look at the rising sun, and it is a child's habit to watch the
lit world instead.)

**e. How does the sun set?**
<span class="marks">[2]</span>

The speaker admits at once that she does not know — "But how he set, I know
not." What she offers instead is a guess in the form of a picture. There
*seemed* to be a purple stile at the horizon, and little yellow boys and girls —
the last golden rays, or the small lit evening clouds — were climbing over it
all the while. When they had all reached the other side, a grey figure like a
schoolmaster or pastor gently put up the evening bars, closing the gap in the
fence, and led them away like a shepherd with his flock. So the sunset is
described not as an event in nature but as children being taken home at the end
of school, and beneath that, as a gentle death.

### Reference to the context

**a. What, according to the speaker, is a day?**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

For this speaker a day is not a measure of time but a small complete story with
two unequal halves, and the shape of that story is the shape of a human life.

The first half is public, noisy and knowable. Dawn is something she can narrate
to somebody else — "I'll tell you how the sun rose" — and she narrates it in
four images drawn from a working town: a ribbon unrolled, steeples swimming in
amethyst, news running like squirrels, hills untying their bonnets. Everything
is busy, coloured and slightly comic, and it ends in the exclamation of a child
who has just worked out what caused the show.

The second half is private, hushed and unknowable. "But how he set, I know not."
A day, then, is something that begins in certainty and ends in mystery. The
evening is not described but imagined: a purple stile at the edge of the world,
little yellow children climbing over it, a dominie in grey who shuts the gate
behind them and leads the flock away.

Put the two halves together and the day becomes an allegory of a lifetime.
Morning is birth and childhood — loud, colourful, easy to tell. Evening is age
and death — quiet, uncertain, and managed by someone else. The stile is the
boundary between this world and whatever is on "the other side"; the flock and
the shepherd are the oldest Christian image of the dead in God's keeping. A day,
for Dickinson, is a life in miniature, and the most important thing she can say
about it is that we can report its beginning and can only guess at its end.

**b. What purpose does the hyphen in the first line serve in the poem?**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

First, a point of accuracy that will improve the answer: the mark Dickinson
wrote there is not a hyphen but a **dash** (a hyphen joins parts of a word; a
dash holds a sentence open). Your textbook prints it as a long rule after the
comma. Dickinson used dashes constantly, and modern editors preserve them
because they do work that no other punctuation mark can do.

In this line — "I'll tell you how the sun rose, ___" — the dash does four
things.

It creates a **pause and an intake of breath** exactly where a storyteller would
pause: the promise has been made, and now the listener must wait half a second
for the goods. The line becomes speech rather than writing.

It works as a **colon without the finality of a colon**. It announces that what
follows is the content of the telling, but it refuses to close the sentence off;
the poem seems to spill forward into stanza 1 rather than to start it.

It establishes the **presence of a listener**. A dash at that point is a turn of
the head towards somebody. Without it the line is an announcement; with it, it
is the opening of a conversation, which matters in a poem whose whole first half
is a performance for someone else.

Finally, it sets up the poem's **structural silence**. The dash is a place where
language stops and the reader supplies something. That is precisely what happens
on a larger scale at "I know not" — the poem hands the unsayable part over to
us. The little silence in line 1 rehearses the big one in line 9.

**c. What makes this poem lyrical and sonorous? Discuss.**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

A **lyric** is a short poem in the voice of a single speaker expressing personal
feeling, and originally a poem meant to be sung; **sonorous** means full and
pleasing in sound. "A Day" earns both words.

*It is built on a song form.* The four abcb quatrains are in **common metre**,
the 8-6-8-6 pattern of the English hymn book that Dickinson heard every Sunday
in Amherst. Any stanza of this poem can be sung to the tune of "Amazing Grace".
The alternation of a long line and a short line gives the poem a rocking,
predictable pulse, and the short fourth line lets each stanza come to rest.

*The rhyme is light but sure.* Only lines 2 and 4 rhyme — *begun / sun*, *stile
/ while*, *gray / away* — so the poem chimes without jingling. The one slant
rhyme, *time / ran*, keeps the opening from sounding too neat.

*The consonants are soft.* Listen to the sibilants running through the sunrise:
"the **s**un ro**s**e", "the **s**teeples **s**wam", "like **s**quirrels",
"I **s**aid **s**oftly". Almost nothing in the poem is harsh; there are no
clusters that stop the mouth. The vowels chime too — *how / sun / must*;
*little / climbing / while*.

*The diction is plain and mostly monosyllabic.* Out of about a hundred words,
only *ribbon, amethyst, steeples, bobolinks, bonnets, softly, purple, yellow,
little, dominie, gently, evening* have more than one syllable. Short words move
quickly and sound clean.

*And there is a single feeling behind it.* Lyric poems are unified by emotion
rather than argument, and the emotion here — delight turning into awe — is
carried right through without a break. The music is the emotion: loud and quick
in the morning stanzas, slow and low in the evening ones.

**d. Who are the target audience of the speaker? Why?**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

The poem is addressed, on its surface, to **children**, and there is plenty of
evidence for it.

The opening is the opening of a story told to a child: "I'll tell you how the
sun rose" is how an adult begins, or how one child begins to another. The
vocabulary is deliberately simple and concrete — ribbon, squirrels, bonnets,
boys and girls, bars, flock. The grammar is a child's grammar: "The bobolinks
begun". The conclusion of stanza 2, "That must have been the sun!", is the
delighted over-obviousness of a young listener working something out. And the
figures of the second half are drawn from a child's world — a stile to climb, a
schoolmaster, being taken home at the end of the day.

But it is worth saying in an answer that the poem is not *only* for children,
and the best answers say so. The allegory of stanzas 3–4 is transparent to any
adult: the stile is the edge of life, the yellow children are the dying light,
the dominie in grey is death or God, "the other side" is the next world. Children
will enjoy the pictures; adults will see what the pictures are of. Dickinson has
written a poem that can be read aloud to a child at bedtime and leave the adult
doing the reading quietly shaken.

There is a third audience: **the speaker herself**. "Then I said softly to
myself" reminds us that half of this poem is a person thinking aloud. That is
usually true of Dickinson, who wrote for a drawer rather than a public.

**e. The poem seems to describe a day for children. How would the adult people
respond to this poem? Discuss this poem with your parents or guardians and write
the answer based on their responses.**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

*This question asks you to talk to somebody and report what they said, so your
answer must contain your own family's words. The model below shows the shape and
the depth expected; replace the reported opinions with real ones.*

I read "A Day" aloud to my mother and my grandfather one evening and asked what
they made of it.

My mother's first response was to the pictures. She said the line about the
hills untying their bonnets was exactly what the ridge above our town looks like
when the mist comes off it in Asar, and that she had never thought of the mist
as a hat before. For her the pleasure of the poem was recognition — a child's
way of seeing a morning she has seen ten thousand times.

My grandfather responded to the second half, and he did not think it was a
children's poem at all. He said that once you are old you know what "the other
side" is, and that the poem is about dying. What struck him was the word
*gently*. He said most people are afraid of death because they imagine it as
violent, and that this poem imagines it as a teacher closing a gate at the end
of the school day — nobody is hurt, nobody is left behind, and the children go
home together. He also noticed that the poet will not say she believes it: "she
says *there seemed*," he said, "not *there was*."

That is the difference an adult reader makes. A child reads a bright morning and
a slightly sad evening. An adult reads innocence and experience — the confident
telling of what we have lived through, and the silence about what is still to
come. The poem does not change; the reader brings the second meaning with them.
That is why it belongs in a Grade 12 textbook and not only in a picture book.

### Reference beyond the text

**a. Observe your surroundings of one fine morning and write a poem based on
your own experience.** <span class="marks">[5]</span>

*Write your own. Here is a model in Dickinson's manner — four abcb quatrains,
8-6-8-6, with the sun described only through its effects, and the last stanza
turning away from what the speaker can explain.*

> I'll tell you how the morning came
> To Kathmandu today —
> It counted out the tin-roofed streets
> The way a shopman weighs.
>
> Ganesh Himal put off its shawl,
> A crow unlocked the tree,
> The milkman's cycle bell went first,
> Then every second bell.
>
> But how the dark had gone, who knows.
> There seemed a gate of smoke
> Through which the small blue morning boys
> Were carrying their loads,
>
> Till, at the corner by the tap,
> A woman dressed in grey
> Put down her water-jar and looked,
> And turned, and walked away.

**How to build your own.** Choose one real morning and one real place. Write
down four things the light *did* to that place — not the sun itself. Give at
least one of them a human action (the hills untied, the crow unlocked). Keep the
lines to eight and six syllables and rhyme only lines 2 and 4. In the last
stanza, put something in that you cannot explain, and stop.

**b. Write a personal essay on "A Day in the School".**
<span class="marks">[5]</span>

*A personal essay is written in the first person, about your own experience, in
a voice that sounds like you. It needs a shape, not just a timetable. The
outline below follows Dickinson's own structure — a bright, knowable morning and
an evening that closes gently — and you should fill it with your own school.*

**Opening.** Begin at a fixed detail, not a generalisation: the sound of the
gate, the smell of the corridor, the particular cold of the assembly ground at
10 a.m. One sentence that could only be your school.

**The morning.** The rush of arrival, the assembly, the first periods. Use the
poem's trick: describe the day through what it does to things and people — the
chalk dust in the window light, the noise moving down the corridor like
Dickinson's squirrels, the mist going off the playing field. Keep it concrete.

**The middle.** Lunch break is where a school is most itself. One small
incident, told properly, is worth three paragraphs of description: a borrowed
tiffin, an argument over a cricket rule, a friend who sat apart.

**The afternoon.** The change of energy after lunch; the last period; the
teacher who runs over time; the clock.

**The close.** Take Dickinson's ending and make it yours. The bell, the gate,
the classroom emptying, the peon putting up the shutters — the *dominie in gray*
of your own school putting up the evening bars. Finish on what you carry home
rather than on "and thus the day ended", which explains a feeling instead of
producing one.

**Length and language.** Aim for 350–400 words in six or seven paragraphs. Past
tense throughout. Vary sentence length: after three long sentences, use a short
one. And resist the urge to say what you learned — a personal essay shows, and
lets the reader conclude.
