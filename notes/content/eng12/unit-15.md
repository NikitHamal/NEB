A student can know the date of every treaty in a history book and still quarrel with a
classmate for a year over a borrowed pen. A scientist can understand the atom perfectly and
still build something that frightens the world. Bertrand Russell's short essay asks why
**more knowledge has not made us wiser** — and what wisdom actually is. He was one of the
great thinkers of the twentieth century, and he wrote this piece in 1954, nine years after the
bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki and in the year the United States tested a hydrogen bomb at
Bikini Atoll. It is a calm, clear, argued essay, and it rewards close reading, because each
paragraph adds one idea to the last.

::: key What the exam wants from this essay
**The central idea**: knowledge and wisdom are different things, and knowledge without wisdom
is dangerous. **The factors of wisdom** Russell names in turn: a *sense of proportion*,
*awareness of the ends of human life*, *freedom from personal prejudice*, and — the essence
— *emancipation from the tyranny of the here and now*. **The examples**: the medical researcher
and the atom; Hegel; the seekers of the philosopher's stone; Mr A and Mr B; the Samaritan.
**The teaching of wisdom**: it can be taught, by widening the intellectual element of moral
instruction. **The three leaders**: Queen Elizabeth I, Henry IV of France and Abraham Lincoln.
Many short questions ask you to *differentiate* the two terms; learn the textbook's simple
definition as well as Russell's own.
:::

## 15.1 About the author

```photo id="eng12/bertrand-russell" caption="Bertrand Russell photographed in 1954, the year he wrote 'Knowledge and Wisdom'. Public-domain portrait." width="40"
crop 0.0 0.05 1.0 0.98
```

Bertrand Arthur William Russell was born on **18 May 1872** in Wales, into one of the great
political families of Britain: his grandfather, Lord John Russell, had been prime minister. He
was orphaned at the age of four and brought up mainly by his grandmother. He won a scholarship
to **Trinity College, Cambridge**, where he studied mathematics and graduated in 1893. He went
on to become one of the founders of **analytic philosophy**. With Alfred North Whitehead he
wrote ***Principia Mathematica*** (1910–13), an attempt to show that mathematics could be
built on pure logic — the view called **logicism** in your textbook's note. He also wrote
*The Problems of Philosophy* (1912) and ***A History of Western Philosophy*** (1945), which
made philosophy readable for millions.

Russell was also a public figure who acted on what he believed. He was a **pacifist** and
went to prison in 1918 for an article against the First World War. Between the wars he wrote
on education, marriage and religion; after 1945 he became one of the leading campaigners
against **nuclear weapons**, and in 1955 he and Albert Einstein issued the **Russell–Einstein
Manifesto**, warning that nuclear war threatened the human race. In **1950** he received the
**Nobel Prize in Literature** "in recognition of his varied and significant writings in which
he champions humanitarian ideals and freedom of thought". He died in Wales on **2 February
1970**, aged 97.

::: note Why the essay is written the way it is
Russell loved clear prose and hated pretentiousness. "Knowledge and Wisdom" has almost no
technical words; every idea is tied to a concrete example that any reader can picture. The
mathematician in him shows in the *order* of the argument: define the problem (what is wisdom
and how can it be taught?), list the factors one by one, test them on cases, then draw the
conclusion.
:::

## 15.2 The essay: context and argument

"Knowledge and Wisdom" is an **argumentative (reflective) essay**. The writer states a
problem, answers it with reasons and examples, anticipates an objection and ends with a
call to action. It was written in **1954**. The date matters: in his own words the power of
the atom was "in everybody's mind at the present time". Nuclear weapons were the perfect
example of knowledge running ahead of wisdom.

```photo id="eng12/castle-bravo" caption="The Bravo test at Bikini Atoll, 1 March 1954: a 15-megaton thermonuclear explosion, photographed by the US Department of Energy. The year Russell wrote his essay, the world saw how far knowledge of the atom had outrun the wisdom to control it." width="46"
```

Your textbook's headnote gives a short, simplified version of the idea: *knowledge* is "the
acquisition of data and information", *wisdom* is "the practical application and use of the
knowledge to create value", gained "through learning and practical experience, not just
memorization". This is a modern summary, **not Russell's wording**. Board answers usually
quote it, so learn it — but in the essay Russell's own account is richer, and it is what you
should use in long answers:

| | **Knowledge** | **Wisdom** (Russell's sense) |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | information and skill; what a specialist knows | a **comprehensive view** of life: proportion, awareness of ends, freedom from prejudice and from self-centredness |
| Where it lives | in the mind | in mind **and feeling** ("knowledge … wide but … feelings narrow" is not wisdom) |
| Its risk | can be misused; "may become harmful" | guards against harm |
| Teaching | taught by instruction | taught by widening the intellectual element in moral instruction |
| Growth | always increasing | grows with the approach to impartiality |

The essay moves in **three stages**. First, it states the problem and defines wisdom through
its factors (paragraphs 1–5). Second, it asks whether wisdom can be taught and answers yes
(paragraphs 6–8). Third, it says how, and why the need is urgent (paragraph 9).

## 15.3 The essay, paragraph by paragraph

### Paragraph 1 — the problem

Most people, Russell says, would agree that "our age far surpasses all previous ages in
knowledge" but has had "no correlative increase in wisdom". *Correlative* means "corresponding":
wisdom has not grown in step with knowledge. Agreement stops, however, as soon as anyone tries
to **define wisdom** or to discuss how to promote it. So his plan is two questions: *what is
wisdom?* and *what can be done to teach it?*

### Paragraph 2 — the first factor: a sense of proportion

Of the factors that contribute to wisdom, Russell puts first **a sense of proportion**: "the
capacity to take account of all the important factors in a problem and to attach to each its
due weight". This has become harder because modern life needs **specialists**. Suppose you are
a medical researcher; the work takes all your energy and you have no time to think about its
effects outside medicine. You succeed — as modern medicine has — in greatly **lowering the
infant death-rate**, in Europe, America, Asia and Africa. The unintended result is that the
**food supply becomes inadequate** and the standard of living falls in the most populous parts
of the world. A still more striking example, "in everybody's mind at the present time": you
study the composition of the **atom** out of a disinterested desire for knowledge and thereby
put into the hands of "powerful lunatics the means of destroying the human race". His
conclusion: "the pursuit of knowledge may become harmful unless it is combined with wisdom; and
wisdom in the sense of **comprehensive vision** is not necessarily present in specialists".

::: note Russell is not against science
The infant death-rate example is not a complaint that saving babies is bad. It is a reminder
that every discovery has effects beyond the field of the discoverer, and that someone must look
at the whole picture. The fault is narrow vision, not medicine or physics.
:::

### Paragraph 3 — the second factor: awareness of the ends of human life

"Comprehensiveness alone, however, is not enough." There must also be "a certain **awareness
of the ends of human life**" — a sense of what life is *for*. He illustrates with **history**.
Many eminent historians "have done more harm than good because they viewed facts through the
distorting medium of their own passions". **Hegel**, the German philosopher, had a philosophy of
history that was certainly comprehensive — it began with the earliest times and stretched
into an indefinite future — but its chief lesson was that from the year 400 AD to Hegel's own
time **Germany** had been the most important nation and the standard-bearer of progress. A
comprehensive mind had been bent by national pride. Russell therefore stretches the
definition: wisdom includes not only intellect but **feeling**. "It is by no means uncommon to
find men whose knowledge is wide but whose feelings are narrow. Such men lack what I call
wisdom."

### Paragraph 4 — wisdom in private life

Wisdom is needed "not only in public ways, but in private life equally": "in the choice of
ends to be pursued and in **emancipation from personal prejudice**". Even a noble end may be
pursued unwisely if it is impossible. Many men in past ages devoted their lives to the search
for the **philosopher's stone** (which alchemists believed could turn base metals into gold)
and the **elixir of life** (a potion to give endless life). If they had found them they would
have benefited mankind, but "their lives were wasted".

He then takes a humbler case: **Mr A and Mr B**, who hate each other and, through that mutual
hatred, bring each other to destruction. Ask Mr A why he hates Mr B and you get "an appalling
list" of B's vices, part true and part false. Ask Mr B and you get "an exactly similar list"
about A. Tell each that the other says the same about him, and at first their hatred grows,
because each is horrified by the other's injustice. But with patience and persuasiveness you
may convince each that the other has "only the normal share of human wickedness" and that
their enmity harms both. "If you can do this, you will have **instilled some fragment of
wisdom**."

### Paragraph 5 — the essence: emancipation from the tyranny of the here and now

"I think the essence of wisdom is **emancipation, as far as possible, from the tyranny of the
here and now**." We cannot help the "egoism of our senses": sight, sound and touch are bound up
with our own bodies, and our emotions start from ourselves. A baby feels only its own hunger
or discomfort. With the years a person's horizon widens, and as thoughts and feelings become
less personal and less concerned with one's own physical states, one "achieves growing
wisdom". This is a matter of degree: no one can view the world with complete impartiality (and
if anyone could, says Russell, he would hardly remain alive). But one can make "a continual
approach towards impartiality", both by **knowing things somewhat remote in time or space**, and
by **giving such things their due weight in our feelings**. "It is this approach towards
impartiality that constitutes growth in wisdom."

### Paragraph 6 — can wisdom be taught?

"Can wisdom in this sense be taught? And, if it can, should the teaching of it be one of the
aims of education?" Russell answers **yes to both**. He reminds the reader that "we are told on
Sundays that we should love our neighbours as ourselves. On the other six days of the week, we
are exhorted to hate." The parable of the **Good Samaritan** made a foreigner the
neighbour; we no longer hate Samaritans, so we miss the point. "If you want to get its point,
you should substitute **Communist or anti-Communist**, as the case may be, for Samaritan." (In
1954, at the height of the Cold War, hatred between the two camps was the most dangerous hatred
of all.) The irony is sharp: people who profess love on Sunday practise hatred on Monday.

### Paragraph 7 — "Hatred of evil is itself a kind of bondage to evil"

"It might be objected that it is right to hate those who do harm. I do not think so." If you
hate them, you will probably become as harmful as they are, and you are unlikely to persuade
them to give up their evil ways. "**Hatred of evil is itself a kind of bondage to evil.** The
way out is through understanding, not through hate." Russell adds a precaution: "I am not
advocating **non-resistance**." Resistance is right, but it should combine "the greatest degree
of understanding and the smallest degree of force that is compatible with the survival of the
good things that we wish to preserve".

### Paragraph 8 — is wisdom weak?

It is often said that such a view is "incompatible with vigour in action". Russell denies that
history supports this. **Queen Elizabeth I** of England and **Henry IV** of France lived in a
world where almost everybody was fanatical on the Protestant or the Catholic side; both "remained
free from the errors of their time" and, by remaining free, "were beneficent and certainly not
ineffective". **Abraham Lincoln** "conducted a great war without ever departing from what I have
called wisdom".

### Paragraph 9 — teaching wisdom, and why the world needs it

"I have said that in some degree wisdom can be taught." The teaching, says Russell, "should have
a larger **intellectual element** than has been customary in what has been thought of as moral
instruction". The "disastrous results of hatred and narrow-mindedness to those who feel them can
be pointed out incidentally in the course of giving knowledge". He does not think that knowledge and morals ought
to be "too much separated". Specialised skills have little to do with wisdom, but they should
be "supplemented in education by wider surveys calculated to put it in its place in the total
of human activities". "Even the best technicians should also be good **citizens**; and when I
say 'citizens', I mean **citizens of the world** and not of this or that sect or nation."

The final argument is the one the examiner loves: "**With every increase of knowledge and skill,
wisdom becomes more necessary**, for every such increase augments our capacity of realizing our
purposes, and therefore augments our capacity for evil, if our purposes are unwise. The world
needs wisdom as it has never needed it before; and if knowledge continues to increase, the world
will need wisdom in the future even more than it does now."

**Elizabeth, Henry IV, Lincoln** → ¶8. **Aim of education; citizens of the world; why the world

## 15.4 Ideas and themes

**1. Knowledge alone is dangerous.** The two examples — the medical researcher and the atomic
scientist — show that discoveries have effects the discoverer never intended. Wisdom is what
notices those effects in advance. Russell does not ask us to know *less*; he asks us to see
*more*.

**2. Wisdom is a wide view, of mind and of feeling.** It is not cleverness. The historian
Hegel was clever but his love of his own nation bent his history. Russell adds feeling to
intellect: a person whose knowledge is wide but whose sympathies are narrow is not wise.

**3. Hatred is unwise — and self-destroying.** Mr A and Mr B ruin each other by hatred; the
seeker of the philosopher's stone wastes his life on an impossible end. Russell's
conclusion, "Hatred of evil is itself a kind of bondage to evil", is the moral heart of the
essay. Understanding replaces hatred; it does not replace resistance.

**4. The tyranny of the here and now.** We begin life locked inside our own bodies and moment.
Wisdom is a lifelong movement outwards — to other people, other places, other times. This is
why history, geography, foreign cultures and imagination matter: they put the here and now
in its place.

**5. Education must teach wisdom.** Skills are necessary, but a technician who is not also a
good citizen of the world is a danger. Education should teach facts *and* show their effects;
moral teaching should be intellectual, not just sermons.

**6. Urgency.** Every increase of knowledge increases our power to realise our purposes —
good or bad. The more powerful we become, the more we need wisdom. The ending points forward:
"the world will need wisdom in the future even more than it does now".

## 15.5 Style and devices

**Argument, not story.** The essay states a problem in the first paragraph, gives reasons,
tests them on examples, raises an objection (¶7) and answers it (¶8) before concluding
(¶9). This "thesis – support – objection – reply – conclusion" shape is the classic form of an
argumentative essay.

**Examples from everyday and world life.** The medical researcher, the atom, Hegel, the
alchemists, Mr A and Mr B, the Samaritan, Elizabeth I, Henry IV and Lincoln move from public
to private life and from ancient to modern times. Each example is chosen to show one idea
clearly.

**Hypothetical dialogue.** "Suppose you go to Mr A and say, 'Why do you hate Mr B?'" Russell
turns an abstract point about prejudice into a little play that the reader can follow and
imagine doing.

**Antithesis and parallelism.** "Knowledge … wide but … feelings … narrow." "The greatest degree
of understanding and the smallest degree of force." "On Sundays … On the other six days."
The balanced sentences make ideas memorable.

**Aphorism.** "Hatred of evil is itself a kind of bondage to evil." "The essence of wisdom is
emancipation … from the tyranny of the here and now." Short, quotable sentences that sum up a
paragraph — often the lines examiners pick for "reference to the context".

**Irony.** The contrast between Sunday love and weekday hatred is ironic: people praise a
teaching they do not practise.

**Metaphor and personification.** "Tyranny" (of the here and now), "bondage" (to evil) and
"distorting medium" (of passion) use political and physical images for mental states; "egoism
of our senses" treats the senses as selfish.

**Allusion.** The Good Samaritan (the Bible), the philosopher's stone and elixir of life
(alchemy), Hegel, Elizabeth I, Henry IV, Lincoln. The reader is assumed to be educated, which
itself suits the subject.

**Tone.** Calm, reasonable, sometimes humorous ("they would hardly be able to remain alive")
and humane. The last sentences rise to a serious warning.

## 15.7 Textbook exercises

**Before you read**

*a. What are the differences between a learned man and a wise man?*

A **learned** person has acquired a great deal of information, often through long study; a
**wise** person knows how to use experience and understanding to judge well and act rightly. A
learned man can tell you *what* is known; a wise man can tell you *what to do* and *what
matters*. A learned man may be proud and narrow; a wise man is usually humble, patient and
fair. Knowledge can be gained by reading; wisdom needs reflection and life.

*b. Why do you think some knowledgeable people are not wise?*

Because knowledge can be collected without any change of character. Some people specialise so
narrowly that they never see the wider effects of what they do; some use their learning for
pride or profit; some are ruled by prejudice, anger or self-interest that no amount of
information removes. Russell's own example is Hegel: wide knowledge, but distorted by national
pride.

**Understanding the text**

*a. What are the factors that contribute to wisdom?*

Russell names several. **First and foremost, a sense of proportion** — the ability to take
account of all the important factors in a problem and give each its due weight. **Second, an
awareness of the ends of human life** — knowing what life is for, rather than seeing facts
through one's own passions. **Third, feeling as well as intellect** — a wide heart as well as a
wide mind. **Fourth, freedom from personal prejudice** in the choice of ends. **Fifth, and
essentially, emancipation from the tyranny of the here and now** — a growing impartiality
reached by knowing things remote in time and space and giving them their due weight in our
feelings.

*b. What message does the writer try to convey with the example of technicians?*

That **specialist knowledge, without a wide view, can cause harm**. The medical researcher who
lowers the infant death-rate unintentionally causes a shortage of food in crowded countries;
the scientist who studies the atom for pure knowledge puts the means of destroying humanity
into dangerous hands. Technicians are so absorbed in their own field that they do not consider
the wider results, so knowledge must be combined with wisdom, which is comprehensive vision.

*c. Which leaders does Russell say were able to mix knowledge and wisdom soundly?*

**Queen Elizabeth I of England, Henry IV of France and Abraham Lincoln.** Elizabeth and Henry
lived among fanatics on both the Protestant and Catholic sides, yet remained free from the
errors of their time and were kind and effective. Lincoln led a great war "without ever
departing from what I have called wisdom".

*d. Why is wisdom needed not only in public ways, but in private life equally?*

Because in private life, too, we must **choose our aims** and free ourselves from **personal
prejudice**. A noble aim pursued unwisely (the search for the philosopher's stone) wastes a
life; and private hatred, as in the case of Mr A and Mr B, destroys both people. Wisdom helps
us see the other side, to see hatred for what it is and to live at peace.

*e. What, according to Russell, is the true aim of education?*

The passage does not use the words "true aim", but it makes Russell's view clear: education
should **teach wisdom along with knowledge and skill**. Specialised training is not enough; it
should be supplemented by "wider surveys" that put it "in its place in the total of human
activities", so that even the best technicians become good **citizens of the world**, not of
"this or that sect or nation". Its aim is thus to produce wise, humane people, not only
skilled ones.

*f. Can wisdom be taught? If so, how?*

Yes, "in some degree". Russell says its teaching should have **a larger intellectual element**
than traditional moral instruction: rather than preaching, teachers should, *in the course of
giving knowledge*, point out the disastrous results of hatred and narrow-mindedness to those who
feel them. Knowledge and morals should not be kept apart. Wisdom can also be taught by
examples like the parable of the Samaritan applied to modern enemies.

*g. Why does the world need more wisdom in the future?*

Because "with every increase of knowledge and skill, wisdom becomes more necessary": more
knowledge increases our power to achieve our purposes, and therefore our power to do evil if our
purposes are unwise. Nuclear weapons show the danger. If knowledge keeps growing, the world
"will need wisdom in the future even more than it does now".

**Reference to the context**

*a. According to Russell, "The pursuit of knowledge may become harmful unless it is combined with wisdom." Justify this statement.*

Russell justifies it with two examples in the second paragraph. A researcher in scientific
medicine, absorbed in his own field, greatly lowers the infant death-rate throughout the world;
but the unintended result is a **food supply that is inadequate** and a **lower standard of life** in
the most populous regions. Still more seriously, the scientist who studies the composition of
the **atom** out of pure curiosity unintentionally places in the hands of "powerful lunatics the
means of destroying the human race". In both cases knowledge was good but was not joined to
the wide view — the sense of proportion and awareness of consequences — that is wisdom. He
adds the example of Hegel, whose great learning produced a false philosophy of history because
his feelings were narrow. The statement is therefore true: without wisdom, knowledge can
harm, and the more knowledge we gain, the more wisdom we need.

*b. What, according to Russell, is the essence of wisdom? And how can one acquire the very essence?*

"I think the essence of wisdom is **emancipation, as far as possible, from the tyranny of the
here and now**." We are born with the "egoism of our senses" and feelings: a baby cares only for
its own hunger or discomfort. As we grow, our horizon widens and our thoughts and feelings
become less personal; in proportion, we become wiser. Complete impartiality is impossible, but
we can move towards it "by knowing things somewhat remote in time or space" and "by giving to
such things their due weight in our feelings". In practice, one acquires it by learning history
and about other places and cultures, by understanding the other side's case (as Mr A and Mr B
need to), by refusing hatred and by treating all humanity as one's neighbours. This
"continual approach towards impartiality", says Russell, "constitutes growth in wisdom".

**Reference beyond the text**

*a. Why is wisdom necessary in education? Discuss.*

Education gives knowledge and skill, which are **power**, and power without wisdom is dangerous.
A student who learns chemistry without learning responsibility may harm others; a student who
learns facts without learning to weigh them is easily misled by prejudice and propaganda. Wisdom
teaches **what to do with** what we learn: to see the whole picture, to respect other people
and to take care of consequences. It also makes learning humane: education that aims only at
marks and jobs produces clever but narrow people. In the words of Russell, the best technicians
should also be good citizens of the world. In Nepal, where education is sought for employment
abroad as well as at home, wisdom reminds us to use our skill for the community. Education is
complete only when it develops both the head and the heart.

*b. How can you become wise? Do you think what you are doing in college contributes to wisdom?*

A personal answer is expected. Model: I can become wise by **reading widely**, especially history
and literature, which show me other times and places; by **listening** to people who disagree with me;
by **learning from mistakes** and thinking about their causes; by **serving** others; and by
practising patience instead of anger. In college I learn science and language, which are
knowledge. They contribute to wisdom only partly — when a teacher relates a lesson to life, when
group work teaches me to respect others, and when essays like this one make me question my own
habits. To contribute more, college should give more time to discussion, to service and to
asking *what is this knowledge for?*

## Chapter summary

- **Bertrand Russell** (1872–1970), British philosopher, logician and social critic, Nobel
  Prize for Literature 1950, wrote "Knowledge and Wisdom" in **1954**.
- Central idea: our age far surpasses earlier ages in knowledge without a matching increase in
  wisdom; **knowledge may become harmful unless combined with wisdom**.
- Textbook headnote: knowledge = acquiring data and information; wisdom = practical application
  of knowledge to create value. Russell's own account: wisdom is **comprehensive vision**.
- Factors of wisdom: **sense of proportion** (medicine and atom examples); **awareness of the
  ends of human life** (Hegel); **feeling as well as intellect**; **freedom from personal
  prejudice** (philosopher's stone; Mr A and Mr B).
- **Essence of wisdom**: emancipation from "the tyranny of the here and now" — moving towards
  impartiality by knowing the remote in time and space.
- Wisdom **can be taught**, by a larger intellectual element in moral instruction; hatred of
  evil is itself "a kind of bondage to evil"; understanding, not force. Elizabeth I, Henry IV
  and Lincoln show that wisdom is not weakness.
- Education should produce good **citizens of the world**; "with every increase of knowledge and
  skill, wisdom becomes more necessary".
- Style: argument and example, hypothetical dialogue, antithesis, aphorism, irony, allusion,
  calm tone.

**Short answer questions (2 marks each)**

1. What is the difference between knowledge and wisdom, according to Russell? (NEB 2079, Set B; NEB 2080, Set C) <span class="marks">[2]</span>
2. Why does the world need more wisdom than knowledge in the future? (NEB 2081, Set C) <span class="marks">[2]</span>

4. What is the central idea of the essay "Knowledge and Wisdom"? (Model) <span class="marks">[2]</span>
5. How can wisdom be taught? Give examples. (Model) <span class="marks">[2]</span>
6. Why does Russell say that Hegel lacked wisdom? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
7. What point does Russell make through Mr A and Mr B? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
8. What does Russell mean by "the tyranny of the here and now"? <span class="marks">[2]</span>
9. Why does Russell refuse to say that it is right to hate those who do harm? <span class="marks">[2]</span>

::: note Model answers — short questions
**1.** According to the textbook's summary, knowledge is the acquisition of data and information,
while wisdom is the practical application of that knowledge to create value. In Russell's own
account, knowledge is what a specialist knows; wisdom is a **comprehensive vision** — a sense of
proportion, awareness of the ends of life, freedom from prejudice and from the "tyranny of the
here and now". Knowledge can be harmful without wisdom.

**2.** Because "with every increase of knowledge and skill, wisdom becomes more necessary".
More knowledge gives people greater power to achieve their purposes, and so greater power for
**evil** if their purposes are unwise. Since knowledge will go on increasing, the world will need
wisdom even more in the future than now.

**3.** Yes, I agree. Knowledge tells us what we *can* do; wisdom tells us what we *should* do.
Without wisdom, knowledge can be harmful, as nuclear weapons and misused medicine show. A
person with wisdom can continue to learn and will use what he knows for good, but a person
with knowledge alone may harm others.

**4.** The central idea is that knowledge and wisdom are different, that our age has gained
enormously in knowledge without a corresponding increase in wisdom, and that **knowledge may
become harmful unless it is combined with wisdom**, which can and should be taught.

**5.** Russell says it can be taught "in some degree", by giving moral teaching a **larger
intellectual element**: in the course of teaching facts, teachers can point out the disastrous
results of hatred and narrow-mindedness. For example, when teaching atomic physics they can
show how the atom has been used for destruction; by applying the Samaritan parable to today's
enemies, teachers can show that hatred harms the hater.

**6.** Hegel's philosophy of history was comprehensive in time, but its chief lesson was that from
400 AD Germany had been the most important nation and the standard-bearer of progress. His
**national pride distorted** the facts, so he lacked the awareness of the ends of human life and
the freedom from prejudice that wisdom needs.

**7.** Two men who hate each other and are ruined by their hatred each give an equally
one-sided list of the other's vices, partly true and partly false. By showing each that the
other says the same, a wise person can make both see that the other has "only the normal share
of human wickedness" and that their **enmity harms both**. That is a "fragment of wisdom":
seeing a quarrel from outside oneself.

**8.** It means the natural **self-centredness** of our senses and feelings, which tie us to our
own bodies, needs and moment. An infant knows only its own hunger. Wisdom is freedom from this
as far as possible, by understanding and caring about things remote in time and space.

**9.** Because hatred makes the hater **as harmful as the enemy** and is unlikely to persuade
anyone to give up evil ways. "Hatred of evil is itself a kind of bondage to evil." The way out is
**understanding**, though he does not advocate non-resistance: resistance should use the
smallest force compatible with preserving what is good.
:::

**Long answer questions (5 marks each)**

1. Discuss Russell's view of wisdom and the factors that contribute to it. (Model) <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. "The pursuit of knowledge may become harmful unless it is combined with wisdom." Discuss
   with reference to the essay. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Can wisdom be taught? Explain Russell's views on wisdom, hatred and education. (Model) <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. Why, according to Russell, is wisdom more necessary today than ever before? Do you agree?
   <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Model answers — long questions
**1.** Russell begins from the fact that our age far surpasses earlier ages in knowledge, without
a corresponding increase in wisdom. He defines wisdom through its factors. The first is a
**sense of proportion**, the capacity to take account of all the important factors in a problem
and give each its due weight; specialists often lack this, as the medical researcher and the
atomic physicist show. The second is **awareness of the ends of human life**: Hegel's
comprehensive history failed because national pride bent it. The third is **feeling** as well as
intellect, since people with wide knowledge but narrow feelings are not wise. The fourth is
**freedom from personal prejudice**, in private life as well as public, as the stories of the
alchemists and of Mr A and Mr B show. The essence of all these is **emancipation from the
tyranny of the here and now** — a continual approach towards impartiality, by knowing things
remote in time and space and giving them their due weight in our feelings. For Russell, wisdom
is therefore a comprehensive vision of mind and heart, and a rare one.

**2.** Russell proves the statement with examples. A medical researcher lowers the infant
death-rate; but because no one weighs the wider results, the food supply becomes inadequate and
living standards fall in crowded regions. A physicist studies the atom from pure curiosity, and
the result is that "powerful lunatics" can destroy the human race. In each case knowledge itself
is good, but without **comprehensive vision** it leads to harm. The same is true in history: Hegel
knew a great deal, but national pride produced a false lesson. And in the final paragraph Russell
explains why this will get worse: each increase of knowledge and skill increases our capacity to
realise our purposes, "and therefore augments our capacity for evil, if our purposes are
unwise". The statement is therefore true: the answer is not less knowledge but more wisdom,
taught alongside it.

**3.** Russell answers yes, "in some degree". The need is great, because people are taught on
Sundays to love their neighbours and on other days to hate them; the Good Samaritan's lesson
would be understood today only if we put "Communist or anti-Communist" in his place. He holds
that hatred of evil is "a kind of bondage to evil": hate makes us as harmful as those we hate
and rarely persuades them; the way out is understanding, combined with resistance using the
smallest force necessary. Examples from history (Elizabeth I, Henry IV, Lincoln) show that such
wisdom is not weakness. In education, moral instruction should have a larger **intellectual
element**: while teaching knowledge, teachers should show the disastrous results of hatred and
narrow-mindedness; skills should be put in the context of human life by wider surveys; and
technicians should be good **citizens of the world**. Knowledge and morals should not be
separated.

**4.** According to Russell, wisdom is more necessary than ever because the power of knowledge
has grown enormously. "With every increase of knowledge and skill, wisdom becomes more
necessary", since greater power to realise our purposes is also greater power to do evil if the
purposes are unwise. Nuclear weapons, unknown to earlier ages, show what this means: for the
first time humans can destroy themselves. Modern specialisation also narrows the vision of
experts, who therefore cannot see the wider effects of their work. And if knowledge continues
to grow, "the world will need wisdom in the future even more than it does now". I agree. Modern
examples such as climate change and the misuse of the internet show that cleverness without
responsibility is dangerous, and that schools and colleges should teach the question "What is
this knowledge for?" as well as the answer.
