This chapter is the arithmetic of chemistry. It carries about **two of the seventeen
Physical Chemistry questions** in the paper (our estimate from the MEC topic list), but
the mole concept it builds is used again in volumetric analysis, equilibrium,
electrochemistry and thermodynamics questions — a slip here costs marks several times
over. The questions come in three kinds: a definition or a law with its author, a
one-line conversion between mass, moles, particles and gas volume, and a short
stoichiometry problem (limiting reactant, percentage yield, empirical formula).

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- **1 u = 1/12 of the mass of one ¹²C atom = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g**; relative atomic mass has no unit; average atomic mass from isotope abundances.
- Writing formulae from valencies and radicals, including the awkward ones (Hg₂²⁺, S₂O₃²⁻, Cr₂O₇²⁻, [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻).
- Empirical formula from percentage composition; molecular formula = n × empirical formula, with M = 2 × vapour density.
- The five laws of chemical combination, their authors and a test example for each; which of Dalton's postulates failed.
- Avogadro's law — equal volumes contain equal numbers of **molecules** (not atoms); 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP.
- The one-line conversions $n = m/M = N/N_A = V/22.4$.
- Limiting reactant (smallest moles ÷ coefficient) and percentage yield = actual ÷ theoretical × 100.
:::

## 1.1 Atoms, molecules, valency and radicals; relative masses and the atomic mass unit

### Atom, molecule and atomicity

::: definition Atom and molecule
An **atom** is the smallest particle of an element that takes part in a chemical
reaction; it may or may not exist independently. A **molecule** is the smallest
particle of an element or a compound that can exist independently and shows all the
properties of that substance.
:::

The number of atoms in one molecule of an element is its **atomicity**.

| Atomicity | Examples | Remember |
|---|---|---|
| 1 (monoatomic) | He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn; metal vapours (Na, Hg) | all noble gases |
| 2 (diatomic) | H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂ | "the seven diatomics" |
| 3 (triatomic) | O₃ (ozone) | allotrope of oxygen |
| 4 (tetra-atomic) | P₄ (white phosphorus), As₄ | tetrahedral molecule |
| 8 (polyatomic) | S₈ (rhombic and monoclinic sulphur) | crown-shaped ring |

### Valency

**Valency** is the combining capacity of an element: the number of hydrogen atoms
(or chlorine atoms, or twice the number of oxygen atoms) that one atom of it combines
with or displaces. In electronic terms it is the number of electrons an atom loses,
gains or shares when it bonds (Unit 4). Several metals show **variable valency**; the
old names use **-ous** for the lower and **-ic** for the higher valency.

| Element | Lower valency (-ous) | Higher valency (-ic) |
|---|---|---|
| Iron | Fe²⁺ ferrous | Fe³⁺ ferric |
| Copper | Cu⁺ cuprous | Cu²⁺ cupric |
| Mercury | Hg₂²⁺ mercurous (each Hg is +1) | Hg²⁺ mercuric |
| Tin | Sn²⁺ stannous | Sn⁴⁺ stannic |
| Lead | Pb²⁺ plumbous | Pb⁴⁺ plumbic |
| Gold | Au⁺ aurous | Au³⁺ auric |

::: caution Valency is not oxidation number
Valency is a plain number with no sign. Oxidation number carries a sign and can be
zero or a fraction. Carbon has valency 4 in CH₄, CH₂Cl₂ and CCl₄, but its oxidation
number is −4, 0 and +4 respectively; in Fe₃O₄ the average oxidation number of iron is
+8/3. Oxidation numbers are developed in Unit 5.
:::

### Radicals

::: definition Radical
A **radical** is an atom or a group of atoms carrying a charge that behaves as a single
unit in chemical reactions. Positive radicals are **basic radicals** (cations);
negative radicals are **acid radicals** (anions).
:::

| Valency | Basic radicals | Acid radicals |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Na⁺, K⁺, Ag⁺, Cu⁺, NH₄⁺ (ammonium), H₃O⁺ (hydronium) | Cl⁻, OH⁻, NO₃⁻ (nitrate), NO₂⁻ (nitrite), HCO₃⁻ (bicarbonate), HSO₄⁻ (bisulphate), CN⁻ (cyanide), CH₃COO⁻ (acetate), MnO₄⁻ (permanganate), ClO₃⁻ (chlorate), ClO⁻ (hypochlorite) |
| 2 | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Ba²⁺, Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺, Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺ | O²⁻, S²⁻, SO₄²⁻, SO₃²⁻ (sulphite), CO₃²⁻, S₂O₃²⁻ (thiosulphate), CrO₄²⁻ (chromate), Cr₂O₇²⁻ (dichromate), C₂O₄²⁻ (oxalate), MnO₄²⁻ (manganate), ZnO₂²⁻ (zincate) |
| 3 | Al³⁺, Fe³⁺, Cr³⁺ | N³⁻ (nitride), PO₄³⁻ (phosphate), AsO₄³⁻ (arsenate), [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ (ferricyanide) |
| 4 | Sn⁴⁺, Pb⁴⁺ | [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ (ferrocyanide), SiO₄⁴⁻ (orthosilicate) |

**Writing a formula — criss-cross.** Write the basic radical first, swap the
valencies into subscripts, cancel any common factor, and bracket a polyatomic radical
that is taken more than once: Al³⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → Al₂(SO₄)₃; Ca²⁺ + PO₄³⁻ → Ca₃(PO₄)₂;
Sn⁴⁺ + O²⁻ → Sn₂O₄ → SnO₂; K⁺ + [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ → K₄[Fe(CN)₆];
NH₄⁺ + Cr₂O₇²⁻ → (NH₄)₂Cr₂O₇. Check: total positive charge = total negative charge.

::: caution Three formulae students get wrong
Mercurous chloride (calomel) is **Hg₂Cl₂**, not HgCl, because mercury(I) exists as the
pair Hg₂²⁺. Ferric sulphate is Fe₂(SO₄)₃ and ferrous sulphate FeSO₄. Hypo (sodium
thiosulphate) is Na₂S₂O₃·5H₂O — not Na₂SO₃, which is sodium sulphite.
:::

### Relative atomic mass, molecular mass and the atomic mass unit

::: definition Atomic mass unit and relative atomic mass
One **atomic mass unit** (u, also called the dalton, Da) is **one-twelfth of the mass
of one atom of carbon-12**: 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
The **relative atomic mass** of an element is the number of times one atom of the
element is heavier than 1/12 of a ¹²C atom. Being a ratio, it has **no unit**.
:::

$$ A_r = \frac{\text{mass of one atom of the element}}{\frac{1}{12}\times \text{mass of one }^{12}\text{C atom}} $$

- **Relative molecular mass** = sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms in the
  formula (H₂SO₄: 2 + 32 + 64 = 98). For ionic solids, which have no molecules, the
  same sum is the **formula mass** (NaCl: 58.5).
- **Gram atomic mass** (one gram-atom) and **gram molecular mass** (one gram-molecule)
  are the same numbers in grams — that is, the mass of one mole.
- **Standards over time:** H = 1 (Dalton) → O = 16 (chemists' scale until 1961) →
  ¹²C = 12 exactly (IUPAC, 1961, still in use).
- Because 1 u = (1/N_A) g, **1 g = 6.02 × 10²³ u**, and the mass of one particle in
  grams is $M/N_A$.

**Average atomic mass.** Most elements are mixtures of isotopes, so the tabulated
atomic mass is the abundance-weighted average:
$\bar{A} = \sum (\text{fraction} \times \text{isotopic mass})$. For two isotopes there
is a faster form — add to the lighter mass the mass difference times the fraction of
the heavier isotope.

::: example Average atomic mass in one line
**Problem.** Copper is 69 % ⁶³Cu and 31 % ⁶⁵Cu. Taking the isotopic masses as 63
and 65, find the average atomic mass of copper.

**Solution.** Lighter mass + (difference × fraction of heavier)
= 63 + 2 × 0.31 = 63 + 0.62 = **63.62 ≈ 63.6**.
Reverse use: if the average and the two masses are given, the fraction of the heavier
isotope is (average − lighter) ÷ (heavier − lighter).
:::

::: example Mass of single particles
**Problem.** Find the mass in grams of one atom of ¹²C and of one molecule of CO₂
(N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹).

**Solution.** Mass of one particle = M ÷ N_A.
One ¹²C atom: 12 ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = **1.99 × 10⁻²³ g** (and 1/12 of this is 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g = 1 u).
One CO₂ molecule: 44 ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = **7.31 × 10⁻²³ g**.
:::

## 1.2 Chemical equations, empirical and molecular formulae, percentage composition

### What an equation tells you — and what it does not

A balanced chemical equation is a statement of conservation: the same number of atoms
of each element on both sides.

| A balanced equation tells | It does **not** tell (unless written in) |
|---|---|
| the reactants and products | the rate or time taken (that is kinetics) |
| the mole ratio of all species | the conditions — temperature, pressure, catalyst |
| the mass ratio (with molar masses) | whether the reaction is complete or reversible |
| the volume ratio of gases at the same T and P | the physical states and the concentrations |
| that atoms (and mass) are conserved | the mechanism, or whether it happens at all |

An equation is made more informative by writing states (s, l, g, aq), conditions over
the arrow, ⇌ for a reversible reaction and ΔH for the heat change:
N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), ΔH = −92 kJ (Fe catalyst, about 450 °C, 200 atm).

**Balancing by inspection.** Balance first the element that appears in the fewest
formulae, leave H and O to the end, and balance a free element (O₂, H₂) last; clear any
fraction by multiplying through. For any combustion there is a formula worth knowing:

$$ \text{C}_x\text{H}_y\text{O}_z + \left(x + \frac{y}{4} - \frac{z}{2}\right)\text{O}_2 \rightarrow x\,\text{CO}_2 + \frac{y}{2}\,\text{H}_2\text{O} $$

So propane C₃H₈ needs 3 + 8/4 = 5 O₂; ethanol C₂H₆O needs 2 + 6/4 − 1/2 = 3 O₂;
glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ needs 6 + 3 − 3 = 6 O₂. Redox equations are balanced in Unit 5.

### Percentage composition

$$ \text{mass \% of an element} = \frac{(\text{atoms of it in the formula}) \times A_r}{M} \times 100 $$

| Compound | M | Result worth remembering |
|---|---|---|
| H₂O | 18 | H 11.1 %, O 88.9 % (H : O = 1 : 8 by mass) |
| CO₂ | 44 | C 27.3 % |
| CH₄ | 16 | C 75 %, H 25 % |
| Glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ | 180 | C 40.0 %, H 6.7 %, O 53.3 % |
| Urea CO(NH₂)₂ | 60 | N 46.7 % — the richest common solid nitrogen fertiliser |
| NH₄NO₃ | 80 | N 35.0 % |
| (NH₄)₂SO₄ | 132 | N 21.2 % |
| CuSO₄·5H₂O | 249.5 | water of crystallisation 36.1 % |
| Na₂CO₃·10H₂O | 286 | water of crystallisation 62.9 % |

### Empirical and molecular formula

::: definition Empirical and molecular formula
The **empirical formula** gives the simplest whole-number ratio of the atoms in a
compound. The **molecular formula** gives the actual number of atoms of each element in
one molecule. Molecular formula = n × empirical formula, where
n = molecular mass ÷ empirical-formula mass (a whole number).
:::

**Method from percentages.** (1) Take 100 g, so % becomes grams. (2) Divide by the
atomic mass to get relative moles. (3) Divide by the smallest. (4) If a ratio ends in
.5, multiply all by 2; in .33 or .67, by 3; in .25 or .75, by 4. (5) Find n from the
molar mass — for a gas or vapour, M = 2 × vapour density.

| Empirical formula | Compounds sharing it |
|---|---|
| CH | ethyne C₂H₂, benzene C₆H₆ |
| CH₂ | ethene C₂H₄, propene C₃H₆, cyclohexane C₆H₁₂ |
| CH₂O | methanal HCHO, ethanoic acid C₂H₄O₂, glucose C₆H₁₂O₆ |
| CH₃ | ethane C₂H₆ |
| HO | hydrogen peroxide H₂O₂ |
| NO₂ | dinitrogen tetroxide N₂O₄ |

::: example Molecular formula from percentages
**Problem.** A hydrocarbon contains 92.3 % C and 7.7 % H; its vapour density is 39.
Find its molecular formula.

**Solution.** C : H = 92.3/12 : 7.7/1 = 7.69 : 7.7 = 1 : 1, so the empirical formula is
CH (mass 13). M = 2 × 39 = 78, so n = 78/13 = 6. Molecular formula **C₆H₆** (benzene).
:::

::: example Empirical formula from combustion data
**Problem.** 0.30 g of an organic compound containing C, H and O burns to give 0.44 g
CO₂ and 0.18 g H₂O. Find its empirical formula.

**Solution.** Mass of C = 0.44 × 12/44 = 0.12 g; mass of H = 0.18 × 2/18 = 0.02 g;
mass of O = 0.30 − 0.12 − 0.02 = 0.16 g. Moles C : H : O = 0.01 : 0.02 : 0.01 =
1 : 2 : 1. Empirical formula **CH₂O**.
:::

::: caution Percentages that do not add up to 100
If only C and H percentages are given and they add to less than 100, the remainder is
oxygen (unless another element is named). And never round 1.5 to 2 or 1.33 to 1 —
multiply the whole ratio instead: C : H = 1 : 1.5 means C₂H₃, not CH₂.
:::

## 1.3 Dalton's atomic theory, the laws of stoichiometry and Avogadro's law

### Dalton's atomic theory (1808) and what survived

| Postulate | Status today |
|---|---|
| Matter is made of tiny indivisible atoms | **Failed** — atoms contain electrons, protons and neutrons |
| Atoms can neither be created nor destroyed | Holds for **chemical** change; fails in nuclear reactions |
| Atoms of one element are identical in mass and properties | **Failed** — isotopes (¹H, ²H, ³H; ³⁵Cl, ³⁷Cl) |
| Atoms of different elements have different masses | **Failed** — isobars have equal mass numbers (⁴⁰Ar and ⁴⁰Ca) |
| Atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios | Whole numbers yes, but not always simple (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, proteins); fails for non-stoichiometric solids |
| The atom is the smallest unit taking part in a chemical reaction | Still true |

### The laws of chemical combination

| Law (author) | Statement | Test example |
|---|---|---|
| Conservation of mass (Lavoisier) | In a chemical change the total mass of the products equals the total mass of the reactants | 4 g H₂ + 32 g O₂ → 36 g H₂O |
| Definite (constant) proportions (Proust) | A pure compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed ratio by mass, whatever its source | water from the Koshi or from a laboratory: H : O = 1 : 8 |
| Multiple proportions (Dalton) | When two elements form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in a simple whole-number ratio | O per 28 g N in N₂O, NO, N₂O₃, NO₂, N₂O₅ = 16, 32, 48, 64, 80 → 1 : 2 : 3 : 4 : 5 |
| Reciprocal proportions (Richter) | When two elements each combine with a fixed mass of a third, the masses in which they do so are in the same ratio (or a simple multiple of it) as the ratio in which they combine with each other | 12 g C takes 4 g H (CH₄) and 32 g O (CO₂): H : O = 1 : 8, the same as in H₂O |
| Gaseous volumes (Gay-Lussac) | Gases react, and gaseous products form, in volumes bearing a simple whole-number ratio, at the same T and P | H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl is 1 : 1 : 2 by volume; N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ is 1 : 3 : 2 |

::: caution Where the laws fail — the favourite distractors
- **Conservation of mass** fails measurably only in **nuclear** reactions ($E = mc^2$);
  the mass change in a chemical reaction exists but is far too small to weigh.
- **Definite proportions** fails for **non-stoichiometric** (berthollide) compounds
  such as Fe₀.₉₅O, and when isotopes differ: H : O by mass is 1 : 8 in H₂O but 1 : 4 in
  D₂O.
- **Gay-Lussac's law** is about gas volumes only; volumes are *not* conserved
  (2 vol H₂ + 1 vol O₂ → 2 vol steam), but mass always is.
:::

::: example Testing the law of multiple proportions
**Problem.** Two oxides of nitrogen contain 63.6 % and 46.7 % nitrogen by mass. Show
that they obey the law of multiple proportions.

**Solution.** Oxygen per 1 g of nitrogen: first oxide 36.4/63.6 = 0.572 g; second oxide
53.3/46.7 = 1.141 g. Ratio 0.572 : 1.141 = **1 : 2**, a simple whole-number ratio (the
oxides are N₂O and NO).
:::

### Avogadro's law and its deductions

::: definition Avogadro's law (1811)
Equal volumes of all gases, measured at the same temperature and pressure, contain
equal numbers of **molecules**.
:::

Avogadro's law reconciled Gay-Lussac's volume ratios with atomic theory. If hydrogen
and chlorine were single atoms, 1 volume + 1 volume would give only 1 volume of HCl; the
observed 2 volumes require **diatomic** molecules: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. Its deductions are
the working tools of this chapter:

1. **Elementary gases such as H₂, N₂, O₂ and Cl₂ are diatomic.**
2. **M = 2 × VD.** Vapour density is the mass of a volume of gas divided by the mass of
   the same volume of hydrogen at the same T and P. Equal volumes hold equal numbers of
   molecules, so VD = M(gas)/M(H₂) = M/2.
3. **Molar volume.** One mole of any gas occupies **22.4 L at STP** (273 K, 1 atm) — so
   the density of a gas at STP is M/22.4 g L⁻¹. (At 273 K and 1 bar the molar volume is
   22.7 L; at 298 K and 1 atm it is 24.5 L. CEE problems use 22.4 L at STP unless they
   say otherwise.)
4. **Gas volumes react in the coefficient ratio.** At the same T and P, volume ratio =
   mole ratio, so gas–gas problems need no conversion to moles at all.

::: example Formula of a gas from volumes alone
**Problem.** 10 mL of a gaseous hydrocarbon needs exactly 30 mL of O₂ for complete
combustion and gives 20 mL of CO₂, all volumes at the same T and P. Find its formula.

**Solution.** For CₓHᵧ, volumes are in the ratio 1 : (x + y/4) : x. CO₂: x = 20/10 = 2.
O₂: x + y/4 = 30/10 = 3, so y = 4. The gas is **C₂H₄** (ethene).
:::

::: caution Equal volumes, not equal atoms
1 L of He and 1 L of O₃ at the same T and P contain the same number of molecules, but
the ozone contains three times as many atoms and twelve times the mass (48 against 4).
:::

## 1.4 The mole concept, limiting reactant and percentage yield

### The mole

::: definition Mole (SI, 2019)
The **mole** is the amount of substance that contains exactly
6.02214076 × 10²³ elementary entities (atoms, molecules, ions, electrons…). This number
is the **Avogadro constant**, N_A. Before 2019 the mole was defined as the number of
atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12 — numerically the same.
:::

The **molar mass** M (g mol⁻¹) has the same number as the relative molecular mass.
Every conversion goes through moles:

$$ n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{N}{N_A} = \frac{V_{\text{gas at STP}}}{22.4\ \text{L}} $$

**Counting what is inside.** Atoms = moles of molecules × N_A × atomicity. Electrons in
a molecule = sum of the atomic numbers (minus the charge for a cation, plus it for an
anion). Useful **ten-electron** species: H₂O, NH₃, CH₄, HF, Ne, NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, OH⁻, Na⁺,
Mg²⁺. N₂ and CO each have 14 electrons and the same molar mass, 28.

::: example Particles in a mass
**Problem.** For 4.4 g of CO₂ find the number of molecules, atoms and electrons
(N_A = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹).

**Solution.** n = 4.4/44 = 0.1 mol. Molecules = 0.1 × 6.02 × 10²³ = **6.02 × 10²²**.
Atoms = 3 × that = **1.81 × 10²³**. Electrons: CO₂ has 6 + 8 + 8 = 22, so
22 × 6.02 × 10²² = **1.32 × 10²⁴**.
:::

### Stoichiometry — the mole-ratio method

1. Write the balanced equation.
2. Convert what is given into moles.
3. Multiply by the coefficient ratio (wanted ÷ given).
4. Convert the answer into the unit asked for — grams, litres at STP, or particles.

For mass-to-mass problems the steps collapse into one line:
mass of B = mass of A × (b × M_B)/(a × M_A), where a and b are the coefficients.
From CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, 50 g of CaCO₃ (0.5 mol) gives 0.5 × 56 = 28 g CaO and
0.5 × 22.4 = 11.2 L CO₂ at STP. For a chain of reactions, follow one atom: in
S → SO₂ → SO₃ → H₂SO₄ every sulphur atom ends up in one H₂SO₄, so 32 g of sulphur
gives at most 98 g of acid (the **principle of atom conservation**).

### Limiting reactant

::: definition Limiting and excess reactant
The **limiting reactant** is the reactant that is used up first in a reaction; it
decides the maximum amount of product. The other reactants are present in **excess**
and are partly left over.
:::

**Rule:** for each reactant compute moles ÷ its coefficient; the **smallest** value
marks the limiting reactant. All products are calculated from it.

::: example Limiting reactant in ammonia synthesis
**Problem.** 28 g of N₂ and 9 g of H₂ react, N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, until one of them is used up.
Which reactant is limiting? What mass of NH₃ forms and what is left over?

**Solution.** N₂: 28/28 = 1 mol, 1/1 = 1. H₂: 9/2 = 4.5 mol, 4.5/3 = 1.5. N₂ gives the
smaller value, so **N₂ is limiting**. NH₃ = 2 × 1 = 2 mol = **34 g**. H₂ used = 3 mol;
left = 1.5 mol = **3 g**. Check: 28 + 9 = 34 + 3.
:::

::: caution "Smaller mass" or "fewer moles" is not the test
In the example above H₂ has the smaller mass (9 g against 28 g), yet N₂ is limiting.
And with 2 mol H₂ + 2 mol O₂ (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O) the moles are equal, yet H₂ is limiting
because its coefficient is 2. Always divide by the coefficient.
:::

### Percentage yield and purity

$$ \text{percentage yield} = \frac{\text{actual yield}}{\text{theoretical yield}} \times 100 $$

$$ \text{percentage purity} = \frac{\text{mass of pure substance}}{\text{mass of sample}} \times 100 $$

The **theoretical yield** is what the limiting reactant would give by the equation.
Real yields are lower because of side reactions, reversible or incomplete reaction and
losses during separation and purification. With an impure reactant, first find the mass
of the pure substance, then do the stoichiometry.

::: example Percentage yield of fermentation
**Problem.** Fermentation, C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂, of 90 g of glucose gives 36.8 g of
ethanol. Find the percentage yield. (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16)

**Solution.** Glucose = 90/180 = 0.5 mol → theoretical ethanol = 1 mol = 46 g.
Yield = 36.8/46 × 100 = **80 %**.
:::

::: tip Reading the options of a stoichiometry MCQ
Setters build the wrong options from predictable slips: using the coefficient ratio
upside down, forgetting the coefficient altogether (a 1 : 1 answer), ignoring purity
(the 100 % answer), or adding the masses of both reactants when one is in excess. If
two options differ by exactly your coefficient ratio, recheck that step before
choosing.
:::

|---|---|
| Atomic mass unit | 1 u = 1/12 × mass of a ¹²C atom = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g; 1 g = 6.02 × 10²³ u |
| Average atomic mass | Σ (fraction × isotopic mass); two isotopes: lighter + difference × fraction of heavier |
| Mass of one particle | M / N_A grams |
| Moles | n = m/M = N/N_A = V(STP)/22.4 L |
| Gas density at STP | ρ = M/22.4 g L⁻¹ |
| Vapour density | M = 2 × VD |
| Molecular formula | n × empirical formula; n = M ÷ empirical-formula mass |
| Mass % of an element | (atoms × A_r)/M × 100 |
| Combustion analysis | m(C) = m(CO₂) × 12/44; m(H) = m(H₂O) × 2/18 |
| O₂ for burning CₓHᵧO_z | x + y/4 − z/2 mol per mol |
| Limiting reactant | smallest (moles ÷ coefficient) |
| Yield and purity | % yield = actual/theoretical × 100; % purity = pure/sample × 100 |

| Law | Author | One-line test |
|---|---|---|
| Conservation of mass | Lavoisier | mass of reactants = mass of products (fails only in nuclear change) |
| Definite proportions | Proust | fixed mass ratio whatever the source (fails: Fe₀.₉₅O, isotopes) |
| Multiple proportions | Dalton | CO/CO₂, H₂O/H₂O₂, SO₂/SO₃, Cu₂O/CuO, the five N oxides |
| Reciprocal proportions | Richter | CH₄, CO₂ and H₂O |
| Gaseous volumes | Gay-Lussac | H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, 1 : 1 : 2 |
| Equal volumes, equal molecules | Avogadro | M = 2 VD; 22.4 L mol⁻¹ at STP |

Molar volumes: 22.4 L (273 K, 1 atm, the CEE value) · 22.7 L (273 K, 1 bar) ·
24.5 L (298 K, 1 atm). Atomicities: noble gases 1, O₃ 3, P₄ 4, S₈ 8.

## Chapter summary

- 1 u = 1/12 of the mass of a ¹²C atom = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁴ g; relative atomic and molecular masses are unitless ratios; the tabulated atomic mass is an isotope-weighted average.
- Formulae come from valencies by criss-cross; learn the radicals with their charges, and remember Hg₂²⁺ (Hg₂Cl₂), S₂O₃²⁻ and Cr₂O₇²⁻.
- Percentage of an element = (atoms × A_r)/M × 100; urea is 46.7 % N, CuSO₄·5H₂O is 36.1 % water.
- Empirical formula: % → ÷ A_r → ÷ smallest → whole numbers; molecular formula = n × empirical formula, with M = 2 × VD.
- Lavoisier (mass), Proust (definite), Dalton (multiple), Richter (reciprocal), Gay-Lussac (gas volumes), Avogadro (equal volumes, equal molecules).
- Dalton's indivisibility and identical-mass postulates failed (subatomic particles, isotopes, isobars); conservation of atoms in chemical change survives.
- One mole = 6.022 × 10²³ entities (exact since 2019) = M grams = 22.4 L of gas at STP; $n = m/M = N/N_A = V/22.4$.
- Limiting reactant = smallest moles ÷ coefficient; it fixes the product. % yield = actual/theoretical × 100.
