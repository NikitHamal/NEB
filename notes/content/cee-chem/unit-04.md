Chemical bonding carries about **two questions** in the paper (our estimate from the MEC
topic list), and its ideas — shape, hybridisation, polarity, hydrogen bonding — reappear
in organic, inorganic and states-of-matter questions. The favourite question types are:
the shape or hybridisation of a given molecule or ion, a bond-angle or dipole-moment order,
counting σ and π bonds, a Fajans'-rule comparison, and "why does water boil so high?".
One counting formula (the steric number) answers most shape questions in seconds.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- Ionic, covalent and coordinate bonds; where coordinate bonds occur (NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, H₃N→BF₃, O₃, CO, complex ions); exceptions to the octet rule.
- Steric number = bond pairs + lone pairs on the central atom; fast formula SN = ½(V + M − c + a).
- Shapes and angles: linear 180°, trigonal planar 120°, tetrahedral 109.5°, NH₃ 107°, H₂O 104.5°, TBP, octahedral, see-saw, T-shape, square planar.
- Hybridisation sp, sp², sp³, sp³d, sp³d², sp³d³; σ from head-on overlap, π from sideways overlap; single = σ, double = σ + π, triple = σ + 2π.
- Dipole moment μ = q × d (debye); symmetric molecules have μ = 0; NH₃ > NF₃; % ionic character = μ(observed)/μ(ionic) × 100.
- Fajans' rules; bond length falls with bond order and with s character.
- Hydrogen bonding (F, O, N), metallic bonding (electron sea) and van der Waals forces, with their effects on boiling point, solubility and density.
:::

## 4.1 Electronic theory of valency; ionic, covalent and coordinate bonds; Lewis structures

### The electronic theory of valency

Kossel and Lewis (1916) explained bonding by the stability of the noble gases: **atoms
combine so as to reach a stable noble-gas configuration** — eight outer electrons (an
**octet**), or two (a **duplet**) for H, Li and Be²⁺. They do this by transferring
electrons (ionic bond), by sharing them (covalent bond), or by sharing a pair that one atom
supplies (coordinate bond). Only the valence electrons take part; in a **Lewis symbol** they
are shown as dots round the element symbol.

### Ionic, covalent and coordinate bonds

| | Ionic (electrovalent) | Covalent | Coordinate (dative) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formed by | complete transfer of electrons | mutual sharing of electron pairs | sharing of a pair supplied by one atom (donor) to another (acceptor) |
| Favoured by | low IE of the metal, high EA of the non-metal, high lattice energy | similar, fairly high electronegativities | a lone pair on the donor, a vacant orbital on the acceptor |
| Example | Na⁺Cl⁻, Mg²⁺O²⁻ | H₂, Cl₂, CH₄, H₂O | NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, H₃N→BF₃ |
| Directional? | no (so no isomerism) | yes (so shapes and isomerism) | yes |

| Property | Ionic compounds | Covalent (molecular) compounds |
|---|---|---|
| State and melting point | crystalline solids, high mp and bp | gases, liquids or soft solids, low mp and bp |
| Conductivity | conduct when molten or in water, not as solids | non-conductors (except graphite, and those that ionise in water, like HCl) |
| Solubility | in polar solvents (water) | in non-polar solvents (benzene, CCl₄) |
| Reactions | fast (between ions) | slow (bonds must break) |

Giant covalent solids (diamond, SiO₂) are the exception to "low melting point": every atom
is held in a three-dimensional covalent network.

::: definition Coordinate bond
A **coordinate (dative) bond** is a covalent bond in which **both shared electrons come
from one atom**. It is shown by an arrow from the donor to the acceptor, as in
H₃N→BF₃. Once formed it is identical to an ordinary covalent bond: all four N–H bonds in
NH₄⁺ are the same.
:::

Coordinate bonds occur in NH₄⁺ (H⁺ ← :NH₃), H₃O⁺, H₃N→BF₃, Al₂Cl₆ (two Cl→Al bridges),
the complex ions such as [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺, and in the Lewis structures of O₃, CO (C≡O with
one dative bond), HNO₃ and N₂O₅.

### Lewis structures and formal charge

To draw one: (1) add up the valence electrons (add one for each negative charge, subtract
one for each positive charge); (2) put the least electronegative atom (never H) in the
centre and join the atoms by single bonds; (3) complete the octets of the outer atoms;
(4) put any electrons left on the central atom; (5) if the central atom is still short of
an octet, convert lone pairs on neighbours into double or triple bonds. The best structure
has the smallest **formal charges**:

$$ \text{formal charge} = V - L - \frac{B}{2} $$

where V = valence electrons of the free atom, L = non-bonding (lone-pair) electrons on it
and B = electrons in its bonds.

::: example Formal charges in carbon monoxide
**Problem.** Find the formal charges in the Lewis structure :C≡O: (one lone pair on each
atom, a triple bond).

**Solution.** C: V = 4, L = 2, B = 6 → 4 − 2 − 3 = **−1**. O: V = 6, L = 2, B = 6 →
6 − 2 − 3 = **+1**. The charges oppose the electronegativity difference, which is why CO
has a very small dipole moment (about 0.1 D).
:::

| Failure of the octet rule | Examples |
|---|---|
| Incomplete octet | BeCl₂ (4 electrons round Be), BF₃ and AlCl₃ (6) |
| Expanded octet (period 3 onwards, using d orbitals) | PCl₅ (10), SF₆ (12), IF₇ (14), H₂SO₄ (S has 12) |
| Odd-electron molecules | NO, NO₂, ClO₂ |
| Noble-gas compounds | XeF₂, XeF₄, XeF₆, XeO₃ |

The octet rule also says nothing about the **shapes** of molecules or the **energies** of
bonds — which is what the next two theories add.

## 4.2 VSEPR theory and shapes; valence bond theory, σ and π bonds, hybridisation

### VSEPR theory

The **valence shell electron pair repulsion** theory (Sidgwick and Powell; developed by
Nyholm and Gillespie) says:

1. The shape of a molecule depends on the number of electron pairs (bond pairs and lone
   pairs) round the central atom.
2. The pairs repel one another and take up positions as far apart as possible.
3. Repulsion falls in the order **lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond
   pair–bond pair**, so lone pairs squeeze the bond angles.
4. A double or triple bond counts as one region (one "pair") for shape.

**Steric number (SN)** = number of atoms bonded to the central atom + number of lone pairs
on it. The fast way to find it:

$$ SN = \frac{1}{2}\left(V + M - c + a\right) $$

V = valence electrons of the central atom; M = number of **monovalent** atoms attached
(H, F, Cl, Br, I — oxygen and sulphur count as 0); c = charge of a cation; a = charge of
an anion. Lone pairs = SN − number of bonded atoms. The SN also gives the hybridisation:
2 sp, 3 sp², 4 sp³, 5 sp³d, 6 sp³d², 7 sp³d³.

| SN | Bond pairs | Lone pairs | Shape | Angle | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 2 | 0 | linear | 180° | BeCl₂, CO₂, HCN, NO₂⁺ |
| 3 | 3 | 0 | trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃, SO₃, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻ |
| 3 | 2 | 1 | bent (V-shaped) | < 120° | SO₂ (119°), O₃ (117°), NO₂⁻ |
| 4 | 4 | 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄, CCl₄, NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻, ClO₄⁻ |
| 4 | 3 | 1 | trigonal pyramidal | 107° (NH₃) | NH₃, PCl₃, H₃O⁺, XeO₃, ClO₃⁻ |
| 4 | 2 | 2 | bent | 104.5° (H₂O) | H₂O, H₂S, OF₂, NH₂⁻ |
| 5 | 5 | 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120° | PCl₅, PF₅ |
| 5 | 4 | 1 | see-saw | — | SF₄, TeCl₄ |
| 5 | 3 | 2 | T-shaped | about 90° | ClF₃, BrF₃ |
| 5 | 2 | 3 | linear | 180° | XeF₂, I₃⁻, ICl₂⁻ |
| 6 | 6 | 0 | octahedral | 90° | SF₆, PCl₆⁻ |
| 6 | 5 | 1 | square pyramidal | — | BrF₅, IF₅ |
| 6 | 4 | 2 | square planar | 90° | XeF₄, ICl₄⁻ |
| 7 | 7 | 0 | pentagonal bipyramidal | 72°, 90° | IF₇ |

In a trigonal bipyramid, lone pairs always take the **equatorial** positions (they have
more room there), which is why SF₄ is a see-saw, ClF₃ a T and XeF₂ linear.

::: example Shapes by the counting formula
**Problem.** Find the hybridisation and shape of SF₄, ClF₃, XeF₄ and NO₃⁻.

**Solution.** SF₄: ½(6 + 4) = 5 → sp³d; 4 bonded atoms, 1 lone pair → **see-saw**.
ClF₃: ½(7 + 3) = 5 → sp³d; 3 bonded, 2 lone pairs → **T-shaped**.
XeF₄: ½(8 + 4) = 6 → sp³d²; 4 bonded, 2 lone pairs → **square planar**.
NO₃⁻: ½(5 + 0 + 1) = 3 → sp²; 3 bonded, no lone pair → **trigonal planar** (O counts 0).
:::

**Bond-angle orders worth knowing.**

- Lone pairs squeeze the angle: CH₄ (109.5°) > NH₃ (107°) > H₂O (104.5°).
- Down a group the central atom becomes less electronegative, the bond pairs sit farther
  from it and repel less: NH₃ (107°) > PH₃ (93.5°) > AsH₃ (92°); H₂O (104.5°) > H₂S (92°).
- Electronegative outer atoms pull the bond pairs away from the centre: NF₃ (102°) < NH₃
  (107°); OF₂ (103°) < H₂O (104.5°) < Cl₂O (111°, where the large Cl atoms also crowd each
  other).

### Valence bond theory: σ and π bonds

A covalent bond forms when **half-filled atomic orbitals of two atoms overlap** and the two
electrons, of opposite spin, pair up between the nuclei. The greater the overlap, the
stronger the bond.

| | Sigma (σ) bond | Pi (π) bond |
|---|---|---|
| Overlap | head-on, along the internuclear axis (s–s, s–p, p–p, hybrid orbitals) | sideways, of parallel p orbitals, above and below the axis |
| Strength | stronger (greater overlap) | weaker |
| Rotation about it | free | restricted (cis–trans isomerism) |
| Existence | can exist alone | forms only after a σ bond |
| Electron density | on the axis | above and below the axis (a nodal plane on the axis) |

A single bond is one σ; a double bond is σ + π; a triple bond is σ + 2π. Counting examples:
C₂H₆ 7σ; C₂H₄ 5σ + 1π; C₂H₂ 3σ + 2π; N₂ 1σ + 2π; CO₂ 2σ + 2π; HCN 2σ + 2π;
benzene 12σ + 3π.

### Hybridisation

::: definition Hybridisation
**Hybridisation** is the mixing of atomic orbitals of nearly equal energy on the **same
atom** to give the same number of new, **equivalent** orbitals (hybrid orbitals) with
definite directions. Hybrid orbitals form σ bonds or hold lone pairs; π bonds are formed
by the unhybridised p orbitals left over.
:::

| Hybridisation | Orbitals mixed | Geometry | Angle | s character | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| sp | s + p | linear | 180° | 50 % | BeCl₂, C₂H₂, CO₂ (C) |
| sp² | s + 2p | trigonal planar | 120° | 33 % | BF₃, C₂H₄, benzene, graphite |
| sp³ | s + 3p | tetrahedral | 109.5° | 25 % | CH₄, diamond, NH₃ and H₂O (with lone pairs) |
| sp³d | s + 3p + d | trigonal bipyramidal | 90°, 120° | 20 % | PCl₅, SF₄, ClF₃, XeF₂ |
| sp³d² | s + 3p + 2d | octahedral | 90° | 17 % | SF₆, XeF₄, BrF₅ |
| sp³d³ | s + 3p + 3d | pentagonal bipyramidal | 72°, 90° | 14 % | IF₇, XeF₆ (distorted) |

More s character means a shorter, stronger bond held closer to the nucleus, and a more
electronegative atom: sp C > sp² C > sp³ C. That is why ethyne's C–H hydrogen is acidic
(Unit 17).

## 4.3 Dipole moment, ionic character of covalent bonds and bond length

### Dipole moment

In a bond between atoms of different electronegativity the shared pair is pulled towards
the more electronegative atom, giving partial charges δ+ and δ−. The **dipole moment** is

$$ \mu = q \times d $$

(charge × distance). It is a **vector**; in chemistry its arrow is drawn from the positive
towards the negative end. Unit: the **debye**, 1 D = 3.33 × 10⁻³⁰ C m.

A molecule's dipole moment is the vector sum of its bond moments (and lone-pair moments).
**Symmetrical molecules have μ = 0** even with polar bonds: CO₂, BeCl₂, BF₃, CH₄, CCl₄,
PCl₅, SF₆, XeF₄, trans-1,2-dichloroethene and p-dichlorobenzene. Bent and pyramidal
molecules are polar: H₂O 1.85 D, NH₃ 1.47 D, SO₂, H₂S.

| Comparison | Order and reason |
|---|---|
| NH₃ (1.47 D) > NF₃ (0.23 D) | in NH₃ the lone-pair moment adds to the N←H bond moments; in NF₃ it opposes the N→F bond moments |
| HF > HCl > HBr > HI | electronegativity difference falls |
| CH₃Cl > CH₂Cl₂ > CHCl₃ > CCl₄ (= 0) | the C–Cl moments cancel more and more as the molecule becomes symmetrical |
| o- > m- > p-dichlorobenzene (= 0) | the angle between the two C–Cl moments widens from 60° to 120° to 180° |

Uses: a zero dipole moment proves a symmetrical shape (CO₂ is linear, H₂O is not); the
moment measures bond polarity and **percentage ionic character**:

$$ \%\ \text{ionic character} = \frac{\mu_{\text{observed}}}{\mu_{\text{ionic}}} \times 100, \qquad \mu_{\text{ionic}} = e \times d $$

::: example Percentage ionic character
**Problem.** A diatomic molecule has μ = 1.2 D and bond length 1.0 Å. Find its percentage
ionic character (e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, 1 D = 3.33 × 10⁻³⁰ C m).

**Solution.** If fully ionic, μ = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ = 1.6 × 10⁻²⁹ C m = 4.8 D.
% ionic = 1.2/4.8 × 100 = **25 %**. (Shortcut: e × 1 Å = 4.8 D.)
:::

### Ionic character in covalent bonds and Fajans' rules

Every polar covalent bond has some ionic character; a large electronegativity difference
(Δχ about 1.7 or more on the Pauling scale) makes a bond mainly ionic. Conversely, an ionic
bond has some covalent character when the cation **polarises** (distorts) the electron
cloud of the anion. **Fajans' rules** — covalent character increases when:

| Factor | Example |
|---|---|
| the cation is small | LiCl is more covalent than NaCl; BeCl₂ is covalent |
| the cation charge is high | NaCl < MgCl₂ < AlCl₃ in covalent character |
| the anion is large | LiI > LiBr > LiCl > LiF |
| the anion charge is high | covalent character rises from F⁻ to O²⁻ to N³⁻ |
| the cation has a pseudo-noble-gas (18-electron) shell | CuCl is more covalent than NaCl although Cu⁺ and Na⁺ are similar in size |

More covalent character means a lower melting point and better solubility in organic
solvents: NaCl (801 °C) > MgCl₂ (714 °C) > AlCl₃ (sublimes near 180 °C).

### Bond length, bond order and bond energy

**Bond length** is the equilibrium distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms. It
**decreases** as bond order increases, as the atoms get smaller and as the s character of
the hybrid orbitals increases; **bond energy** moves the other way.

| Bond | C–C | C=C (benzene) | C=C | C≡C | C–H (sp³ / sp² / sp) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Length (pm) | 154 | 139 | 134 | 120 | 109 / 108 / 106 |

In a molecule with **resonance** (O₃, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻, benzene) all the equivalent bonds have
the same intermediate length — the real structure is a hybrid of the Lewis structures, and
the bond order is averaged (C–O bond order 4/3 in CO₃²⁻). Halogen bond energies are an
exam favourite: Cl–Cl (243) > Br–Br (193) > F–F (158) > I–I (151 kJ mol⁻¹); F–F is
abnormally weak because the lone pairs on the two small F atoms repel each other.

## 4.4 Hydrogen bonding, metallic bonding and van der Waals forces

### Hydrogen bonding

::: definition Hydrogen bond
A **hydrogen bond** is the attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a small,
highly electronegative atom (F, O or N) and a lone pair on another such atom:
X–H···Y. It is much weaker than a covalent bond (about 10–40 kJ mol⁻¹ against about
400) but stronger than van der Waals forces.
:::

- **Intermolecular** hydrogen bonds join different molecules (H₂O, HF, NH₃, alcohols,
  carboxylic acids — which form dimers): they raise boiling points, viscosity and
  solubility in water.
- **Intramolecular** hydrogen bonds form within one molecule (o-nitrophenol,
  salicylaldehyde, salicylic acid): they *lower* the boiling point and the solubility,
  because the molecule's own H-bond is no longer available to its neighbours —
  o-nitrophenol is steam-volatile, p-nitrophenol is not.

Effects to quote: H₂O (100 °C), HF (19.5 °C) and NH₃ (−33 °C) boil far above the hydrides
of the next elements; **ice is less dense than water** because each water molecule is
hydrogen-bonded tetrahedrally to four others in an open cage, which collapses partly on
melting (water is densest at 4 °C); ethanol, glucose and ammonia dissolve in water; HF is a
weak acid and forms the HF₂⁻ ion (KHF₂); the double helix of DNA and the α-helix of
proteins are held by hydrogen bonds. Water boils higher than HF although F–H···F is the
stronger bond, because each H₂O molecule averages **two** hydrogen bonds (two H atoms and
two lone pairs) while HF averages one.

### Metallic bonding

In the **electron sea (electron gas) model**, a metal is a lattice of positive ions
(kernels) immersed in a "sea" of valence electrons that belong to the whole crystal and
move freely. The attraction between the kernels and the mobile electrons is the metallic
bond. It explains electrical and thermal **conductivity** (mobile electrons), **lustre**,
**malleability and ductility** (layers of kernels slide without breaking the bond) and
generally high melting points. The bond is stronger with **more valence electrons** and
**smaller kernels**: m.p. Na 98 °C < Mg 650 °C < Al 660 °C, and Li 181 °C > Na 98 °C >
K 63 °C. Transition metals, with many d electrons available, are very hard and high
melting; mercury, with a tightly held 6s² pair, is a liquid.

### van der Waals forces

**van der Waals forces** are the weak attractions between molecules (and noble-gas atoms):

| Type | Between | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Dipole–dipole (Keesom) | polar molecules | HCl, SO₂, CH₃Cl |
| Dipole–induced dipole (Debye) | a polar and a non-polar molecule | HCl with Ar; O₂ dissolved in water |
| London (dispersion) forces | all molecules, the only force between non-polar ones; due to momentary dipoles | noble gases, H₂, Cl₂, I₂, CH₄ |
| Ion–dipole (not van der Waals, but often asked with them) | an ion and a polar molecule | Na⁺ hydrated by water |

**London forces grow with the size of the electron cloud** (molar mass, polarisability)
and with the **surface area** of contact. Hence the boiling points rise He < Ne < Ar < Kr <
Xe; F₂ and Cl₂ are gases, Br₂ a liquid and I₂ a solid at room temperature; and among
isomers, branching lowers the boiling point: n-pentane (36 °C) > isopentane (28 °C) >
neopentane (9.5 °C).

| Force | Typical energy (kJ mol⁻¹) |
|---|---|
| Ionic bonding (lattice energy) | about 600–4000 |
| Covalent bond | about 150–1000 |
| Metallic bonding (enthalpy of atomisation) | about 60–850 |
| Hydrogen bond | about 10–40 |
| van der Waals forces | below about 10 |

::: caution Hydrogen bonds need F, O or N
A C–H hydrogen does not form hydrogen bonds — that is why CH₄ fits the normal boiling-point
trend while NH₃, H₂O and HF do not. And a hydrogen bond lies **between** an H on one
electronegative atom and a lone pair on another; HCl, with a large, less electronegative Cl,
forms only dipole–dipole attractions.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Atoms bond to reach a noble-gas configuration: ionic (transfer), covalent (sharing), coordinate (a pair donated by one atom — NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺, H₃N→BF₃). The octet rule fails for BF₃, PCl₅, SF₆, NO and xenon fluorides.
- Formal charge = V − L − B/2; the best Lewis structure minimises formal charges.
- VSEPR: shape from the steric number SN = ½(V + M − c + a); lp–lp > lp–bp > bp–bp squeezes angles: CH₄ 109.5° > NH₃ 107° > H₂O 104.5°.
- Lone pairs in a trigonal bipyramid go equatorial: SF₄ see-saw, ClF₃ T-shaped, XeF₂ linear; XeF₄ is square planar.
- σ bonds come from head-on overlap, π bonds from sideways overlap; double = σ + π, triple = σ + 2π. Hybridisation sp, sp², sp³, sp³d, sp³d², sp³d³ for SN 2–7; more s character, shorter and stronger bond.
- μ = q × d (debye); symmetric molecules have μ = 0; NH₃ > NF₃; % ionic character = μ(obs)/(e × d) × 100. Fajans: small, highly charged cations and large anions raise covalent character.
- Bond length falls as bond order and s character rise; resonance makes equivalent bonds equal.
- Hydrogen bonds (F, O, N) explain the high boiling points of H₂O, HF and NH₃, ice floating and o-nitrophenol's volatility; metallic bonds are an electron sea; London forces grow with size and surface area.

::: note Answers and explanations — Set A
**1.** (d) — The Kossel–Lewis theory: atoms transfer or share electrons to reach an octet (or duplet), the configuration of a noble gas.
**2.** (b) — In NH₄⁺ the nitrogen lone pair is donated to H⁺. CH₄ and H₂O have ordinary covalent bonds; NaCl is ionic.
**3.** (a) — SN 4 with no lone pair gives a tetrahedron (CH₄). Square planar needs two lone pairs on an SN of 6.
**4.** (c) — 109.5° (109° 28′). 104.5° is water, 120° trigonal planar, 90° octahedral.
**5.** (b) — Each C forms two σ bonds (to C and H) and two π bonds: SN 2, sp, linear.
**6.** (d) — Lone pairs are held by one nucleus only, spread wider and repel most.
**7.** (a) — Sideways (lateral) overlap of parallel p orbitals; head-on overlap gives σ bonds.
**8.** (c) — The debye, 1 D = 3.33 × 10⁻³⁰ C m. The SI unit is the coulomb metre, not the coulomb.
**9.** (a) — CO₂ is linear, so its two C=O moments cancel. Bent H₂O, pyramidal NH₃ and HCl are polar.
**10.** (d) — Only F, O and N are small and electronegative enough to leave H strongly δ+ and to offer a compact lone pair.
**11.** (c) — In ice each molecule is H-bonded tetrahedrally to four others, an open, low-density cage; water is densest at 4 °C, not 0 °C.
**12.** (b) — Metal kernels in a sea of mobile valence electrons (Drude–Lorentz model).
**13.** (c) — Momentary dipoles inducing dipoles: London (dispersion) forces, the only attraction between non-polar particles.
**14.** (a) — SN = ½(6 + 6) = 6, no lone pair: octahedral, all angles 90°.
**15.** (d) — SN = ½(5 + 5) = 5: sp³d, trigonal bipyramidal.
**16.** (b) — Boron in BF₃ has only six electrons round it (incomplete octet).
**17.** (a) — The ions must be free to move: in the melt or in solution. In the solid they are locked in the lattice.
**18.** (c) — C≡C 120 pm < C=C 134 pm < benzene 139 pm < C–C 154 pm: higher bond order, shorter bond.
**19.** (d) — N≡N is one σ and two π bonds.
**20.** (b) — A small, highly charged cation polarises the anion strongly. Noble-gas cations polarise less than pseudo-noble-gas ones of the same size.
:::

   (a) a π bond can exist without a σ bond (b) a π bond is stronger than a σ bond (c) the π bond prevents free rotation about a double bond (d) a triple bond has two σ bonds and one π bond

   (a) BF₃ (b) CCl₄ (c) CO₂ (d) CHCl₃

   (a) I₃⁻ (b) H₂O (c) SO₂ (d) NO₂⁻

   (a) hydrogen bonding increases (b) London forces grow with atomic size and polarisability (c) their ionisation energies increase (d) they begin to form covalent bonds

   (a) the number of valence electrons falls (b) the metals become non-metallic (c) larger kernels hold the electron sea less strongly (d) their atomic masses decrease

::: note Answers and explanations — Set B
**1.** (a) — All have SN 4; lone pairs squeeze the angle: 109.5° (0 lp) > 107° (1 lp) > 104.5° (2 lp).
**2.** (c) — SN = ½(7 + 3) = 5; three bond pairs and two equatorial lone pairs leave a T shape.
**3.** (b) — SN = ½(8 + 4) = 6: octahedral arrangement with the two lone pairs opposite each other (trans), leaving the four F atoms in a square.
**4.** (d) — Both are SN 4 with no lone pair: tetrahedral. NH₃ is pyramidal but BF₃ planar; H₂O bent but CO₂ linear; SF₄ see-saw but CH₄ tetrahedral.
**5.** (c) — In NF₃ the bond moments point from N to F, away from the lone pair, so they largely cancel its moment; in NH₃ all point the same way and add.
**6.** (a) — Only water forms hydrogen bonds; the others boil higher down the group only through London forces, still far below 100 °C.
**7.** (d) — The O–H···O=N bond forms inside the ortho molecule, so its molecules do not stick to one another; para molecules H-bond to their neighbours.
**8.** (b) — Al³⁺ is the smallest, most highly charged cation, so it polarises Cl⁻ the most (AlCl₃ sublimes at about 180 °C).
**9.** (a) — SN = ½(5 + 4 − 1) = 4 with four bonded H: sp³ tetrahedral, like CH₄. NH₃, with a lone pair, is the pyramidal one.
**10.** (c) — Twisting would break the sideways p–p overlap, so rotation about C=C is restricted; a π bond forms only after a σ bond and is weaker.
**11.** (d) — In CHCl₃ the three C–Cl moments and the C–H moment do not cancel. BF₃, CCl₄ and CO₂ are symmetrical.
**12.** (a) — I₃⁻: SN = ½(7 + 2 + 1) = 5, three equatorial lone pairs, the two I atoms axial: linear. H₂O, SO₂ and NO₂⁻ are bent.
**13.** (b) — Bigger atoms have bigger, more polarisable electron clouds, so the momentary dipoles and London forces are larger.
**14.** (c) — All have one valence electron; as the kernel grows, the mobile electrons are farther from it and the metallic bond weakens (Li 181 °C, Cs 28 °C).
:::

::: note Answers and explanations — Set C

**2.** (d) — SN = ½(7 + 4 + 1) = 6: sp³d², with two lone pairs, so the ion is square planar. dsp² is the square-planar hybridisation of complexes such as [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻, not of ICl₄⁻.
