Metallurgy is the branch of chemistry that turns a rocky ore into a useful metal, and this
chapter carries **about four of the fifty Chemistry questions** — one of the heaviest
inorganic units in the paper. The questions are of three kinds: a named step with its
definition (calcination, roasting, poling, zone refining), a metal with its ore, reagent or
characteristic compound (blue vitriol, white vitriol, calomel, corrosive sublimate), and a
property of the 3d series explained by electronic configuration or crystal field theory.
Almost every mark here is recall of a name, a formula or a colour, so the tables in this
chapter are the part to learn by heart.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- The vocabulary of extraction, each with one example: ore, mineral, gangue, flux, slag, concentration, calcination, roasting, smelting, bessemerisation, aluminothermy, electrolytic reduction, poling, electro-refining, zone refining.
- **Calcination = heating without air; roasting = heating in excess air.** Slag = flux + gangue; gangue is the impurity, slag is the product that removes it.
- Sodium by Down's cell (fused NaCl + CaCl₂, graphite anode, iron cathode); NaOH by Castner–Kellner (mercury cathode); Na₂CO₃ by Solvay with the ammonia-recovery loop.
- The 3d series: configuration, variable oxidation states, colour, paramagnetism, catalysis, alloy formation — and **Zn is not a transition metal** (d¹⁰ in atom and ion).
- Crystal field theory: t₂g below eg by Δ₀, high spin when Δ₀ < P, colour from d–d transitions, μ = √(n(n+2)) BM, the spectrochemical series.
- Copper: roast, smelt, bessemerise, blister copper, electro-refine; blue vitriol CuSO₄·5H₂O. Zinc: blende, roast, reduce with coke; white vitriol ZnSO₄·7H₂O.
- Mercury from cinnabar through HgO; calomel Hg₂Cl₂ against corrosive sublimate HgCl₂. Silver by cyanide leaching and the Parkes process. Iron in the blast furnace, zone by zone.
- Steel by the basic oxygen and open-hearth processes; rusting as an electrochemical cell and the four ways to stop it.
:::

## 14.1 Metallurgical principles

Few metals occur free. The reactive ones are found as oxides, sulphides, carbonates or
silicates, mixed with worthless rock, and the whole business of metallurgy is to separate
the metal compound from the rock and then pull the metal out of the compound. Every
extraction, from iron to sodium, follows the same five stages, and the paper tests the
name, the purpose and one example of each.

### The vocabulary, defined once

::: definition Ore, mineral and gangue
A **mineral** is a naturally occurring inorganic substance with a definite chemical
composition and a characteristic crystal structure. An **ore** is a mineral (or a mixture
of minerals) from which a metal can be extracted **profitably** — the word "profitably" is
part of the definition, which is why bauxite is an ore of aluminium and ordinary clay is
not. The **gangue** is the unwanted earthy matter — sand, clay, limestone, rock — mixed
with the ore.
:::

::: definition Flux and slag
A **flux** is a substance added to the charge to combine with the gangue and form a
fusible product. A **slag** is that fusible product. The slag melts at a lower temperature
than the gangue alone, floats on the molten metal because it is less dense, and protects
the metal from re-oxidation by the air.
:::

The choice of flux is fixed by the chemistry of the gangue, and this is the one rule worth
memorising:

| Gangue | Nature | Flux used | Slag formed |
|---|---|---|---|
| SiO₂ (sand) | acidic | basic flux: CaO (from limestone) | CaSiO₃ (calcium silicate) |
| CaO or MgO | basic | acidic flux: SiO₂ (sand) | CaSiO₃ or MgSiO₃ |
| FeO | basic | acidic flux: SiO₂ | FeSiO₃ |
| P₂O₅ | acidic | basic flux: CaO | Ca₃(PO₄)₂ |

**Acidic gangue needs a basic flux, and basic gangue needs an acidic flux.** The slag must
be of the opposite character to the gangue so that the two combine.

::: caution Gangue is not slag
Gangue is the impurity that is *already* in the ore; slag is the *new* compound made when
flux is added to it. A question that says "the impurity present in an ore is called" wants
gangue; one that says "the fusible mass formed by flux and impurity" wants slag. Mixing
these two up is the commonest single error in this unit.
:::

### Concentration (dressing) of the ore

**Concentration** is the removal of gangue from the crushed ore. It is a **physical**
separation: the chemical nature of the ore does not change, only its purity.

| Method | Used for | How it works |
|---|---|---|
| Hand picking | large lumps | visible difference in colour or lustre |
| Washing / levigation | oxide ores of Fe, Sn (heavy) | gangue is lighter and is washed away in a stream of water |
| **Froth flotation** | **sulphide ores** (Cu, Zn, Pb, Ag) | powdered ore + water + pine oil (frother) + a collector such as sodium ethyl xanthate; air is blown through. The sulphide particles are wetted by oil, stick to the froth and rise; the gangue is wetted by water and sinks |
| **Magnetic separation** | ores containing Fe₃O₄ (magnetite), and wolframite | the magnetic ore is held by an electromagnet while the non-magnetic gangue falls away |
| Gravity separation | oxide ores | the heavier ore settles faster on a shaking Wilfley table |
| **Leaching** | bauxite (with hot NaOH), Ag and Au (with dilute NaCN) | the ore is *dissolved* selectively in a reagent and recovered from the solution; used when the ore and the gangue differ chemically |

Froth flotation is the method the paper asks about most often, and its two-part logic is
worth stating: the **collector** makes the sulphide surface water-repellent, and the
**frother** makes a stable foam that carries those particles to the top. Depressants and
activators (NaCN, CuSO₄) are used to separate one sulphide from another in complex ores.

### Calcination and roasting — the two that are always confused

::: definition Calcination and roasting
**Calcination** is the heating of an ore strongly **in the absence of air** (or in a
limited supply of air) to drive off water and carbon dioxide and to decompose the
compound. **Roasting** is the heating of an ore strongly **in excess air** so that the ore
is oxidised and volatile impurities such as sulphur, arsenic and antimony are removed as
their oxides.
:::

| | Calcination | Roasting |
|---|---|---|
| Air | absent or limited | excess |
| What is removed | water of crystallisation, CO₂ | S (as SO₂), As, Sb, and carbon |
| Chemical change | decomposition | **oxidation** |
| Used for | carbonates, hydrated oxides | sulphide ores |
| Example | CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ | 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂ |
| Second example | Al₂O₃·2H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 2H₂O | 2CuFeS₂ + O₂ → Cu₂S + 2FeS + SO₂ |

A calcined ore is *decomposed*; a roasted ore is *oxidised*. Both leave a metal oxide
behind, which is why the next step is the same for both — reduction.

::: caution The two exceptions that give the game away
- Roasting a **sulphide** produces the **oxide plus SO₂** — never the metal directly, and
  never SO₃ at furnace temperature.
- Calcination of **limestone** is the same reaction as the one in a lime kiln, so a
  question about "the reaction in the zone of slag formation of a blast furnace" is
  asking about calcination, not roasting.
:::

### Reduction: smelting, aluminothermy and electrolysis

**Smelting** is the reduction of a roasted or calcined ore at high temperature in the
presence of a flux, giving molten metal and molten slag. The reducing agent is chosen from
the reactivity of the metal:

| Method | Reducing agent | Metals | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbon reduction (smelting) | coke, C | Zn, Fe, Pb, Sn | ZnO + C → Zn + CO |
| Carbon monoxide | CO | Fe (blast furnace) | Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ |
| **Aluminothermy** (thermite) | Al powder | Cr, Mn, Fe, and metals whose oxides are very stable | Cr₂O₃ + 2Al → 2Cr + Al₂O₃ |
| Hydrogen reduction | H₂ | W, Mo | WO₃ + 3H₂ → W + 3H₂O |
| **Electrolytic reduction** | electric current | Na, K, Mg, Ca, Al (very reactive) | 2NaCl → 2Na + Cl₂ |
| Self-reduction | the ore itself | Cu, Pb | Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂ |
| Displacement | a more reactive metal | Ag, Au | 2Na[Ag(CN)₂] + Zn → 2Ag + Na₂[Zn(CN)₄] |

**Aluminothermy** (the Goldschmidt process) works because aluminium has a very high
affinity for oxygen: the reaction is so exothermic that the temperature rises above
2000 °C and the metal comes out molten. The mixture of Fe₂O₃ and Al is the **thermite**
used to weld railway tracks. Metals low in the electrochemical series (Cu, Ag, Au, Hg) can
be obtained by heating their oxides alone; metals high in the series (Na, Mg, Al) can only
be obtained by electrolysis of a fused salt, because no chemical reducing agent is strong
enough.

### Refining

| Method | Principle | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| Liquation | the metal has a lower melting point than its impurities and is allowed to flow away | Sn, Pb, Bi |
| Distillation | the metal is volatile | Zn, Hg, Cd |
| **Poling** | molten metal is stirred with green wood; the gases (CH₄, CO) reduce the oxide back to metal | Cu (blister copper) |
| Cupellation | the impurity is oxidised and absorbed by a bone-ash cupel | Ag (from Pb) |
| **Electro-refining** | impure metal is the anode, pure metal the cathode; metal dissolves and redeposits | Cu, Zn, Ag, Al, Ni |
| **Zone refining** | a molten zone is moved slowly along a rod; impurities stay in the melt and are swept to one end | Si, Ge (semiconductor grade) |
| Vapour phase (Mond) | the metal forms a volatile compound which is decomposed elsewhere | Ni: Ni + 4CO → Ni(CO)₄ at 60 °C, decomposed at 180 °C |

**Electro-refining** of copper uses the impure blister copper as the **anode**, a thin sheet
of pure copper as the **cathode**, and acidified copper sulphate as the electrolyte. Copper
dissolves from the anode, migrates as Cu²⁺ and deposits on the cathode at 99.95 % purity;
less active impurities (Ag, Au, Pt) fall as **anode mud** and are recovered, while more
active ones (Fe, Ni, Zn) stay in solution. **Zone refining** relies on the fact that an
impurity is more soluble in the melt than in the solid: the molten zone carries the
impurity along and leaves pure crystal behind it, and repeating the pass gives the
99.9999999 % silicon needed for chips.

::: example Lime for an acidic gangue
**Problem.** A copper charge contains 120 kg of ore that is 10 % SiO₂ by mass. How much
limestone, 90 % pure CaCO₃, must be added to remove all the silica as slag?
(Ca = 40, Si = 28, O = 16, C = 12)

**Solution.** Mass of SiO₂ = 0.10 × 120 = 12 kg = 12/60 = 0.20 kmol.
CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃, so 0.20 kmol of CaO is needed = 0.20 × 56 = 11.2 kg.
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂, so CaCO₃ needed = 0.20 × 100 = 20.0 kg.
At 90 % purity: 20.0/0.90 = **22.2 kg of limestone**.
:::

## 14.2 Alkali and alkaline-earth metals; sodium, sodium hydroxide and sodium carbonate

### Occurrence and the trends down the group

The alkali metals (group 1: Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs) have the configuration **ns¹** and the
alkaline-earth metals (group 2: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba) have **ns²**. Both are so reactive that
they are never found free.

| Metal | Chief minerals |
|---|---|
| Na | rock salt NaCl, Chile saltpetre NaNO₃, borax Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O, feldspar |
| K | sylvine KCl, carnallite KCl·MgCl₂·6H₂O, saltpetre KNO₃ |
| Mg | magnesite MgCO₃, dolomite CaCO₃·MgCO₃, carnallite, sea water |
| Ca | limestone CaCO₃, gypsum CaSO₄·2H₂O, fluorspar CaF₂, apatite |

**Trends down group 1.** Atomic and ionic radius increase, ionisation energy and
electronegativity decrease, metallic character and reactivity increase, melting point
decreases (Li 180 °C to Cs 28.5 °C, because the atoms are held by weaker metallic bonding
as the size grows), density generally increases (Li 0.53 to Cs 1.87 g cm⁻³, with
potassium lighter than sodium as the one exception), and the **hydration enthalpy of the
ion decreases** — Li⁺ is the most heavily hydrated and therefore has the largest hydrated
radius but the highest hydration energy.

**Trends down group 2.** The same fall in ionisation energy and rise in reactivity, but the
group is harder, denser and much less reactive than group 1 because of the extra nuclear
charge and the pair of electrons. Two solubility trends are examined:

- **Hydroxides become more soluble down the group**: Be(OH)₂ is insoluble and amphoteric, Mg(OH)₂ is sparingly soluble, Ca(OH)₂ is fairly soluble, Ba(OH)₂ is very soluble.
- **Sulphates and carbonates become less soluble down the group**, and the **thermal stability of the carbonates increases** down the group, because the larger cation polarises the carbonate ion less.

**Chemical character.** Sodium reacts vigorously with water: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂. It
burns in air to the monoxide and, on strong heating, to the peroxide Na₂O₂. It dissolves in
liquid ammonia to give a deep blue solution of solvated electrons, which is a strong
reducing agent. Sodium is stored under kerosene because it would otherwise react with the
moisture and oxygen of the air. Calcium reacts with cold water more gently than sodium
(Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂), magnesium only with hot water or steam
(Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂), and beryllium not at all. Oxides: BeO and Al₂O₃ are amphoteric,
MgO and CaO are basic.

Flame colours are worth a line each: **Li crimson, Na golden yellow, K lilac, Rb red-violet,
Cs blue**; Ca brick red, Sr crimson, Ba apple green. Sodium street lamps are yellow for
this reason.

### Sodium by Down's cell

Sodium cannot be reduced by carbon or aluminium — it is too reactive — so it is made by
**electrolysis of fused sodium chloride**. The melting point of pure NaCl is 801 °C, too
high to work comfortably, so about 40 % **calcium chloride** is added: the mixture melts
near 600 °C and the CaCl₂ also lowers the solubility of the metal in the melt.

- **Anode:** graphite (carbon), at the centre. Chlorine is liberated: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.
- **Cathode:** a ring of iron gauze around the anode. Sodium is deposited: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na.
- The **bell** above the cathode collects the molten sodium, which is less dense than the
  melt and floats up; the chlorine is drawn off from the central hood. The two products
  must be kept apart, or they recombine.

$$ \text{2NaCl (fused)} \rightarrow \text{2Na} + \text{Cl}_2 $$

**Uses of sodium:** as a coolant in fast-breeder nuclear reactors (it has a low melting
point and a high heat capacity), in sodium-vapour lamps, in the manufacture of Na₂O₂,
NaCN and sodium amalgam, and as a reducing agent in organic chemistry.

### Sodium hydroxide — the Castner–Kellner process

**Castner–Kellner** electrolyses **concentrated brine** with a **flowing mercury cathode**
and a graphite anode. Sodium is discharged at the mercury in preference to hydrogen
because of the high overvoltage of hydrogen on mercury, and it dissolves to form **sodium
amalgam**. The amalgam is run into a separate decomposer where water oxidises it:

- At the mercury cathode: Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na (dissolves in Hg).
- At the graphite anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻.
- In the decomposer: 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂.

$$ \text{2NaCl} + \text{2H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{2NaOH} + \text{Cl}_2 + \text{H}_2 $$

The mercury is pumped back, so it is a continuous process, and the NaOH produced is very
pure and free of chloride. The older **diaphragm cell** and the modern **membrane cell**
do the same overall reaction without mercury; the membrane cell avoids both the mercury
hazard and the asbestos diaphragm, and is what new plants build.

**Properties and uses.** NaOH is a white, deliquescent solid that absorbs CO₂ from the air
(2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O) and dissolves in water with a large release of heat. It is
used in soap making (saponification of fats), in paper and textile processing, in rayon, in
petroleum refining, in the extraction of alumina from bauxite, and as a drain cleaner.

### Sodium carbonate — the Solvay (ammonia–soda) process

Solvay's genius was to use a **cheap, recyclable base** (ammonia) to make an **insoluble**
salt (sodium bicarbonate) precipitate out of brine, and then to recover the ammonia with
lime. Five steps, and every equation is examinable.

**Step 1 — carbon dioxide and lime.** Limestone is calcined in a kiln:
CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. The CO₂ goes to the carbonating tower; the CaO is slaked:
CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂, and the milk of lime goes to the ammonia-recovery tower.

**Step 2 — brine saturated with ammonia.** Purified brine is saturated with NH₃ in an
absorption tower. The ammonia makes the solution alkaline, which is what allows it to
absorb CO₂ in quantity.

**Step 3 — carbonation.** CO₂ is bubbled through the ammoniated brine under pressure:
NaCl + NH₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → NaHCO₃↓ + NH₄Cl. Sodium bicarbonate is only sparingly soluble,
and the presence of NH₄Cl and unchanged NaCl (the common-ion effect) pushes it out of
solution almost completely. It is filtered off.

**Step 4 — calcination of the bicarbonate.** 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O. The CO₂ is
returned to step 3, so no carbon dioxide is wasted.

**Step 5 — ammonia recovery.** The filtrate contains NH₄Cl. It is heated with milk of
lime: 2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → 2NH₃ + CaCl₂ + 2H₂O, and the ammonia is returned to step 2. This
loop is what makes the process economic: the only raw materials consumed are brine and
limestone, and the only waste is calcium chloride.

**Overall:** CaCO₃ + 2NaCl → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂.

**Properties and uses.** Anhydrous Na₂CO₃ (soda ash) is a white powder; the decahydrate
Na₂CO₃·10H₂O is **washing soda**, which effloresces in dry air. It is used in glass
making, soap and detergent manufacture, water softening (it precipitates Ca²⁺ as CaCO₃),
paper making, and in the laboratory as a primary standard for acids.
Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂.

::: caution Why Solvay cannot make potassium carbonate
The process depends on NaHCO₃ being **sparingly soluble** so that it crystallises out.
Potassium bicarbonate is much more soluble, so it never precipitates and the ammonia-soda
route fails for K₂CO₃ — potassium carbonate is still made from wood ash or by the
carbonation of KOH.
:::

::: example Soda ash from brine
**Problem.** A Solvay plant treats 585 kg of pure NaCl a day with excess limestone and
ammonia, and the overall yield of Na₂CO₃ is 80 %. What mass of soda ash is obtained?
(Na = 23, Cl = 35.5, C = 12, O = 16)

**Solution.** NaCl = 585/58.5 = 10 kmol. From CaCO₃ + 2NaCl → Na₂CO₃ + CaCl₂,
2 mol NaCl give 1 mol Na₂CO₃, so 10 kmol NaCl give 5 kmol Na₂CO₃.
Mass = 5 × 106 = 530 kg at 100 %; at 80 %: 530 × 0.80 = **424 kg**.
:::

## 14.3 3d transition metals: complex shapes and crystal field theory

A **transition metal** has an incomplete d subshell in some common oxidation state
(Sc → Zn across the 3d row; Zn, with only d¹⁰ Zn²⁺, sits on the honorary bench). The nearness
of 4s and 3d energies yields the family's party tricks: **variable oxidation states**
(Mn: +2 to +7), **coloured compounds**, **paramagnetism** (unpaired d electrons, counted by
the spin-only moment $\mu = \sqrt{n(n+2)}$ BM conceptually), **catalysis** (empty d orbitals
and easy redox swapping), and a passion for **complex ions** — a central metal accepting
lone pairs from **ligands** (NH₃, H₂O, Cl⁻, CN⁻, en…; chelating ligands like EDTA grip with
several teeth — and are used clinically to drag Pb²⁺ out of poisoned patients, Ch. 15).

| Coordination no. | Geometry | Familiar examples |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | linear | [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ (Tollens'), [AuCl₂]⁻ |
| 4 | tetrahedral | [Zn(NH₃)₄]²⁺, [NiCl₄]²⁻, [FeCl₄]⁻ |
| 4 | square planar | [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻, [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] (cisplatin — the cis form is the anticancer drug; geometry is destiny) |
| 6 | octahedral | [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻, [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺, [Cr(H₂O)₆]³⁺, haem's Fe |

**Crystal field theory** treats ligands as point charges approaching the metal. In an
**octahedral** complex they march down the x, y, z axes — straight at the
d$_{z^2}$ and d$_{x^2-y^2}$ orbitals (**e_g**, repelled upward) while d$_{xy}$, d$_{yz}$,
d$_{zx}$ point between the axes (**t₂g**, sheltered, lower). The gap is **Δo**, sized by
the ligand (spectrochemical series: I⁻, Br⁻, Cl⁻, F⁻, OH⁻, H₂O < NH₃, en < CN⁻, CO),
the metal's oxidation state and row. Electrons then face a rent-vs-roommates decision:
if Δo is small (weak field), they spread out obeying Hund (high spin, e.g.
[FeF₆]³⁻ d⁵ all unpaired); if Δo is large (strong field), they pair up in t₂g first
(low spin, e.g. [Fe(CN)₆]³⁻ with one unpaired). Absorbing a photon of energy Δo promotes
an electron t₂g → e_g: the absorbed colour is removed and the **complementary colour is seen**
(red absorbed → blue-green observed, as in Cu²⁺'s aqua complex; d⁰ Sc³⁺ and d¹⁰ Zn²⁺ have no
d–d moves and stay colourless).

## 14.4 Copper, zinc, mercury, silver and iron (with their signature salts)

**Copper** — chief ore **copper pyrites/chalcopyrite CuFeS₂** (also cuprite Cu₂O, malachite
CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂, azurite). Froth-float, roast gently (only part-oxidize), smelt to a **matte**
of Cu₂S + FeS, slag off Fe as FeSiO₃, bessemerize to blister copper, **electrorefine**
(acidsulphate bath: impure anode dissolves, pure Cu plates the cathode; Ag, Au, Pt drop as
valuable **anode mud**; more reactive impurities stay in solution). The metal is the
wiring/coinage standard (alloys brass Cu–Zn, bronze Cu–Sn). **Blue vitriol, CuSO₄·5H₂O**,
is the signature salt: deep blue crystals (4 waters coordinate Cu²⁺, the 5th is
H-bonded) turning **white on heating** as the anhydrous salt forms — the classic test for
water; with lime it makes Bordeaux mixture, the vineyards' fungicide; Fehling's solution
uses its tartrate complex to detect reducing sugars (Ch. 19). In moist air copper slowly
coats with green basic carbonate — the skin of old roofs and temple bells.

**Zinc** — ores **zinc blende ZnS** and **calamine ZnCO₃**. Roast/calcine to ZnO, reduce
with coke at ~1400 K; zinc (b.p. 1180 K) leaves as **vapour and is condensed** (a built-in
distillation refining; care to stop it reoxidizing). Amphoteric like Al (Zn + 2NaOH (hot) →
Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂), it galvanizes iron (sacrificial coat, Ch. 10) and fills dry cells (the can
is the anode). **White vitriol, ZnSO₄·7H₂O**, is its colourless everyday salt; ZnO is
"philosopher's wool" (from burning Zn) and calamine lotion's ZnO/Fe₂O₃ is a first-aid-box
citizen.

**Mercury** — from **cinnabar HgS**, which merely roasts in air:
HgS + O₂ → Hg + SO₂ (the oxide first formed also self-reduces: 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂ when hot).
The liquid metal (m.p. −39 °C, vapour poisonous) amalgamates most metals (not iron — hence
iron flasks), fills thermometers and barometers (retreating, post-Minamata conventions —
Ch. 15). Two chlorides must never be confused: **calomel, Hg₂Cl₂** — white, Hg(I) — with
NH₄OH turns **black** (HgNH₂Cl + Hg), the test for Hg₂²⁺; and **corrosive sublimate,
HgCl₂** — Hg(II), violently poisonous protein coagulant (albumen is the antidote:
milk/egg-white give it innocent protein to curdle), whose solution with excess KI gives
the Nessler complex (K₂[HgI₄]).

**Silver** — native and as **argentite Ag₂S**; extracted by the **cyanide process**:
Ag₂S + 4NaCN ⇌ 2Na[Ag(CN)₂] + Na₂S (air oxidizes the sulphide on), then zinc dust
displaces the metal: 2Na[Ag(CN)₂] + Zn → Na₂[Zn(CN)₄] + 2Ag↓. Best electrical conductor of
all metals; untarnished by air alone but blackened by trace H₂S (Ag₂S film); AgNO₃
(lunar caustic) blackens skin (to Ag) and with halides gives the Ch. 13 precipitates;
AgBr emulsions powered a century of photography (light → Ag nuclei → developer amplifies).

**Iron** — ores **haematite Fe₂O₃** (chief), magnetite Fe₃O₄, siderite FeCO₃, iron pyrites
FeS₂ (usually left for its sulphur). The **blast furnace** is charged with ore + coke +
limestone; descending solids meet rising heat: coke burns at the tuyères (≈ 2000 K), CO
forms (C + CO₂ ⇌ 2CO) and does the reducing upstairs
(Fe₂O₃ → Fe₃O₄ → FeO → Fe), limestone calcines and its CaO fluxes silica to a molten
**CaSiO₃ slag** floating on the iron. Out flows **pig iron** (~4 % C plus Si, Mn, P, S —
hard, brittle, low-melting) much of it cast directly (**cast iron**, ~3 % C) or converted:
**wrought iron** (pig iron's carbon burnt out in a puddling furnace — the purest, softest,
weldable form) and **steel** (0.1–1.5 % C, tuned by alloying).

## 14.5 Steel: basic oxygen and open hearth; corrosion of iron

| | Basic oxygen process (LD) | Open-hearth (Siemens–Martin) |
|---|---|---|
| Charge | ~70 % molten pig iron + scrap, lime flux | pig iron + scrap + ore, any proportions |
| Heat source | oxidation itself — a water-cooled lance blows **pure O₂** onto the melt (no fuel) | producer-gas flames over a shallow hearth, regenerative chequer-work recycles waste heat |
| Chemistry | C → CO/CO₂; Si, Mn, P oxidized into the basic slag (CaO-rich lining grabs P as calcium phosphate) | same oxidation, slower and sampled as it goes |
| Time/control | ~20–30 min per heat, huge tonnage, less mid-course correction | 6–10 hours, analysis before tapping — the quality-control classic, now largely superseded |
| Nitrogen | low (pure O₂: no air N₂) — better than old Bessemer (air-blown: brittle nitrided steel) | moderate |

**Corrosion (rusting)** is an electrochemical duet (Ch. 10) needing water *and* oxygen
(both — the classic three-tube school proof): at anodic spots Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻; at
cathodic spots O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻; the Fe²⁺ is further oxidized by air to hydrated
ferric oxide, **Fe₂O₃·xH₂O**, the flaky, non-protective rust (in the dry-salt air of the
Terai winter and the monsoon's wet both electrolytes and O₂ abound). Salts accelerate
(conductivity), acid rain too; attached nobler metals (Cu rivets) create ruthless local cells.
Defences: barrier coats (paint, oil, plastic, tin plate — works only until scratched,
whereupon Fe, the baser metal, rusts *faster*), **galvanizing** (Zn is baser still: it
corrodes sacrificially and protects even at scratches — the tin/zinc contrast is a
perennial question), cathodic protection with Mg blocks on pipelines and hulls,
alloying (stainless steel's Cr₂O₃ skin), and keeping the metal dry/earthed in design.

::: example Worked example 14.1 — following electrons in refining
**Problem.** In copper electrorefining a current is passed for 10 hours depositing
3.175 kg of pure copper at the cathode. (a) Average current used (F = 96 500 C). (b) What
happens to the Ag and Fe impurities in the anode?

**Solution.** (a) Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu: moles = 3175/63.5 = 50 mol ⇒ 100 F of charge
= 9.65×10⁶ C over 36 000 s ⇒ I = 268 A (refineries do indeed run hundreds of amps per
cell, many cells in series). (b) Silver (and Au, Pt) — nobler than Cu — never ionizes:
as the anode dissolves around it, it simply falls as **anode mud** (recovered eagerly).
Iron/zinc, baser than copper, ionize but their ions lose the discharge race to Cu²⁺ at the
cathode and accumulate in the electrolyte — icily obedient to Ch. 10's series.
:::

::: caution Tin plate vs galvanized iron at a scratch
Tin is *nobler* than iron: a pinhole in tinplate makes the exposed iron the anode of a
cell and rusting accelerates there. Zinc is *baser*: a scratch in galvanizing sacrifices
the surrounding zinc and the iron stays cathodic and safe. One sentence, one recurring mark.
:::

::: example Water out of blue vitriol
**Problem.** 25.0 g of blue vitriol CuSO₄·5H₂O (M = 250) is heated until
constant mass at ~230 °C. What mass of white residue remains, and what
mass of water left?
**Solution.** Moles of vitriol = 25.0/250 = 0.1 mol; each mole carries one

leaves is the water of crystallisation holding the copper's d–d colour —
anhydrous CuSO₄ is white until a drop of water returns the blue, the
classic test for water itself.

## Chapter summary

- Ores are concentrated (flotation owns the sulphides), converted to oxides (calcination
  without air, roasting with it), then reduced by the cheapest agent the Ellingham ladder
  allows — carbon for the middle class, electricity for the aristocrats, self-reduction
  for copper and mercury.
- Refining polishes with green poles, electric baths and moving molten zones; impurities
  are sorted by the same electrochemical series that runs the cells.
- The s-block supplies sodium's industrial trio (metal, hydroxide, carbonate) by Downs,
  chlor-alkali and Solvay routes, and calcium's lime cycle closes on itself.
- Transition metals wear complexes whose colours, spins and geometries crystal-field
  splitting predicts — from tetraammine blue to chemotherapy's cisplatin.
- Iron's blast furnace and the two steel converters turn carbon content into a dial
  (pig ≫ cast > steel > wrought), while rust — a wet electrochemical cell — is fought by
  smarter neighbours: zinc above all.
