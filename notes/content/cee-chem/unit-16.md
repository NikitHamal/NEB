Organic chemistry becomes manageable when you stop memorizing molecules one at a time and
start reading their architecture. This chapter, worth **about 3 questions**, supplies that
grammar: how carbon builds frameworks, how functional groups create families, how IUPAC names
encode structure, how the same formula can represent different substances, and how electrons
move when bonds break. These ideas decide nearly every mechanism in Chapters 17–20.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- Carbon is **tetravalent** (four covalent bonds in neutral compounds) and strongly **catenating**: it links to itself in long chains, branches and rings, using single, double and triple bonds.
- A **functional group** is the reactive atom or bond pattern defining a family. A **homologous series** has the same functional group and general formula, with successive members differing by CH₂ (14 mass units).
- Remove H from an alkane to get an **alkyl group** (CH₃–, C₂H₅–). Remove H from an arene ring to get an **aryl group** (phenyl, C₆H₅–). Benzyl (C₆H₅CH₂–) is an aralkyl group, not an aryl group.
- Count unsaturation before drawing isomers: degree of unsaturation $= (2C + 2 + N - H - X)/2$. Each ring or π bond contributes one; a triple bond contributes two. Oxygen does not enter the formula.
- IUPAC naming: identify the senior functional group, choose the parent chain containing it (and maximum multiple bonds), number for lowest locants, then cite substituents alphabetically. Principal-group order for school work: acid > anhydride > ester > acid halide > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > amine > alkene > alkyne.
- **Structural isomers** differ in connectivity: chain, position, functional-group, metamerism and tautomerism. **Stereoisomers** have the same connectivity but different spatial arrangement: geometrical (cis/trans or E/Z) and optical isomerism are the school forms.
- A carbon carrying four different groups is normally a stereogenic/chiral centre. Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images; diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images; a meso compound is achiral through internal symmetry despite stereocentres.
- **Homolysis** splits a bond one electron to each atom and gives free radicals (fishhook arrows). **Heterolysis** sends both electrons to one atom and gives ions (double-headed curly arrow).
- Carbocation stability generally increases methyl → 1° → 2° → 3°, and allylic/benzylic resonance gives extra stabilization. Simple alkyl carbanions show the reverse substitution trend. Free radicals broadly follow the carbocation order.
- An **electrophile** accepts an electron pair; a **nucleophile** donates one. Carbocations are electrophiles, carbanions are nucleophiles.
- The **inductive effect** is permanent σ-electron displacement toward a more electronegative atom/group (−I) or away from a donor (+I), weakening rapidly with distance. **Resonance** delocalizes π electrons or a lone pair over a conjugated system; the true structure is the hybrid, not a mixture flickering between drawings.
:::

## 16.1 Tetravalency and catenation; classification; alkyl and aryl groups; functional groups and homologous series

Carbon has four valence electrons. In ordinary neutral organic compounds it shares four pairs
of electrons, acquiring an octet while retaining a formal charge of zero. The four bonds may be
arranged as four single bonds (tetrahedral, sp³), a double and two single bonds (trigonal
planar, sp²), or a triple and one single bond/two double bonds (linear, sp). Charged carbon is
the deliberate exception encountered later in the chapter: a carbocation has three bonds and an
empty p orbital; a carbanion has three bonds and a lone pair.

**Catenation** is self-linking. It is exceptionally strong in carbon because the small atom forms
strong C–C and C–H bonds and can also use p orbitals effectively in multiple bonds. Chains can
branch, close into rings and support many functional groups. Silicon sits below carbon but forms
weaker Si–Si bonds and much stronger Si–O bonds, so silicon chemistry flows toward silicate
networks rather than an equally rich family of discrete Si-chain molecules.

### Classification by carbon skeleton

| Class | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Acyclic/aliphatic | Open chain, straight or branched | butane, 2-methylpropane, but-2-ene |
| Alicyclic | Ring with broadly aliphatic behaviour | cyclopropane, cyclohexane |
| Aromatic | Cyclic conjugated system with special stability; benzene is the prototype | benzene, phenol, aniline |
| Heterocyclic | Ring contains an atom other than carbon | furan (O in ring), pyridine (N in ring), thiophene (S in ring) |

Compounds are also called **saturated** when the carbon framework has only single bonds and
**unsaturated** when it contains a double bond, triple bond or ring equivalent. A ring creates
the same hydrogen deficiency as one π bond even though cycloalkanes do not behave like alkenes in
addition reactions.

### Alkyl, aryl and related groups

A group obtained by removing one hydrogen from an alkane is an **alkyl group**, general formula
CₙH₂ₙ₊₁–: methyl CH₃– (from methane), ethyl CH₃CH₂–, propyl CH₃CH₂CH₂– and
propan-2-yl/isopropyl (CH₃)₂CH–. The atom bearing the free valence dictates names such as
butan-2-yl. Carbon atoms are described as 1°, 2°, 3° or 4° according to how many *other carbons*
are attached to them; alcohols and alkyl halides are similarly classified by the carbon carrying
–OH or halogen.

An **aryl group** is formed by removing hydrogen directly from an aromatic ring. Phenyl is
C₆H₅–; a methylphenyl group is tolyl. By contrast, C₆H₅CH₂– is **benzyl**: its free valence is on
a saturated carbon next to the ring, making it an aralkyl group. This distinction matters in
reactivity — a benzylic carbocation or radical is resonance-stabilized through the benzylic
carbon, whereas a phenyl cation has its empty orbital in the ring plane and is very unstable.
Two other pattern names recur: **vinyl**, CH₂=CH–, and **allyl**, CH₂=CH–CH₂–.

### Functional groups and homologous series

The carbon skeleton is the stage; the functional group is usually where the action occurs.
Members of one family therefore have similar chemistry, while physical properties change
gradually as the hydrocarbon part grows.

| Family | Functional pattern | Typical suffix/prefix | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkane | C–C, C–H only | -ane | propane |
| Alkene | C=C | -ene | but-2-ene |
| Alkyne | C≡C | -yne | pent-1-yne |
| Alcohol | –OH on carbon | -ol / hydroxy- | propan-2-ol |
| Ether | C–O–C | alkoxy- (or ether name) | methoxyethane |
| Aldehyde | –CHO, terminal carbonyl | -al / oxo- | butanal |
| Ketone | C–CO–C | -one / oxo- | pentan-3-one |
| Carboxylic acid | –CO₂H | -oic acid | ethanoic acid |
| Ester | –CO₂R | alkyl …-oate | methyl ethanoate |
| Acid halide | –COX | -oyl halide | ethanoyl chloride |
| Amide | –CONH₂ or substituted | -amide | ethanamide |
| Nitrile | –C≡N | -nitrile / cyano- | propanenitrile |
| Amine | –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂ | -amine / amino- | ethanamine |
| Haloalkane | C–F/Cl/Br/I | halo- prefix | 2-bromobutane |
| Nitro compound | –NO₂ | nitro- prefix | nitroethane |

A **homologous series** shares a general formula and functional group, has a common method of
preparation, shows similar chemical reactions and displays gradual changes in melting point,
boiling point, density and solubility. Adjacent members differ by –CH₂–. Useful general formulae
for acyclic compounds are:

| Series | General formula | Also fits |
|---|---|---|
| Alkanes | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | — |
| Alkenes | CₙH₂ₙ | cycloalkanes |
| Alkynes | CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ | dienes, cycloalkenes |
| Monohydric alcohols/ethers | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂O | each other (functional isomers) |
| Aldehydes/ketones | CₙH₂ₙO | each other |
| Carboxylic acids/esters | CₙH₂ₙO₂ | each other |

The **degree of unsaturation** (DU, also index of hydrogen deficiency) packs that comparison
into one calculation:

$$
\mathrm{DU}=\frac{2C+2+N-H-X}{2}
$$

Halogen X is counted as hydrogen because both are monovalent; nitrogen adds one available
hydrogen equivalent; divalent oxygen or sulfur does not alter the count. Benzene,
C₆H₆, has DU = 4 — one ring plus three π bonds. DU never tells you *which* rings or π bonds are
present, but it prevents impossible structures when enumerating isomers.

## 16.2 IUPAC nomenclature of aliphatic compounds and isomerism

### A reliable four-pass naming routine

1. **Seniority pass.** Find the principal functional group. It decides the suffix and should be
   included in the parent chain. The carbon of –COOH, –CHO or –CN is counted in the chain.
2. **Parent pass.** Choose the longest continuous carbon chain containing the principal group;
   if there is a tie, prefer the chain with more multiple bonds, then more substituents.
3. **Numbering pass.** Give the principal group the lowest possible locant. Next consider
   multiple bonds (for an enyne, the direction giving the lower locant to the first cited
   multiple bond, normally the alkene), then substituents as a set. If a set is tied, give the
   lower locant to the substituent cited first alphabetically.
4. **Assembly pass.** Write substituents alphabetically with locants, ignoring multiplicative
   prefixes such as di- and tri- in alphabetization. Separate numbers with commas and numbers
   from words with hyphens: 3-ethyl-2,4-dimethylhexane.

When a lower-priority group is present it becomes a prefix: –OH is **hydroxy-** in the presence
of a ketone, C=O is **oxo-** in the presence of a carboxylic acid, –NH₂ is **amino-**, –OR is
**alkoxy-**, and halogens and –NO₂ are always prefixes in substitutive names. The parent chain
name drops its terminal “e” before a vowel-starting suffix (pentan-2-one), while locants for
unsaturation are placed immediately before -ene or -yne in modern format (but-2-ene,
pent-1-yne).

::: example Worked example 16.1 — name from the functional group outward
**Problem.** Name CH₃–C(CH₃)₂–CH₂–CHO and calculate its degree of unsaturation.

**Solution.** The aldehyde carbon must be C-1. Numbering from –CHO gives
CHO = C1, CH₂ = C2, the substituted carbon = C3 and one methyl carbon completes a four-carbon
butanal parent. Two additional methyl groups sit on C3, so the name is
**3,3-dimethylbutanal**. Its molecular formula is C₆H₁₂O. Hence
DU = (2×6 + 2 − 12)/2 = 1, accounted for by C=O. The trap is to call the branched left-hand
end “tert-butyl” and lose the requirement that the aldehyde carbon begins the parent chain.
:::

### Isomerism: same formula, different substance

**Isomers** have the same molecular formula but different structures and therefore at least some
different properties. First ask whether atom-to-atom connections differ.

**Structural (constitutional) isomerism**

- **Chain isomerism:** different carbon skeleton. C₄H₁₀ is CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ (butane) or
  (CH₃)₃CH (2-methylpropane).
- **Position isomerism:** same skeleton and functional group at a different position. Butan-1-ol
  and butan-2-ol; but-1-ene and but-2-ene.
- **Functional-group isomerism:** different functional family. C₂H₆O is ethanol or
  methoxymethane; C₃H₆O is propanal or propanone.
- **Metamerism:** different alkyl groups flank a polyvalent functional atom/group, as in
  ethoxyethane versus 1-methoxypropane (both C₄H₁₀O), or different substitution patterns around
  a ketone/secondary amine.
- **Tautomerism:** rapidly interconverting structural isomers differing chiefly in position of
  a proton and a π bond, classically keto ⇌ enol. For simple ketones the keto form dominates
  overwhelmingly. Tautomers are distinct chemical species in equilibrium; resonance forms are not.

Useful alkane counts are C₄H₁₀ = 2, C₅H₁₂ = 3, C₆H₁₄ = 5 and C₇H₁₆ = 9 structural isomers.
When counting substituted compounds, work skeleton by skeleton and mark symmetry-equivalent
positions only once.

**Stereoisomerism**

**Geometrical isomerism** occurs when rotation is restricted — most often about C=C, but also in
some rings. Each alkene carbon must carry two different substituents. In but-2-ene, cis has the
two CH₃ groups on the same side and trans has them opposite. When all four substituents are
different, cis/trans language has no clear reference pair; the **E/Z system** assigns priority
to the two groups on each carbon by atomic number (Cahn–Ingold–Prelog rules). High-priority
groups on the same side give **Z** (*zusammen*, together); opposite sides give **E**
(*entgegen*, opposite).

**Optical isomerism** is the school label for chirality at stereogenic centres. A tetrahedral
carbon attached to four different groups lacks a symmetry plane through that arrangement and
exists as two **enantiomers**. Enantiomers rotate plane-polarized light by equal magnitudes in
opposite directions and have identical melting points, boiling points and spectra in achiral
environments. In a chiral environment — an enzyme pocket, receptor or chiral reagent — they can
behave very differently, which is why drug stereochemistry matters. An equal mixture is a
**racemate** and shows no net rotation (external compensation).

Stereoisomers that are not mirror images are **diastereomers**; they generally have different
physical properties and can be separated by ordinary crystallization or chromatography. A
**meso** compound contains stereocentres but also an internal symmetry element, so the molecule
is superposable on its mirror image and optically inactive (internal compensation). Thus “has
two stereocentres” does not automatically mean “has four optically active forms”. Likewise,
R/S is a configuration label assigned from viewing priorities; it does not by itself predict
whether rotation is (+) or (−).

::: caution Two counting traps
Identical drawings produced by turning the paper over are not new isomers, and resonance forms
are not isomers. Conversely, a chiral constitutional isomer represents a pair of enantiomers when
a question asks for total stereoisomers. Always check whether the wording says *structural*,
*stereoisomers* or *optically active* forms.
:::

## 16.3 Bond fission, reactive intermediates and electronic effects

### Homolytic and heterolytic fission

A covalent bond contains two electrons, so there are only two ways to divide them.

| Feature | Homolysis | Heterolysis |
|---|---|---|
| Electron division | One electron to each fragment | Both electrons to one fragment |
| Products | Two free radicals | A cation and an anion |
| Arrow | Single-barbed “fishhook” arrow | Double-barbed curly arrow |
| Favoured by | Heat, UV light, peroxides; nonpolar/gas-phase conditions | Polar solvents and polarized bonds; stable ions |
| Typical chemistry | Radical chain substitution, combustion | Ionic substitution, elimination and addition mechanisms |

For A–B heterolysis, the electron pair normally follows the more electronegative atom. In
(CH₃)₃C–Br, heterolysis gives (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Br⁻; in aqueous solvent the ions are solvated and the
tertiary carbocation is relatively stable. Homolysis of the same bond would give
(CH₃)₃C• + Br•.

::: example Worked example 16.2 — the same bond, two scripts
**Problem.** tert-Butyl bromide reacts rapidly with aqueous NaOH. Which fission begins the
reaction, and which species attacks which?

**Solution.** Water is polar and Br is more electronegative than carbon, so
**heterolysis** is favoured: the C–Br pair moves to Br, producing the tertiary carbocation
(CH₃)₃C⁺ and Br⁻. The carbocation is the electron-pair acceptor (electrophile). Hydroxide, with
lone pairs, is the electron-pair donor (nucleophile) and attacks it. UV-driven homolysis to two
radicals tells a different story and is not the script under aqueous ionic conditions.
:::

### Carbocations, carbanions and free radicals

| Intermediate | Carbon electrons/geometry | Stability pattern | Usual reactivity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbocation, C⁺** | 6 valence electrons; usually planar sp² with empty p orbital | 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl; allyl/benzyl resonance strongly stabilizing | Electrophile; rearranges by hydride/alkyl shift if a better cation can form |
| **Free radical, C•** | 7 valence electrons; near-planar at carbon; one unpaired electron | Benzyl/allyl and 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl (context dependent) | Abstracts atoms, adds to π bonds, combines with another radical |
| **Carbanion, C⁻** | 8 valence electrons; lone pair, often pyramidal unless resonance flattens it | Methyl > 1° > 2° > 3° for alkyl substitution; resonance and −I/−R groups stabilize | Nucleophile and strong base |

Why are tertiary carbocations and radicals relatively stable? Neighbouring C–H/C–C σ bonds can
donate electron density into the empty or half-filled p orbital (**hyperconjugation**), and
alkyl groups exert a weak electron-releasing inductive effect. A positive charge or unpaired
electron is spread over a larger region. In a carbanion, alkyl donation pushes still more
electron density onto an already negative carbon and destabilizes it; electron-withdrawing and
resonance-accepting groups (carbonyl, nitrile, nitro) stabilize carbanions by dispersal.

### Electrophiles and nucleophiles

An **electrophile** (“electron-pair loving”, despite acting as an acceptor) is electron-poor:
H⁺, NO₂⁺, Br⁺ equivalents, BF₃, AlCl₃, carbocations and the δ+ carbon of a carbonyl or alkyl
halide. A **nucleophile** has an electron pair available: OH⁻, CN⁻, halide ions, NH₃, H₂O,
thiolates, carbanions and the π electrons of an alkene/arene. The curly arrow always starts at
the nucleophile's electron pair (or a bond) and points to the electrophilic atom/bond — never
the reverse.

**Basicity and nucleophilicity are not synonyms.** Basicity is an equilibrium affinity for a
proton; nucleophilicity is a rate of attack at an electrophilic atom, often carbon. Solvent,
size and polarizability matter. In protic solvents, larger and less strongly solvated I⁻ is
generally more nucleophilic than F⁻ even though F⁻ is more basic. A bulky base such as
tert-butoxide can remove a proton yet be a poor nucleophile at crowded carbon.

### Inductive effect: a σ-bond tug that fades with distance

If X is more electronegative than carbon in C–X, the bonding electrons sit closer to X. That
polarity is relayed weakly through adjacent σ bonds. Electron-withdrawing groups have a
**−I effect**: –NO₂, –CN, –COOH, carbonyl groups and halogens, with halogen order
F > Cl > Br > I. Electron-releasing alkyl groups have a modest **+I effect** relative to hydrogen,
increasing roughly tert-butyl > isopropyl > ethyl > methyl. The effect is permanent but becomes
small after about three bonds.

This explains carboxylic-acid strength. A −I substituent stabilizes the negatively charged
carboxylate ion and pulls the acid equilibrium toward ionization. One chlorine near the acid
increases strength, more chlorines increase it further, and moving chlorine farther away weakens
the effect. Conversely, +I alkyl groups destabilize a nearby carboxylate slightly and make a
carbocation slightly more stable.

### Resonance: delocalization through a conjugated π system

Resonance appears when π electrons or a lone pair can overlap through adjacent p orbitals:
π–π conjugation in butadiene/benzene, lone pair–π conjugation in phenoxide or an amide, and
π–empty-p overlap in an allyl cation. Only electrons move in resonance drawings; atomic nuclei
and σ frameworks stay fixed. Each drawing is a **contributing structure**, and the actual
molecule is a lower-energy hybrid with fractional bond orders. Equivalent contributors
contribute equally — acetate has two equivalent C–O bonds, benzene has six equal C–C bonds.

A group that donates π/lone-pair density into a conjugated system has a **+R (or +M) effect**:
–NH₂, –OH, –OR and halogens (although halogens are simultaneously −I). A group that withdraws by
π conjugation has a **−R effect**: –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COR and –CO₂H when conjugated with the
system. Resonance requires orbital alignment; twisting a π system out of coplanarity reduces it.

High-yield consequences are best read from the ion or molecule left after reaction:

- **Phenol is more acidic than ethanol** because phenoxide is resonance-stabilized by the ring,
  whereas ethoxide localizes negative charge on oxygen.
- **Aniline is a weaker base than an alkylamine** because nitrogen's lone pair is delocalized
  into the benzene ring and less available to bind H⁺.
- **Carboxylate is stabilized** by two equivalent resonance forms, one reason carboxylic acids
  are far more acidic than alcohols.
- **Amide nitrogen is weakly basic and the C–N bond has partial double-bond character** because
  the nitrogen lone pair conjugates with C=O; this is the electronic basis of the planar peptide
  bond.
- **Allyl and benzyl cations/radicals** disperse charge or spin over several carbons and form
  more readily than ordinary primary systems.

| Effect | Path | Distance behaviour | Needs π system? | Classic readout |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Inductive | Through σ bonds | Falls rapidly with each bond | No | Chloroacetic-acid strengths |
| Resonance | Through overlapping p orbitals | Can extend across the conjugated system | Yes (or adjacent p/lone pair) | Phenol acidity, aniline basicity |
| Hyperconjugation | Neighbouring σ(C–H/C–C) → p/π* | Immediate neighbour | Needs adjacent p/π system | Carbocation and alkene stability |

::: memory Read intermediates by charge comfort

or delocalizes (−I, −R). Radical? Donation and delocalization both help. That one question
unlocks most order-of-stability items.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Carbon's tetravalency, strong self-linking and multiple bonding create an enormous set of stable molecular architectures.
- Functional groups and homologous series convert that diversity into learnable families; alkyl, aryl and benzyl must be distinguished by where the free valence lies.
- Degree of unsaturation counts rings plus π bonds and is the fastest check before drawing or naming structures.
- IUPAC names are built by seniority, parent selection, low-locant numbering and alphabetical assembly.
- Structural isomers differ in connectivity; stereoisomers keep connectivity but differ in arrangement. E/Z, enantiomers, diastereomers, racemates and meso forms each answer a different question.
- Homolysis makes radicals, heterolysis makes ions. Curly arrows track electron pairs from nucleophile to electrophile.
- Alkyl donation and hyperconjugation stabilize carbocations/radicals but destabilize carbanions; resonance can dominate all three.
- Inductive effects fade through σ bonds, while resonance operates only where p orbitals overlap; acid/base and intermediate-strength orders usually reveal which effect controls the system.

### Model answer D1: electronic effects in organic molecules (10 marks)

**Introduction.** The movement of electrons in a molecule explains its reactivity, acidity, basicity and the stability of its intermediates.

**Electronic effects.**

1. **Inductive effect (I).** The permanent polarisation of a $\sigma$ bond by an electronegative atom, passing along the chain and falling off rapidly (negligible after three carbons). **$-I$ groups:** $-\text{NO}_2 > -\text{CN} > -\text{F} > -\text{Cl} > -\text{Br} > -\text{OH} > -\text{C}_6\text{H}_5$. **$+I$ groups:** alkyl groups, increasing in the order $\text{CH}_3 < \text{C}_2\text{H}_5 < (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH} < (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}$.
2. **Resonance (mesomeric) effect (M or R).** Delocalisation of $\pi$ electrons or lone pairs through a conjugated system. The actual structure is a **resonance hybrid** that is more stable than any contributing structure, and the stabilisation is the **resonance energy** (benzene $150\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$). $+M$ groups: $-\text{OH}$, $-\text{NH}_2$, $-\text{OR}$, halogens. $-M$ groups: $-\text{NO}_2$, $-\text{CHO}$, $-\text{CN}$, $-\text{COOH}$.
3. **Electromeric effect.** A temporary shift of $\pi$ electrons to one atom in the presence of an attacking reagent, which reverses when the reagent is removed.
4. **Hyperconjugation.** Delocalisation of the $\sigma$ electrons of a C-H bond next to a double bond, a carbocation or a free radical ("no-bond resonance"). The more the $\alpha$-hydrogens, the greater the stability, so $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{C}^+ > (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{CH}^+ > \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2^+ > \text{CH}_3^+$ (the order is also followed by free radicals).

**Applications.**

| Property | Order | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Acid strength | FCH₂COOH > ClCH₂COOH > BrCH₂COOH > CH₃COOH | $-I$ of halogen stabilises the anion |
| Acid strength | HCOOH > CH₃COOH | $+I$ of the methyl group destabilises the anion |
| Acid strength | picric acid > phenol > ethanol | resonance stabilisation of the anion |
| Basicity (gas phase) | $3^{\circ} > 2^{\circ} > 1^{\circ} > \text{NH}_3$ | $+I$ of alkyl groups |
| Carbanion stability | $\text{CH}_3^- > 1^{\circ} > 2^{\circ} > 3^{\circ}$ | alkyl groups destabilise |

The C-Cl bond in chlorobenzene is shorter and less reactive than in chloroethane because the lone pair of Cl is delocalised in the ring (partial double-bond character).

**Reactive intermediates.** Carbocations (sp², planar, electrophilic), carbanions (sp³, pyramidal), free radicals (sp², planar) and carbenes (:CH₂).

**Numerical (pKa).** Acetic acid $\text{p}K_a = 4.76$, chloroacetic $2.86$ and trichloroacetic $0.65$. So chloroacetic acid is stronger by $K_a$ ratio $10^{4.76 - 2.86} = 10^{1.9} \approx 80$ times.

**Conclusion.** The $-I$ and $-M$ groups increase acidity, and hyperconjugation with $+I$ effects explains the stability order of carbocations.

### Model answer D2: isomerism and stereochemistry (10 marks)

**Introduction.** **Isomers** have the same molecular formula but different structures or arrangements of atoms.

**Structural isomerism.** Chain (butane and isobutane), position (1-propanol and 2-propanol), functional group (ethanol and dimethyl ether, C₂H₆O), metamerism (diethyl ether and methyl propyl ether), tautomerism (keto-enol of acetone) and ring-chain isomerism.

**Stereoisomerism.**

1. **Geometrical (cis-trans, E-Z) isomerism.** Arises from restricted rotation about a C=C bond or in a ring, when each carbon of the double bond has two different groups. In the **CIP rules**, the group with higher atomic number gets higher priority. **Z** (zusammen) means the higher priority groups are on the same side and **E** (entgegen) on opposite sides. Trans isomers are usually more stable and have higher melting points; cis isomers have higher dipole moment and boiling points (maleic acid and fumaric acid).
2. **Optical isomerism.** Arises from **chirality**: a carbon attached to four different groups (a stereocentre) gives a molecule not superimposable on its mirror image. The pair of mirror images are **enantiomers**, which rotate plane-polarised light equally in opposite directions ($d$ or $+$, $l$ or $-$) and are otherwise identical in physical properties. A $50:50$ mixture is a **racemic mixture** and is optically inactive (external compensation).
3. **Diastereomers** are stereoisomers that are not mirror images and have different physical properties. A **meso** compound has stereocentres but an internal plane of symmetry, so it is inactive (internal compensation), as in meso-tartaric acid.

**R/S configuration.** Rank the four groups by CIP priority and view with the lowest priority away from you. A clockwise path from highest to lowest is **R** and anticlockwise is **S**.

| Number of stereocentres $n$ | Stereoisomers without symmetry | Enantiomer pairs |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 2 | 1 |
| 2 | 4 | 2 |
| 3 | 8 | 4 |

**Examples.** Lactic acid, CH₃CH(OH)COOH, has one stereocentre and $2$ enantiomers. Tartaric acid, HOOC-CH(OH)-CH(OH)-COOH, has $2$ stereocentres but only $3$ stereoisomers ($d$, $l$ and meso) because of symmetry. Biologically, enzymes recognise one enantiomer only, such as $\text{L}$-amino acids and $\text{D}$-glucose.

**Numerical.** The compound CH₃-CH(OH)-CH(Cl)-CH₃ has $n = 2$ different stereocentres, so it has $2^2 = 4$ stereoisomers. A compound with $n$ different stereocentres has $2^n$ stereoisomers. The specific rotation is $[\alpha] = \dfrac{\alpha_{obs}}{l\,c}$, so a reading of $+6.6^{\circ}$ in a $2\ \text{dm}$ tube with $c = 0.5\ \text{g mL}^{-1}$ gives $[\alpha] = \dfrac{6.6}{2\times0.5} = +6.6^{\circ}$.

**Conclusion.** Chirality needs four different groups and geometrical isomerism needs restricted rotation with two different groups on each carbon.
