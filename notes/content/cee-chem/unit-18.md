This is a **4-question** chapter built around one bond competition: carbon–halogen bonds
can be replaced by carbon–oxygen bonds, and the resulting alcohols, phenols and ethers then
show how structure controls acidity, hydrogen bonding and reactivity. Learn haloalkanes by
mechanism (SN1, SN2, elimination), chloroform and chlorobenzene by their special traps, and
oxygen compounds by asking whether oxygen carries H (alcohol/phenol) or bridges two carbons
(ether).

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- Monohaloalkanes are named as halo-substituted alkanes and classified 1°, 2° or 3° by the carbon bearing X. C₄H₉Cl has four structural isomers; 2-chlorobutane is chiral, so five stereoisomers exist in total.
- For the same R, nucleophilic substitution reactivity is generally RI > RBr > RCl >> RF because C–X lengthens/weakens and halide leaving-group ability improves down the group.
- **SN2:** one concerted backside-attack step, rate = k[RX][Nu], inversion of configuration, methyl/1° fastest, strong nucleophile and polar aprotic solvent favoured. **SN1:** ionization to a planar carbocation is rate-determining, rate = k[RX], 3°/allylic/benzylic fastest, racemization/rearrangement possible, polar protic solvent favoured.
- Haloalkane conversions: aqueous OH⁻ → alcohol; KCN → nitrile (chain +1 C); AgCN → isocyanide; NH₃ → amines; alkoxide → ether; alcoholic KOH → alkene; Mg/dry ether → Grignard reagent; reduction → alkane; Na/dry ether → Wurtz coupling.
- Chloroform is made by chlorinating/hydrolysing ethanol or propanone with bleaching powder. In air/light it oxidizes to poisonous **phosgene**, so it is stored dark with about 1% ethanol stabilizer. With a primary amine and alcoholic KOH it gives the foul carbylamine (isocyanide) test; with phenol/KOH it is the Reimer–Tiemann reagent.
- Chlorobenzene is made by Cl₂/FeCl₃ chlorination or from a diazonium salt (CuCl). Its C–Cl bond has partial double-bond character and the carbon is sp², so ordinary SN1/SN2 fails; Dow's process needs aqueous NaOH at about 623 K under pressure to reach phenol.
- Monohydric alcohols (one –OH, CₙH₂ₙ₊₂O for saturated acyclic members) are isomeric with ethers. Industrial routes: alkene hydration (Markovnikov), hydroboration–oxidation (anti-Markovnikov), the **oxo process** (alkene + CO + H₂ → aldehyde → primary alcohol, one extra carbon) and fermentation by yeast enzymes.
- Alcohol landmarks: Na → alkoxide + H₂; conc. H₂SO₄ with ethanol gives ethene at 443 K but ethoxyethane with excess ethanol at 413 K; oxidation 1° → aldehyde → acid, 2° → ketone, 3° resists mild oxidation. Lucas reagent (conc. HCl/ZnCl₂): 3° cloudy at once, 2° in minutes, 1° essentially clear at room temperature.
- Ethanol forms: **rectified spirit** is the 95.6% azeotrope; **absolute alcohol** is nearly water-free; **denatured/methylated spirit** is made undrinkable; **power alcohol** is an ethanol–petrol fuel blend.
- Phenol is made industrially mainly from **cumene** (co-product propanone), and in school preparations from chlorobenzene (Dow), benzenesulfonate fusion or a diazonium salt. It is acidic (pKₐ ≈ 10): dissolves in NaOH but does not effervesce with NaHCO₃.
- Phenol signatures: violet colour with neutral FeCl₃; bromine water → white 2,4,6-tribromophenol; Kolbe–Schmitt (CO₂/NaOH, then acid) → salicylic acid; Reimer–Tiemann (CHCl₃/KOH) → salicylaldehyde; diazonium coupling → orange azo dye.
- Williamson ether synthesis is SN2: an alkoxide/phenoxide attacks a preferably primary alkyl halide. Diethyl ether is volatile, forms explosive peroxides in air/light, forms oxonium salts with strong acids, and is cleaved by hot concentrated HI.

## 18.1 Monohaloalkanes: nomenclature, isomerism, preparation and properties; SN1 and SN2

A monohaloalkane replaces one H of an alkane by F, Cl, Br or I. The substitutive name uses a
halo- prefix: CH₃CH₂CHBrCH₃ is 2-bromobutane and (CH₃)₃CCl is 2-chloro-2-methylpropane.
If halogens differ, cite alphabetically. The class 1°, 2° or 3° is read from the carbon
carrying halogen, regardless of chain length. Two structural cousins deserve separate labels:
**allylic/benzylic halides** (X on a saturated carbon next to a π system) ionize unusually
easily, while **vinylic/aryl halides** (X directly on sp² carbon) resist normal substitution.

The C–X bond is polarized Cδ+–Xδ−, so carbon is electrophilic. Bond enthalpy and length pull
in opposite educational directions: C–F is shortest and strongest; C–I is longest and easiest
to break, and I⁻ is also a weak, stable base — an excellent leaving group. Haloalkanes cannot
donate hydrogen bonds and are mostly insoluble in water, despite being polar; boiling points
increase with size and polarizability.

### Preparation

- **From alkanes:** Cl₂ or Br₂ with UV/heat gives radical substitution, usually as a mixture.
- **From alkenes:** HX adds by Markovnikov orientation (HBr/peroxide reverses orientation);
  X₂ gives a vicinal dihalide rather than a monohaloalkane.
- **From alcohols:** HCl/ZnCl₂, HBr, red P/I₂, PCl₃/PCl₅ or SOCl₂ replace –OH by halogen.
  SOCl₂ is often clean for chlorides because SO₂ and HCl leave as gases.
- **Halogen exchange:** NaI in acetone converts RCl/RBr to RI (Finkelstein), driven by
  precipitation of NaCl/NaBr from acetone; AgF-type reagents can install F (Swarts).

### Substitution mechanisms: SN2 versus SN1

**SN2** is a single choreographed step. The nucleophile approaches carbon from the side
opposite the leaving group; the C–Nu bond forms as the C–X bond breaks through one
pentacoordinate transition state. Because attack occurs from behind, a stereogenic carbon
undergoes **Walden inversion**. Crowding defines the rate order
methyl > 1° > 2° >> 3°; even a primary neopentyl halide is slow because bulk one carbon away
blocks the backside. Polar aprotic solvents (acetone, DMF, DMSO) solvate the counter-cation
more than the anionic nucleophile and therefore often accelerate SN2.

**SN1** begins with slow heterolysis to a planar carbocation. The nucleophile can then attack
either face, so an optically active secondary/tertiary substrate tends toward
**racemization** (often with some excess inversion because the leaving group may still shield
one face as an ion pair). Carbocation stability gives the order
3° ≈ benzylic/allylic > 2° >> 1°/methyl, and hydride or alkyl shifts may rearrange the skeleton
before capture. Water/alcohols help by solvating both ions; the nucleophile need not appear
in the rate law because it arrives after the slow step.

| Evidence | SN1 | SN2 |
|---|---|---|
| Rate law | k[RX] | k[RX][Nu] |
| Substrate order | 3° > 2° >> 1°, methyl | Methyl > 1° > 2° >> 3° |
| Stereochemistry | Racemization (planar ion) | Inversion |
| Rearrangement | Common if a better cation exists | No carbocation, so no ionic rearrangement |
| Solvent that helps | Polar protic | Polar aprotic for anionic nucleophiles |
| Nucleophile strength | Less important to rate | Stronger nucleophile, faster reaction |

Substitution competes with **elimination**. Hot alcoholic KOH removes HX, usually to the
Saytzeff alkene. Heat, a strong/bulky base and a tertiary substrate push toward elimination;
a strong non-bulky nucleophile in a polar aprotic solvent pushes a primary substrate toward
SN2. Exam questions are often decided by spotting **aqueous vs alcoholic KOH**: aqueous
favours alcohol formation, alcoholic favours alkene formation.

Useful conversions from RX include RON with aqueous hydroxide; RCN with ethanolic KCN (the
one-carbon homologation used before hydrolysis to an acid in Ch. 19); RNC with AgCN because
the ambident cyanide attacks through N under covalent AgCN conditions; amines with excess NH₃
(over-alkylation is the practical problem); ethers with alkoxides; alkyl nitrites with KNO₂
but mainly nitroalkanes with AgNO₂ (the same ambident-ion logic); thiols/thioethers with
KSH/RSNa; alkanes by reduction; coupled alkanes by Wurtz reaction; and RMgX with Mg in dry
ether, the Grignard doorway to Ch. 20.

::: example Worked example 18.1 — read the mechanism from the carbon skeleton
**Problem.** Predict the pathway and product when (i) CH₃Br and (ii) (CH₃)₃CBr react with
aqueous KOH.

**Solution.** (i) Methyl bromide offers an unhindered backside and cannot form CH₃⁺ usefully,
so **SN2** gives methanol. (ii) tert-Butyl bromide cannot be attacked from behind, but it
ionizes to a tertiary carbocation; water/hydroxide capture gives tert-butanol by **SN1**
(alkene by elimination becomes important on heating with alcoholic base). The substrate, not
a memorized halide label, chooses the mechanism.
:::

## 18.2 Chloroform and chlorobenzene

### Chloroform, CHCl₃ (trichloromethane)

Industrial chloroform comes from methane chlorination. The laboratory preparation heats
ethanol or propanone with bleaching powder and water, then distils. Bleaching powder does
three jobs: it supplies chlorine, oxidizes ethanol to ethanal, chlorinates the methyl group
to a trichloromethyl carbonyl compound, and its Ca(OH)₂ cleaves that intermediate. From
ethanol the sequence passes through chloral, CCl₃CHO, which alkali splits into CHCl₃ and
formate; propanone similarly gives CHCl₃ and acetate. This is the same haloform logic as the
iodoform test in Ch. 19.

Chloroform is a dense, volatile, sweet-smelling, nonflammable liquid once used as an
anaesthetic but abandoned for liver/cardiac toxicity. Its most important storage reaction is
slow oxidation in air and light:

2CHCl₃ + O₂ → 2COCl₂ + 2HCl.

The product COCl₂ is **phosgene**, a war-gas-level pulmonary poison. Chloroform is therefore
kept in dark, nearly full bottles with about 1% ethanol: ethanol converts traces of phosgene
into comparatively harmless diethyl carbonate. On strong heating with concentrated HNO₃,
chloroform gives chloropicrin, CCl₃NO₂. Hot aqueous KOH hydrolyses it to potassium formate.
Zinc/HCl reduces it stepwise (first toward CH₂Cl₂), while silver powder couples two
molecules to ethyne (Ch. 17).

Alcoholic KOH removes HCl from CHCl₃ to make highly reactive **dichlorocarbene, :CCl₂**.
That intermediate powers two name reactions: the **carbylamine reaction**, in which a
primary amine with CHCl₃/alcoholic KOH forms an intensely foul isocyanide (a test for a
primary amine), and the **Reimer–Tiemann** formylation of phenol in §18.4.

### Chlorobenzene, C₆H₅Cl

Benzene with Cl₂/FeCl₃ in the dark gives chlorobenzene by electrophilic substitution.
Industrially, the Raschig–Hooker process passes benzene, HCl and air over a copper catalyst.
In the laboratory it is also made from benzene diazonium chloride with CuCl/HCl
(Sandmeyer reaction, Ch. 20).

Chlorobenzene looks like “CH₃Cl on a ring” only in a formula-counting sense. The C–Cl bond
is shorter/stronger because a chlorine lone pair conjugates with the ring (partial C=Cl
character), the carbon is sp², a phenyl cation would place positive charge in an orbital
that cannot be resonance-stabilized by the ring, and the ring blocks backside SN2 attack.
Hence aqueous KOH at room temperature does nothing useful. **Dow's process** forces
hydrolysis with aqueous NaOH near 623 K and about 300 atm, giving sodium phenoxide and then
phenol on acidification. Nitro groups ortho/para to Cl can change the story by stabilizing
an addition intermediate for nucleophilic aromatic substitution — a contrast worth one line
and no more at this level.

The ring still does electrophilic substitution, more slowly than benzene and under
ortho/para direction by Cl (Ch. 17). Sodium in dry ether couples two molecules to biphenyl
(**Fittig**); chlorobenzene plus an alkyl halide/Na gives an alkylbenzene (**Wurtz–Fittig**).
Reduction can return benzene. Condensation of two molecules of chlorobenzene with chloral
in concentrated H₂SO₄ gives **DDT**, historically a powerful insecticide and now a standard
example of persistence and bioaccumulation in environmental chemistry (Ch. 21).

## 18.3 Monohydric alcohols: preparation and properties; oxo process, hydroboration–oxidation, fermentation and types of ethanol

A **monohydric alcohol** has one hydroxyl group. It is primary, secondary or tertiary from the
carbon bearing –OH. The –OH group makes alcohols polar, hydrogen-bonding liquids with much
higher boiling points and greater water solubility than comparable hydrocarbons/ethers; both
effects fade as the hydrocarbon chain grows. Ethanol's complete miscibility with water is a
small-chain privilege, not a property of every alcohol.

### Preparation, with the regiochemistry made explicit

- **Acid-catalysed hydration of alkenes** gives Markovnikov alcohol. Ethene is hydrated
  industrially with steam over H₃PO₄ on a support at high temperature/pressure to ethanol.
- **Hydroboration–oxidation** (BH₃·THF or B₂H₆, then H₂O₂/OH⁻) adds H and OH across C=C in a
  syn, concerted manner. Boron attaches to the less hindered carbon and oxidation replaces
  B by OH with retention at that carbon, so the net result is **anti-Markovnikov alcohol**
  without carbocation rearrangement. Ethene gives ethanol; propene gives propan-1-ol.
- **Oxo process (hydroformylation):** an alkene + CO + H₂ over Co/Rh catalysis inserts a
  formyl carbon to make an aldehyde with one more carbon; hydrogenation then gives the
  primary alcohol. Ethene → propanal → propan-1-ol is the clean school example. Modern
  phosphine-modified rhodium catalysts favour the straight-chain aldehyde used for detergent
  alcohols and plasticizers.
- **From haloalkanes:** aqueous KOH/NaOH substitution, best for primary substrates; tertiary
  systems may eliminate.
- **By reduction:** aldehydes give primary alcohols and ketones secondary alcohols with
  H₂/catalyst, NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄; esters/acids need stronger reduction (Ch. 19). Grignard
  addition to carbonyl compounds builds more complex alcohols (Ch. 20).
- **From primary amines:** nitrous acid replaces –NH₂ by –OH with N₂ evolution, but
  carbocation rearrangement/alkene by-products make it a better test than preparation.

**Fermentation** is enzyme chemistry. Yeast invertase hydrolyses sucrose to glucose and
fructose; zymase then converts glucose anaerobically:

C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂.

Starch from grain/potato must first be malted/enzymatically saccharified to sugars. Rising
ethanol inhibits yeast, so naturally fermented liquor stops near 12–15% alcohol; stronger
spirit requires distillation. This route now also supplies fuel bioethanol, with a
food-versus-fuel and land-use debate that belongs in Ch. 21.

**Types of ethanol.** Distillation cannot pass the ethanol–water azeotrope at about
**95.6% ethanol**, called **rectified spirit**. Removing the last water (azeotropic
distillation, drying agents or molecular sieves) gives **absolute alcohol**.
**Denatured alcohol/methylated spirit** contains methanol and bitter/aversive additives so
industrial ethanol is not diverted for drinking; denaturing does not make it safe —
methanol metabolizes to formic acid and can blind or kill. **Power alcohol** is ethanol
blended with petrol as motor fuel; modern E10-type blends are its descendants.

### Chemical properties

Alcohols are very weak acids (ethanol pKₐ ≈ 16) and weak bases at oxygen. Sodium gives an
alkoxide and hydrogen more gently than with water: 2ROH + 2Na → 2RONa + H₂. Alkoxides are
strong bases and good nucleophiles (the Williamson reagents of §18.5).

**Substitution of –OH.** –OH is a poor leaving group until protonated or converted by a
reagent such as SOCl₂/PCl₅/PBr₃. With HX the alcohol order is 3° > 2° > 1° because the
mechanism leans SN1 under acidic aqueous conditions, and HX reactivity is HI > HBr > HCl.
**Lucas reagent** (concentrated HCl + anhydrous ZnCl₂) turns that order into a stopwatch:
tertiary alcohol clouds immediately as insoluble alkyl chloride forms, secondary in about
five minutes, primary remains clear at room temperature. Allylic/benzylic primary alcohols
can break the simple stopwatch by forming stabilized cations.

**Dehydration** with conc. H₂SO₄/Al₂O₃ gives alkenes, ease 3° > 2° > 1°, commonly Saytzeff.
Ethanol is the condition trap: excess ethanol with conc. H₂SO₄ at about **413 K** gives
ethoxyethane by intermolecular dehydration, whereas excess acid at about **443 K** gives
ethene by intramolecular dehydration.

**Oxidation** distinguishes the classes. A primary alcohol under controlled conditions
(PCC, or distillation away from oxidant) stops at the aldehyde; strong aqueous
K₂Cr₂O₇/KMnO₄ carries it to the carboxylic acid. A secondary alcohol gives a ketone. A
tertiary alcohol has no H on the carbinol carbon and resists mild oxidation, preferring
dehydration under hot acid. Passing vapours over heated Cu dehydrogenates 1° → aldehyde and
2° → ketone; a tertiary alcohol dehydrates to an alkene. Ethanol and other
CH₃–CH(OH)– alcohols give the **iodoform test** (yellow CHI₃) with I₂/NaOH because they are
oxidized to a methyl carbonyl compound in situ.

The older **Victor Meyer** distinction converts ROH to RI, then to RNO₂ with AgNO₂, and
treats with HNO₂/alkali: primary gives a red nitrolic-acid salt, secondary a blue
pseudonitrol, tertiary remains essentially colourless. It is longer than Lucas and now
largely of examination-historical interest.

## 18.4 Phenol: preparation and properties

Phenol is hydroxybenzene — not a typical alcohol. The –OH oxygen is attached to sp² carbon,
its lone pair conjugates with the ring, and the phenoxide ion is resonance-stabilized.
Those two facts make phenol much more acidic and its ring much more reactive to
electrophiles than ethanol/benzene comparisons might suggest.

**Preparation.** The dominant industrial route is the **cumene process**: benzene + propene
→ cumene (isopropylbenzene); air oxidation to cumene hydroperoxide; acid-catalysed cleavage
to phenol + propanone. School preparations remain valuable mechanisms in miniature:
chlorobenzene by Dow's process; sodium benzenesulfonate fused with NaOH and acidified; and
benzene diazonium chloride warmed with water, releasing N₂ to give phenol.

Phenol forms colourless, hygroscopic crystals (m.p. about 43 °C) that pinken on oxidation
in air. It is only moderately soluble in cold water, is corrosive to skin (the old name is
carbolic acid) and was Lister's original surgical antiseptic — now replaced by safer agents.

**Acidity.** Phenol (pKₐ ≈ 10.0) is about a million times stronger an acid than ethanol
because phenoxide delocalizes charge to ortho/para ring carbons and because the ring carbon
is sp². It reacts with Na to give H₂ and with NaOH to form soluble sodium phenoxide, but it
does **not** liberate CO₂ from NaHCO₃: carbonic acid (first pKₐ ≈ 6.4) is stronger, so CO₂
passed into phenoxide actually reprotonates it to phenol. Electron-withdrawing –NO₂ groups
increase phenol acidity (2,4,6-trinitrophenol is picric acid, pKₐ ≈ 0.4); electron-donating
methyl groups make cresols slightly weaker acids.

**Reactions at oxygen and at the ring.** Zinc dust distillation removes oxygen to benzene.
Ammonia under forcing catalytic conditions can replace –OH by –NH₂ (aniline). Phenol is
acylated by acetic anhydride/acyl chlorides to phenyl esters; benzoyl chloride in aqueous
NaOH gives phenyl benzoate by Schotten–Baumann conditions. Sodium phenoxide undergoes
**Kolbe–Schmitt carboxylation** with CO₂ under pressure/heat; acidification gives salicylic
acid, the precursor of aspirin. With CHCl₃/KOH, dichlorocarbene chemistry gives mainly
salicylaldehyde (**Reimer–Tiemann**). With phthalic anhydride/conc. H₂SO₄ it condenses to
**phenolphthalein**, colourless in acid and pink in alkali. Diazonium salts couple at the
para position in mild alkali to give orange azo dyes.

The strongly activating –OH group directs ortho/para. **Bromine water** is decolorized
instantly and white **2,4,6-tribromophenol** precipitates — even with dilute phenol —
because water partly converts phenol to the still more reactive phenoxide. Dilute HNO₃ gives
o-/p-nitrophenols; concentrated acid pushes toward picric acid. Sulfonation is reversible
and condition-dependent. Neutral FeCl₃ gives an intense violet/purple coloration, a standard
phenol test; Libermann's nitroso test (NaNO₂/conc. H₂SO₄, then dilution/alkali) gives the
classic blue-green to red colour play.

::: caution Phenol is not separated from a carboxylic acid by “both dissolve in bicarbonate”
Use NaHCO₃ deliberately: a carboxylic acid effervesces and dissolves as its salt; phenol
does not. NaOH dissolves phenol as phenoxide. That two-reagent ladder — NaHCO₃, then NaOH —
is an entire qualitative-analysis answer.
:::

## 18.5 Ethers: Williamson synthesis and properties of diethyl ether

An ether has C–O–C connectivity. Symmetrical ethers have the same group on both sides;
unsymmetrical ethers differ and show metamerism with other ethers/alcohols of the same
formula. In substitutive IUPAC names ethoxyethane (not the casual “diethyl ether” alone)
makes the smaller alkoxy side explicit; CH₃OC₆H₅ is methoxybenzene (anisole).

**Williamson synthesis** treats an alkoxide or phenoxide with an alkyl halide. Mechanistically
it is SN2, so the halide partner should be methyl or primary — and never an aryl halide.
The disconnection is chosen accordingly: to make tert-butyl ethyl ether, use sodium
tert-butoxide + ethyl iodide, not sodium ethoxide + tert-butyl iodide (which eliminates).
Phenoxide + CH₃I gives anisole, whereas CH₃O⁻ + chlorobenzene fails because the aryl C–Cl
bond will not undergo backside attack.

Diethyl ether is prepared commercially by continuous dehydration of ethanol with conc.
H₂SO₄ near 413 K (excess ethanol, ether distilled out) and by Williamson from sodium
ethoxide + ethyl bromide. It is a very volatile liquid (b.p. 34.6 °C), only slightly soluble
in water, an excellent solvent for organics and Grignard reagents, and a former anaesthetic.
Having no O–H bond, it cannot donate hydrogen bonds to itself; hence its boiling point is
far below its functional isomer butan-1-ol (b.p. 117 °C).

Ethers are relatively inert — that is why they are solvents — but oxygen is a Lewis base.
Cold concentrated H₂SO₄ or HCl forms soluble oxonium salts. Concentrated HBr and especially
HI cleave C–O: with two identical ethyl groups, one equivalent of hot conc. HI gives ethanol
+ ethyl iodide first, and excess/hotter HI converts the ethanol too, netting two ethyl
iodide molecules (water takes the oxygen). PCl₅ can convert diethyl ether to ethyl chloride.
Chlorine in the dark substitutes α-hydrogens to chlorinated ethers.

The safety reaction is slow **autoxidation in air/light** at the α-C–H bond to hydroperoxides
and polymeric peroxides. They concentrate in the still pot and may explode on heating or
friction. Store ether dark, cool and tightly closed; test old stock for peroxide and never
distil an ether to dryness. The same warning returns in laboratory-safety questions because it
is a real accident mechanism, not a textbook ornament.

Five swaps the paper sets: **aqueous KOH substitutes (alcohol), alcoholic
KOH eliminates (alkene)** — same halide, different destiny; **SN1

::: example Choosing the Williamson ether-synthesis pair for tert-butyl methyl ether
**Problem.** A student attempts to prepare *tert*-butyl methyl ether by heating *tert*-butyl bromide with sodium methoxide ($\text{CH}_3\text{ONa}$). What major product actually forms, and what reagent pair must be used instead?

## Chapter summary

- Monohaloalkanes react through a mechanistic fork: unhindered substrates invite concerted SN2 backside attack, while cation-stabilizing substrates ionize in SN1; bases and heat open the elimination side-road.
- The leaving-group order RI > RBr > RCl >> RF and the aqueous/alcoholic KOH distinction settle a large share of conversion questions.
- Chloroform's examination identity is haloform preparation, phosgene formation in air and dichlorocarbene reactions; chlorobenzene's is a strong aryl C–Cl bond that refuses ordinary substitution until Dow conditions.
- Alcohols combine hydrogen bonding with four reaction exits — alkoxide formation, substitution, dehydration and oxidation — whose class orders identify 1°, 2° and 3° alcohols.
- Hydroboration–oxidation and hydration are regiochemical opposites, the oxo process adds a carbon before reduction, and fermentation is enzyme-limited ethanol production.
- Phenol's acidity and ring activation both flow from conjugation with the ring: phenoxide is stabilized, NaHCO₃ is not decomposed, and bromine water gives instant tribromination.
- Williamson synthesis succeeds when the SN2 partner is chosen as the less hindered halide; diethyl ether is a useful inert solvent whose peroxide formation is its defining hazard.

### Model answer D1: nucleophilic substitution and elimination (10 marks)

**Introduction.** In a haloalkane R-X the carbon-halogen bond is polar, so carbon is electrophilic. A **nucleophile** (OH⁻, CN⁻, NH₃) replaces the halide, and with a strong base elimination competes.

**SN2 mechanism.** A **one-step**, bimolecular process in which the nucleophile attacks from the back side, as the leaving group departs, through a single transition state. Rate $= k[\text{RX}][\text{Nu}^-]$ (second order). **Inversion of configuration** (Walden inversion) occurs.

**SN1 mechanism.** A **two-step** process: slow ionisation to a planar carbocation, then fast attack by the nucleophile. Rate $= k[\text{RX}]$ (first order). Optically active compounds give **racemisation** (and rearrangements are possible).

| Feature | SN1 | SN2 |
|---|---|---|
| Rate law | $k[\text{RX}]$ | $k[\text{RX}][\text{Nu}]$ |
| Steps | two, via carbocation | one |
| Favoured substrate | $3^{\circ} > 2^{\circ}$, allyl, benzyl | $\text{CH}_3 > 1^{\circ} > 2^{\circ}$ |
| Nucleophile | weak, any | strong, high concentration |
| Solvent | polar protic (water, alcohol) | polar aprotic (DMSO, acetone) |
| Stereochemistry | racemisation | inversion |
| Leaving group | I > Br > Cl > F | I > Br > Cl > F |

**Elimination.** In **E2** a strong base removes the $\beta$-hydrogen in one step as the leaving group departs (anti-periplanar arrangement) and the rate is second order. In **E1** the carbocation formed in the slow step loses a proton. **Saytzeff's rule:** the more substituted (more stable) alkene is the major product, for example 2-bromobutane with alcoholic KOH gives mainly 2-butene.

**Substitution or elimination?** Alcoholic KOH, a strong bulky base, high temperature and $3^{\circ}$ halides favour elimination. Aqueous KOH, a good nucleophile with a weak base, and low temperature favour substitution.

**Special reactions.** Wurtz reaction ($2\text{RX} + 2\text{Na} \to \text{R-R}$), Grignard reagent formation ($\text{RX} + \text{Mg} \to \text{RMgX}$ in dry ether), Finkelstein reaction ($\text{RCl} + \text{NaI} \to \text{RI}$ in acetone), Swarts reaction (formation of RF with AgF) and the Sandmeyer reaction. Aryl halides do not undergo SN1 or SN2 easily because of the partial double-bond character of the C-X bond, but they react by nucleophilic aromatic substitution when there is a nitro group ortho or para to the halogen.

**Numerical.** In SN2 reaction of CH₃Br with OH⁻, doubling both $[\text{CH}_3\text{Br}]$ and $[\text{OH}^-]$ makes the rate $2\times2 = 4$ times larger. For SN1 of $(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}$, doubling $[\text{OH}^-]$ has no effect and doubling the substrate doubles the rate.

**Conclusion.** Primary halides prefer SN2, tertiary halides prefer SN1 or E1, and a strong bulky base favours E2.

### Model answer D2: alcohols, phenols and ethers (10 marks)

**Introduction.** Alcohols (R-OH), phenols (Ar-OH) and ethers (R-O-R') are the hydroxy and alkoxy derivatives of hydrocarbons.

**Alcohols.**

1. **Preparation:** hydration of alkenes, reduction of aldehydes, ketones and esters ($\text{LiAlH}_4$, $\text{NaBH}_4$), Grignard reagent with carbonyls, and fermentation of sugar for ethanol.
2. **Hydrogen bonding** gives alcohols a much higher boiling point than ethers of similar mass and a high solubility in water.
3. **Classification and tests.** In the **Lucas test** (ZnCl₂ in conc. HCl), $3^{\circ}$ alcohols turn cloudy at once, $2^{\circ}$ in about five minutes, and $1^{\circ}$ only on heating, so the order of reactivity is $3^{\circ} > 2^{\circ} > 1^{\circ}$ (carbocation stability).
4. **Oxidation:** $1^{\circ}$ gives an aldehyde (PCC) then acid (KMnO₄ or K₂Cr₂O₇), $2^{\circ}$ gives a ketone, and $3^{\circ}$ resists oxidation.
5. **Dehydration:** at $443\ \text{K}$ with conc. H₂SO₄ gives an alkene and at $413\ \text{K}$ an ether.

**Phenol.**

- **Acidity.** Phenol ($\text{p}K_a = 10$) is more acidic than alcohols ($16$ to $18$) because the phenoxide ion is stabilised by resonance, but it is weaker than carbonic acid, so it does not dissolve in NaHCO₃. Electron-withdrawing groups raise the acidity: $\text{p}K_a$ of $2,4,6$-trinitrophenol (picric acid) is $0.4$.
- **Tests:** violet colour with neutral FeCl₃; white precipitate of $2,4,6$-tribromophenol with bromine water.
- **Reactions:** Kolbe's reaction (sodium phenoxide with CO₂ gives salicylic acid, the precursor of aspirin), Reimer-Tiemann reaction (CHCl₃ and NaOH give salicylaldehyde) and EAS in the ortho and para positions.

**Ethers.** Prepared by **Williamson's synthesis** ($\text{RONa} + \text{R'X} \to \text{ROR'}$, SN2 and so best with a primary halide). They are inert, volatile and flammable, and form explosive peroxides on standing. Cleavage by HI: $\text{ROR'} + \text{HI} \to \text{ROH} + \text{R'I}$.

| Property | Ethanol | Phenol | Diethyl ether |
|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling point | $78^{\circ}\text{C}$ | $182^{\circ}\text{C}$ | $35^{\circ}\text{C}$ |
| Hydrogen bonding | yes | yes | no |
| Reaction with Na | yes, H₂ | yes, H₂ | no |
| Reaction with NaOH | no | yes | no |
