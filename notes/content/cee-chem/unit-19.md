The carbonyl group is organic chemistry's busiest crossroads, and this chapter is worth
**about 4 questions**. One polarized C=O explains why aldehydes and ketones accept
nucleophiles, why only some carbonyl compounds reduce Tollens' reagent, why α-hydrogens open
the aldol pathway, and why replacing –OH of a carboxylic acid by Cl, OCOR, OR or NH₂ creates
a ladder of derivative reactivity. Keep asking: **is the carbonyl carbon being attacked, is
the α-carbon forming an enolate, or is a group leaving from an acyl carbon?**

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- Aldehydes (R–CHO, suffix **-al**, carbonyl carbon is C-1) and ketones (R–CO–R′, suffix **-one**) share CₙH₂ₙO for saturated acyclic members and are functional isomers. The carbonyl carbon is sp², planar and electrophilic (Cδ+).
- Prepare aldehydes by controlled oxidation of 1° alcohols, Rosenmund reduction of RCOCl (H₂/Pd–BaSO₄, poisoned), Stephen reduction of nitriles (SnCl₂/HCl then hydrolysis) and ozonolysis. Prepare ketones by oxidation of 2° alcohols, alkyne hydration, Friedel–Crafts acylation (aryl ketones), RCOCl + organocadmium/Gilman reagent, nitrile + Grignard then hydrolysis, and dry distillation of calcium carboxylates.
- Nucleophilic additions: H₂ or hydride → alcohol; HCN → cyanohydrin; NaHSO₃ → crystalline bisulfite adducts (aldehydes and most methyl ketones); Grignard → alcohol after workup; ammonia derivatives (hydroxylamine, hydrazine, phenylhydrazine, 2,4-DNP) → crystalline C=N derivatives with loss of water.
- Aldehydes are more reactive than ketones (less steric crowding, one rather than two electron-donating alkyl groups). Tollens gives a silver mirror; Fehling gives red Cu₂O with most aliphatic aldehydes but not benzaldehyde; ketones resist these mild oxidants. 2,4-DNP detects aldehydes **and** ketones. Schiff's reagent is restored to magenta by aldehydes.
- Carbonyl compounds with α-H undergo base-catalysed **aldol addition** (then dehydration on heating). Aldehydes with **no α-H** undergo **Cannizzaro** disproportionation in concentrated alkali. Methyl ketones (and CH₃CH(OH)– alcohols/ethanal) give yellow iodoform with I₂/NaOH.
- Clemmensen (Zn–Hg/conc. HCl, acidic) and Wolff–Kishner (NH₂NH₂/KOH, heat, basic) both reduce C=O all the way to CH₂; choose by acid/base sensitivity. PCl₅ converts C=O to a gem-dichloride.
- Benzaldehyde: Etard oxidation of toluene or Gattermann–Koch formylation; Tollens positive, Fehling negative, no α-H; Cannizzaro, benzoin condensation (KCN/EtOH), Perkin condensation to cinnamic acid and easy air oxidation to benzoic acid.
- Carboxylic acids are prepared by vigorous oxidation of 1° alcohols/aldehydes, hydrolysis of nitriles/esters/amides, Grignard + CO₂, haloform oxidation of methyl ketones, and side-chain oxidation of alkylbenzenes to benzoic acid.
- Carboxylate resonance makes RCO₂H acidic (typical pKₐ 4–5) and gives CO₂ with NaHCO₃. –I groups strengthen the acid; alkyl donation weakens it slightly (HCOOH > CH₃COOH). Formic acid is also a reducing agent and is dehydrated by conc. H₂SO₄ to CO.
- Acid reactions: salts with bases/carbonates, Fischer esterification, SOCl₂ to acid chloride, LiAlH₄ to 1° alcohol, soda-lime decarboxylation, Kolbe electrolysis, and **HVZ** α-bromination with Br₂/red P (α-H required).
- Nucleophilic acyl substitution reactivity: **acid chloride > anhydride > ester > amide**. Poorer resonance donation into C=O and a weaker-base leaving group mean faster reaction. All ultimately hydrolyse to the carboxylic acid, amides most reluctantly.
- Derivative signatures: acid chloride as the universal acylating gateway; anhydride acylation (aspirin); ester alkaline hydrolysis = saponification; ester α-H + strong base = Claisen condensation; amide dehydration → nitrile, LiAlH₄ → amine with the same carbon count, **Hofmann bromamide** (Br₂/NaOH) → primary amine with one carbon fewer.
:::

## 19.1 Aliphatic aldehydes and ketones: nomenclature, preparation and reactions; benzaldehyde

### The carbonyl group and names

In C=O, carbon and oxygen are sp² hybridized. The σ framework is planar with angles near
120°, while the π bond sits above and below that plane. Oxygen's electronegativity makes the
carbon electron-poor. A nucleophile therefore attacks carbon, the π electrons move to oxygen,
and the resulting tetrahedral alkoxide is protonated — the standard two-step script of
carbonyl addition.

An aldehyde has at least one H on the carbonyl carbon and is terminal in a chain:
CH₃CH₂CHO is propanal; HCHO is methanal (formaldehyde). A ketone has two carbon groups on
C=O: CH₃COCH₂CH₃ is butan-2-one. Number a ketone to give C=O the lowest locant; in an
aldehyde the –CHO carbon is automatically C-1 and needs no locant. Lower aldehydes/ketones
are water-soluble carbonyl acceptors, but they cannot donate O–H hydrogen bonds, so their
boiling points lie between ethers and alcohols of comparable mass.

### Preparation map

| Target | Reliable preparations | Control point |
|---|---|---|
| Aldehyde from 1° alcohol | PCC or another controlled oxidant; Cu-catalysed dehydrogenation | Stop before RCO₂H; vigorous aqueous dichromate overshoots |
| Aldehyde from acyl chloride | **Rosenmund:** H₂, Pd/BaSO₄ poisoned with sulfur/quinoline | Poisoned catalyst must not reduce RCHO to RCH₂OH |
| Aldehyde from nitrile | **Stephen:** SnCl₂/HCl, then hydrolysis of the iminium salt | Formal one-carbon retention from RCN |
| Aldehyde/ketone from alkene | Reductive ozonolysis | Each alkene carbon becomes a carbonyl carbon |
| Ketone from 2° alcohol | K₂Cr₂O₇/H⁺, PCC, Cu dehydrogenation | No aldehydic H, so no easy further oxidation |
| Ketone from alkyne | HgSO₄/H₂SO₄ hydration | Terminal alkyne gives a methyl ketone; ethyne gives ethanal |
| Aryl ketone | Friedel–Crafts acylation: arene + RCOCl/AlCl₃ | Installed acyl group deactivates the ring to a second acylation |
| Ketone from acyl chloride | R₂Cd or R₂CuLi | These softer organometallics stop where RMgX would add twice |
| Ketone from nitrile | RMgX, then acidic hydrolysis | The imine salt resists a second addition until workup |
| Simple symmetrical ketone | Dry-distil a calcium carboxylate: Ca(CH₃CO₂)₂ → CH₃COCH₃ + CaCO₃ | Mixed salts give mixed ketone products |

### Addition reactions and carbonyl derivatives

Reduction with H₂/Ni, NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ converts aldehydes to primary alcohols and ketones to
secondary alcohols. Hydrogen cyanide (generated/used with great care because of toxicity)
adds under base catalysis to a **cyanohydrin**, lengthening the carbon skeleton and opening
routes to α-hydroxy acids. Concentrated aqueous NaHSO₃ gives crystalline α-hydroxy
sulfonates with aldehydes and many methyl ketones; acid or alkali regenerates the carbonyl
compound, so the adduct is both a test and a purification handle. Bulky diaryl or hindered
ketones may fail for steric/equilibrium reasons.

Ammonia derivatives of the form H₂N–Z condense with C=O and lose water:

| Reagent H₂N–Z | Product name |
|---|---|
| Hydroxylamine (Z = OH) | Oxime |
| Hydrazine (Z = NH₂) | Hydrazone |
| Phenylhydrazine (Z = NHC₆H₅) | Phenylhydrazone |
| 2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine | 2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazone — yellow/orange/red crystals |
| Semicarbazide | Semicarbazone |

The sharp melting points of these solids historically identified liquid carbonyl compounds.
**2,4-DNP (Brady's reagent)** therefore proves “a carbonyl”, not “an aldehyde”. Formaldehyde
is anomalous with ammonia itself, condensing to cage-like hexamethylenetetramine
(urotropine); with phenol under acid/base catalysis it polymerizes to Bakelite-type
phenol–formaldehyde resin. Formalin is an approximately 37–40% aqueous formaldehyde
solution, used as a disinfectant and biological preservative.

### Oxidation tests that separate aldehydes from ketones

Tollens' reagent — ammoniacal silver nitrate, [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺ — oxidizes an aldehyde to a
carboxylate while Ag(I) is reduced to a **silver mirror**. Fehling's solution keeps Cu²⁺ in
alkaline tartrate solution; many aliphatic aldehydes reduce it on warming to brick-red
Cu₂O. Ketones lack an aldehydic C–H and would require C–C cleavage to oxidize, so they resist
these mild reagents. α-Hydroxy ketones and reducing sugars can be exceptions in biochemical
settings because base-catalysed isomerization creates oxidizable forms — do not transfer the
school rule blindly to carbohydrates.

Formic acid also reduces Tollens/Fehling reagents because its structure, H–CO₂H, contains an
aldehyde-like H on the carbonyl carbon. Schiff's reagent (decolourized magenta) regains a
pink-violet colour with aldehydes. With PCl₅, carbonyl oxygen is replaced by two Cl atoms;
with strong oxidants, ketones cleave to carboxylic acids of fewer carbons.

### Reactions at the α-carbon: aldol, Cannizzaro and haloform

Hydrogens on the carbon adjacent to C=O are weakly acidic because removal gives a resonance-
stabilized **enolate**. In dilute NaOH/KOH, the enolate of one molecule attacks the carbonyl
carbon of another: **aldol addition**. Ethanal gives 3-hydroxybutanal
(CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₂–CHO); heating dehydrates it to but-2-enal (crotonaldehyde), an α,β-unsaturated
aldehyde stabilized by conjugation. Ketones react more sluggishly, and crossed aldols are
cleanest when one partner has no α-H and acts only as the electrophile.

If an aldehyde has **no α-hydrogen**, concentrated KOH cannot divert it into enolate chemistry,
so one molecule transfers hydride to another: **Cannizzaro reaction**. Formaldehyde gives
methanol + potassium formate; benzaldehyde gives benzyl alcohol + potassium benzoate. It is
a disproportionation — one molecule oxidized, one reduced.

A methyl group directly attached to C=O is exhaustively halogenated by I₂/NaOH, then cleaved:
CH₃COCH₃ + 3I₂ + 4NaOH → CHI₃↓ + CH₃CO₂Na + 3NaI + 3H₂O. Yellow **iodoform**
(m.p. about 119 °C, antiseptic smell) signals a CH₃CO– group or a CH₃CH(OH)– group that the
reagent oxidizes to one; ethanal is the only aldehyde that responds.

Finally, deoxygenation C=O → CH₂ is achieved under either acidic **Clemmensen** conditions
(Zn–Hg/conc. HCl) or basic **Wolff–Kishner** conditions (first form the hydrazone, then KOH
in a high-boiling solvent with heat). The products match; compatibility with the rest of the
molecule makes the choice.

::: example Worked example 19.1 — three test tubes, one structure
**Problem.** A compound C₃H₆O gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNP and a silver mirror
with Tollens' reagent, but no yellow precipitate with I₂/NaOH. Identify it.

**Solution.** DU = 1 and 2,4-DNP confirms an aldehyde or ketone. Tollens selects **aldehyde**.
For C₃ the aldehyde is propanal, CH₃CH₂CHO. The rival propanone would fail Tollens and pass
iodoform. Each test is allowed to veto one candidate — that is qualitative analysis done
efficiently.
:::

### Benzaldehyde, C₆H₅CHO

Benzaldehyde is the bitter-almond oil aldehyde. It is prepared by **Etard oxidation** of
toluene with chromyl chloride (CrO₂Cl₂) followed by hydrolysis; by side-chain chlorination of
toluene to benzal chloride and hydrolysis; by Rosenmund reduction of benzoyl chloride; and
by **Gattermann–Koch** formylation of benzene with CO/HCl over AlCl₃/CuCl.

Conjugation with the ring makes benzaldehyde less reactive toward nucleophiles than ethanal.
It restores Schiff's colour and reduces Tollens' reagent but **does not reduce Fehling's
solution**. Exposed to air it autoxidizes to benzoic acid. It has no α-H on the side chain,
so concentrated KOH gives Cannizzaro products. With ethanolic KCN, two molecules undergo
**benzoin condensation**: cyanide temporarily converts one aldehyde carbon into a nucleophilic
equivalent, yielding benzoin, C₆H₅–CH(OH)–CO–C₆H₅. With acetic anhydride/sodium acetate it gives
cinnamic acid (**Perkin reaction**); with acetone/dilute alkali it gives crossed-aldol
(Claisen–Schmidt) enones. Ring substitution is slowed and meta-directed by –CHO.

::: example Iodoform from acetone, by the mole
**Problem.** The haloform cleavage of propanone runs CH₃COCH₃ + 3 I₂ +
4 NaOH → CHI₃ + CH₃COONa + 3 NaI + 3 H₂O. From 0.2 mol of propanone with
excess reagents, how many moles of iodine are consumed and what mass of
iodoform falls? (M of CHI₃ = 394 g/mol.)
**Solution.** The equation taxes **3 mol I₂ per ketone**: 0.2 mol ketone
consumes **0.6 mol I₂** and yields **0.2 mol CHI₃**, whose mass is
0.2 × 394 = **78.8 g** of yellow precipitate — the balance confirms
itself: 6 iodine atoms in, 3 in CHI₃ and 3 in NaI; sodium 4 in, 1 in the
acetate and 3 in the iodide.
:::
## 19.2 Monocarboxylic acids: preparation and properties

A carboxylic acid contains –CO₂H: a carbonyl and a hydroxyl on the same carbon. That carbon
is C-1 in the suffix name (ethanoic acid), while common names persist for formic (methanoic),
acetic (ethanoic), propionic and benzoic acids. Carboxylic acids dimerize through two
hydrogen bonds in the vapour and in nonpolar solvents, raising boiling points; solubility in
water is high for the first few members and then falls as the hydrocarbon part grows.

### Preparation

- **Oxidation:** a primary alcohol or aldehyde with hot acidified K₂Cr₂O₇/KMnO₄ gives the acid.
- **Nitrile hydrolysis:** RX →(KCN) RCN →(H₃O⁺ or OH⁻, heat) RCO₂H; the product has one carbon
  more than RX, a favourite chain-extension sequence.
- **Grignard carboxylation:** RMgX + CO₂, then acid workup (also one carbon more).
- **Derivative hydrolysis:** esters, amides, anhydrides and acid chlorides all return the acid.
- **Haloform reaction:** a methyl ketone gives the carboxylate with one carbon fewer.
- **Alkylbenzene oxidation:** hot alkaline KMnO₄ oxidizes any side chain bearing a benzylic H
  all the way to benzoic acid; ethylbenzene and propylbenzene converge on the same product.
  tert-Butylbenzene, with no benzylic H, resists.

### Acidity and reactions

Loss of H⁺ gives a carboxylate ion in which the negative charge is shared equally by two
oxygens; both C–O bonds become equivalent. Electron-withdrawing substituents stabilize that
anion and lower pKₐ (Ch. 16's chloroacetic acids), whereas electron-donating alkyl groups
raise pKₐ modestly. Formic acid (pKₐ 3.75) is therefore stronger than acetic acid (4.76).
Benzoic acid (pKₐ 4.20) is strengthened by the sp² ring carbon; ring substituents then tune it
by the usual –I/+R logic, with ortho effects able to override simple predictions.

Sodium bicarbonate gives brisk CO₂ effervescence — the practical acid test that phenol
fails. Metals and bases give salts. With an alcohol and acid catalyst, **Fischer
esterification** reaches equilibrium; removing water or using excess alcohol drags it forward.
SOCl₂ or PCl₅ converts –OH into the far more reactive acid chloride (§19.3). LiAlH₄ (or
borane) reduces the acid to a primary alcohol; NaBH₄ is generally too mild. Soda lime
decarboxylates the sodium salt to an alkane with one fewer carbon, and Kolbe electrolysis of
a concentrated carboxylate couples two alkyl fragments with loss of CO₂.

The **Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky (HVZ)** reaction brominates the α-carbon with Br₂/red phosphorus.
A trace of acyl bromide forms first and enolizes far more readily than the acid, so
substitution lands specifically at α-C; an α-H is mandatory. α-Bromo acids are gateways to
α-hydroxy and α-amino acids.

**Methanoic (formic) acid** is the family rebel. Because H–CO₂H still carries a formyl
hydrogen, it reduces Tollens' and Fehling's reagents, turns HgCl₂ first white (Hg₂Cl₂) then
grey (Hg), and decolourizes acidified KMnO₄, itself becoming CO₂. Concentrated H₂SO₄
dehydrates it neatly to CO + H₂O — the standard laboratory preparation of carbon monoxide in
Ch. 13. Benzoic acid's ring, deactivated and meta-directed by –CO₂H, undergoes nitration and
related substitutions mainly at the meta position.

## 19.3 Acid halides, anhydrides, amides and esters: preparation, properties and relative reactivity

Replace the –OH of RCO₂H and a family appears: **RCOCl** (acid/acyl chloride),
**(RCO)₂O** (anhydride), **RCO₂R′** (ester) and **RCONH₂** (amide; N-substituted amides
replace one or both N-hydrogens). A nucleophile still attacks the acyl carbon, but now the
tetrahedral intermediate can expel a leaving group and restore C=O: **nucleophilic acyl
substitution**. The observed order,

acid chloride > anhydride > ester > amide,

follows two reinforcing effects. The substituent donates its lone pair into C=O most strongly
for nitrogen and least effectively for chlorine (poor 3p–2p overlap), so an amide carbonyl is
least electrophilic. The leaving groups also become more basic across the series
(Cl⁻, RCO₂⁻, RO⁻, NH₂⁻), and stronger bases leave more reluctantly. The pKₐ of each leaving
group's conjugate acid compresses the point into one scale.

### Preparation from the acid (and from one another)

- **Acid chloride:** RCO₂H + SOCl₂ (cleanest; SO₂/HCl gases escape), PCl₅ or PCl₃.
- **Anhydride:** heat the acid with P₂O₅, or react an acid chloride with a sodium carboxylate;
  suitable dicarboxylic acids cyclize on heating (Ch. 21's phthalic anhydride).
- **Ester:** Fischer esterification, or alcohol + acid chloride/anhydride when speed and yield
  matter. Diazomethane methylates acids to methyl esters almost quantitatively.
- **Amide:** acid chloride/anhydride/ester + NH₃ or an amine; heating an ammonium carboxylate;
  or carefully stopping nitrile hydrolysis at the amide stage.

Movement down the reactivity ladder is easy (an acid chloride will make the other three);
movement up requires hydrolysis to the acid and reconversion, because an amide will not
directly become an acid chloride under examination conditions.

### Properties and signature reactions

**Hydrolysis.** Acid chlorides fume and react violently with water to the acid + HCl.
Anhydrides hydrolyse on warming to two acid molecules. Acid-catalysed ester hydrolysis is
the reverse of Fischer esterification, whereas NaOH hydrolysis (**saponification**) is
driven to completion because the acid product is immediately trapped as carboxylate — the
chemistry by which fats become soap plus glycerol. Amides need prolonged strong acid/alkali
and heat; enzymes called proteases perform the same cleavage gently on peptide bonds.

**Acid chlorides** are acylation couriers: alcohols/phenols give esters, ammonia/amines give
amides (Schotten–Baumann conditions in aqueous NaOH for benzoylation), arenes give ketones by
Friedel–Crafts, Rosenmund reduction gives aldehydes, organocadmium/Gilman reagents stop at
ketones, and LiAlH₄ reduces to the primary alcohol.

**Anhydrides** acylate similarly but waste one equivalent as carboxylic acid. Acetic anhydride
converts salicylic acid to aspirin — perhaps the most medically important reaction in this
section for a CEE candidate. Cyclic anhydrides open with alcohols/amines to half-esters and
amic acids.

**Esters** are volatile, often fruity-smelling liquids. Besides hydrolysis, they undergo
**transesterification** (exchange of the –OR group with another alcohol), reduction by
LiAlH₄ to two alcohols (the acyl side becomes a primary alcohol), reaction with two
equivalents of a Grignard reagent to a tertiary alcohol after workup, and **Claisen
condensation**: an ester with α-H plus a strong base such as sodium ethoxide gives a
β-ketoester (ethyl acetate → ethyl acetoacetate). The base must match the ester's alkoxy
group to avoid transesterification scrambling — a detail examiners love.

**Amides** are high-melting, strongly hydrogen-bonded solids (except liquid formamide/DMF-type
solvents); nitrogen's lone pair is busy conjugating with C=O, so amides are only feebly basic
and the C–N bond has partial double-bond character (the planar peptide bond). They hydrolyse
under forcing conditions, dehydrate with P₂O₅ to nitriles, and reduce with LiAlH₄ to amines
with the carbonyl carbon retained as CH₂. A **primary** amide with Br₂/NaOH undergoes the
**Hofmann bromamide reaction**: N-bromination, rearrangement to an isocyanate with loss of the
carbonyl carbon's bond to nitrogen, then hydrolysis to a primary amine with **one carbon
fewer** plus carbonate. Retention at the migrating group is a mechanistic jewel; the carbon
count is the examination takeaway.

::: example Worked example 19.2 — climb down the ladder, never up it
**Problem.** Outline conversions of ethanoic acid into (i) ethanamide and (ii) methylamine,
checking the carbon count at each stage.

**Solution.** (i) CH₃CO₂H + SOCl₂ → CH₃COCl; then excess NH₃ → **CH₃CONH₂** (two carbons
throughout; each step moves down or across the reactivity ladder). (ii) Treat ethanamide with
Br₂/NaOH: Hofmann rearrangement expels the carbonyl carbon as carbonate and yields
**CH₃NH₂** (one carbon). Trying to aminate ethanoic acid directly in one step, or expecting
LiAlH₄ to give methylamine (it would give ethylamine), are the standard carbon-count errors.
:::

Five traps the paper sets in this chapter: **Tollen's answers for every
aldehyde but Fehling's only for aliphatic ones** — benzaldehyde mirrors
silver yet leaves Fehling's blue; **aldol needs an α-hydrogen and dilute
base, Cannizzaro needs none and concentrated base**; **Clemmensen runs in
strong acid, Wolff–Kishner in strong base**, so each rescues the other's
casualties; **NaHCO₃ fizzes with carboxylic acids but not phenols**, the
cleanest family separation; and **Rosenmund's poisoned catalyst stops at
the aldehyde** where LiAlH₄ would run on to the alcohol.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Aldehydes and ketones are distinguished by oxidation behaviour, not by 2,4-DNP: aldehydes reduce Tollens' reagent; ordinary ketones do not.
- Controlled stopping power is the theme of preparation — PCC stops at aldehyde, poisoned Pd stops Rosenmund at aldehyde, and soft organometallics stop at ketone.
- Enolate chemistry at the α-carbon gives aldol products and the haloform reaction; blocking α-H switches an aldehyde to Cannizzaro disproportionation.
- Benzaldehyde combines an aldehyde's Tollens response with aromatic specialities: no Fehling reaction, Cannizzaro, benzoin and Perkin condensations.
- Carboxylic acids owe their acidity and high boiling points to carboxylate resonance and hydrogen-bonded dimers; substituent effects and HVZ chemistry operate through that same functional group.
- Formic acid breaks the family pattern by reducing mild oxidants and dehydrating to CO.
- Acyl derivatives react by addition–elimination in the order chloride > anhydride > ester > amide; easy conversions run down that ladder.
- Saponification, Claisen condensation and Hofmann bromamide degradation are the derivative reactions most likely to be recognized by product and carbon count.

   (a) NaOH (b) HCl (c) HNO₃ solution only (d) ammonia

25. Hofmann bromamide reaction converts a primary amide into a primary amine with
   (a) one carbon more (b) the same carbons (c) one carbon fewer (d) no nitrogen

::: note Answers and explanations — Set A
**1.** (d) — planar sp² geometry leaves the π bond open to nucleophilic attack from either face.
**2.** (a) — methanal, ethanal, propanal; the aldehyde carbon is C-1.
**3.** (c) — principal-group low locant outranks substituent preferences.
**4.** (b) — the poisoned catalyst stops hydrogenation at RCHO.
**5.** (a) — SnCl₂/HCl reduces RCN to an iminium salt hydrolysed to RCHO.
**6.** (d) — oxidation removes the carbinol H and the O–H proton equivalent.
**7.** (b) — both families condense with the hydrazine reagent.
**8.** (c) — a silver mirror (or grey Ag precipitate if the glass is not clean).
**9.** (b) — Cu(I) oxide; do not confuse the Cu₂O of cuprite/Ch. 14 contexts.
**10.** (a) — aromatic aldehydes are the textbook Fehling exception while still reducing Tollens'.
**11.** (d) — the product bears –OH and –CN on the former carbonyl carbon.
**12.** (c) — sterically accessible methyl ketones; many hindered ketones fail.
**13.** (a) — enolate formation is impossible without an α-H.
**14.** (c) — HCHO and C₆H₅CHO are the standard examples in concentrated alkali.
**15.** (b) — strongly acidic deoxygenation; protect acid-sensitive groups by choosing Wolff–Kishner instead.
**16.** (d) — the hydrazone is decomposed by KOH at high temperature with N₂ loss.
**17.** (c) — triiodomethane, yellow crystals of m.p. about 119 °C.
**18.** (b) — cyanide is both nucleophile and leaving group in the umpolung cycle.
**19.** (a) — stabilized with some methanol; it is a preservative/disinfectant, not “formic acid solution”.
**20.** (d) — RCO₂H is stronger than carbonic acid; phenol cannot do this.
**21.** (b) — PBr₃ generated in situ creates the enolizable acyl bromide form.
**22.** (d) — the laboratory source of CO from Ch. 13.
**23.** (c) — Cl⁻ is the weakest base/best leaving group in the series and donates least into C=O.
**24.** (a) — amide resonance is strongest and –NH₂ would be a very poor leaving group.
**25.** (c) — the carbonyl carbon is expelled as carbonate through an isocyanate.
:::

   (a) donating (+I), destabilizing acetate slightly (b) withdrawing by resonance (c) neutral in charge always (d) bonded to oxygen

   (a) acylium ion (b) carbon dioxide (c) enolate only (d) carboxylate

   (a) nucleophile (b) solvent (c) leaving group (d) acid
13. Acetyl chloride reacts violently with water because its carbonyl is highly electrophilic and chloride is a
   (a) good leaving group (b) strong base (c) reducing agent (d) bulky group
14. An anhydride is more reactive than an ester mainly because a carboxylate leaving group is a weaker base than an
   (a) alkyl group (b) proton (c) hydride (d) alkoxide
15. In Hofmann bromamide degradation, the product amine has one carbon fewer because the carbonyl carbon is lost (as carbonate) through an intermediate
   (a) carbene only (b) isocyanate (c) nitrile oxide (d) diazonium salt

::: note Answers and explanations — Set B
**1.** (c) — two +I groups make ketone carbon less δ+ and block the approach trajectory.
**2.** (d) — there is no aldehydic C–H to convert into C–OH; the carbon skeleton itself must be cleaved.
**3.** (a) — the equilibrium stops favouring a congested sp³ adduct.
**4.** (b) — an α,β-unsaturated carbonyl gains conjugation energy on losing water.
**5.** (d) — methanal has no α-carbon at all, so hydride-transfer disproportionation takes over.
**6.** (b) — ground-state conjugation lowers carbonyl electrophilicity relative to a small aliphatic aldehyde.
**7.** (c) — after CN addition and proton transfer, the former electrophilic carbon attacks a second benzaldehyde.
**8.** (a) — two equivalent resonance contributors produce bond order 1.5 for each C–O.
**9.** (b) — the same substituent argument quantified by pKₐ in Ch. 16.
**10.** (a) — HCO₂⁻ receives no comparable alkyl donation.
**11.** (d) — acid–base trapping removes a product and defeats the Fischer equilibrium.
**12.** (c) — amide resonance also flattens nitrogen and lowers carbonyl electrophilicity.
**13.** (a) — HCl's low pKₐ is the quick numerical proxy (see the pKₐ figure).
**14.** (d) — compare conjugate-acid pKₐ values near 5 (carboxylic acid) and 16 (alcohol).
**15.** (b) — R–N=C=O hydrolyses to RNH₂ + CO₂/carbonate under the alkaline conditions.
:::

**Set C — Application (10 questions)**

1. A C₃H₆O compound gives a silver mirror but no iodoform precipitate. It is
   (a) propanone (b) propanal (c) propan-1-ol (d) methoxyethane
2. A C₄H₈O compound gives a 2,4-DNP precipitate, no silver mirror, and a positive iodoform test. It is
   (a) butanal (b) butan-1-ol (c) butan-2-one (d) ethoxyethane
3. Ethanal with dilute NaOH, followed by heating the aldol product, gives mainly
   (a) but-2-enal (b) butan-1-ol (c) ethanoic acid (d) butane
4. Benzaldehyde heated with concentrated KOH gives benzyl alcohol together with potassium
   (a) formate (b) carbonate only (c) phenoxide (d) benzoate
5. Propanone treated with Zn–Hg and concentrated HCl gives
   (a) propan-2-ol (b) propene (c) propane (d) propanal
6. Toluene subjected to Etard oxidation (CrO₂Cl₂, then hydrolysis) gives
   (a) benzaldehyde (b) benzoic acid (c) benzyl alcohol (d) phenol
7. Benzaldehyde with ethanolic KCN gives
   (a) benzoic acid (b) hydrobenzamide only (c) cinnamic acid (d) benzoin
8. Ethylbenzene boiled with alkaline KMnO₄ and then acidified gives
   (a) phenylacetic acid (b) benzoic acid (c) acetophenone (d) phenol
9. Ethyl ethanoate boiled with aqueous NaOH gives ethanol and sodium
   (a) ethanoate (b) formate (c) propanoate (d) carbonate
10. Ethanamide treated with Br₂/NaOH gives
   (a) ethylamine (b) methylamine (c) ethanenitrile (d) dimethylamine

::: note Answers and explanations — Set C
**1.** (b) — the same evidence pattern as Worked example 19.1: Tollens demands an aldehyde; iodoform excludes the methyl-ketone rival.
**2.** (c) — a ketone by Tollens, and specifically a CH₃CO– ketone by iodoform; the C₄ skeleton leaves butan-2-one.
**3.** (a) — aldol addition gives 3-hydroxybutanal and dehydration conjugates the new double bond with –CHO.
**4.** (d) — Cannizzaro: one C₆H₅CHO is reduced to the alcohol, the other oxidized to benzoate.
**5.** (c) — Clemmensen replaces C=O by CH₂ without changing the three-carbon skeleton.
**6.** (a) — Etard stops side-chain oxidation at the aldehyde stage, unlike hot KMnO₄.
**7.** (d) — C₆H₅CH(OH)COC₆H₅, the α-hydroxy ketone from two benzaldehyde molecules.
**8.** (b) — a benzylic side chain with benzylic H is degraded to –CO₂H regardless of its length.
**9.** (a) — saponification: CH₃CO₂C₂H₅ + NaOH → CH₃CO₂Na + C₂H₅OH.
**10.** (b) — Hofmann degradation removes the amide carbonyl carbon: CH₃CONH₂ → CH₃NH₂.
:::
