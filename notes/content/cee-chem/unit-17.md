Hydrocarbons are the carbon skeletons on which the rest of organic chemistry is built, and
they contribute **about 3 questions**. The paper moves quickly from fuels to reaction
patterns: first how refineries reshape molecules for engines, then how σ-bonded alkanes,
π-bonded alkenes/alkynes and the delocalized benzene ring each react. Keep one mechanistic
question in front of you — **where are the available electrons?** Alkanes hide them in strong
σ bonds, alkenes and alkynes offer π electrons to electrophiles, and benzene offers π
electrons but fights to keep its aromatic sextet.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- **Cracking** breaks long hydrocarbons into shorter, more useful alkanes and alkenes. **Pyrolysis** is thermal decomposition without oxygen; steam cracking is high-temperature pyrolysis used especially for ethene/propene. **Reforming** rearranges/cyclizes/aromatizes straight chains to improve petrol quality and make BTX aromatics plus H₂.
- Octane number measures resistance to knocking in a spark-ignition engine: 2,2,4-trimethylpentane (“isooctane”) = 100, n-heptane = 0. It is **not** a measure of energy content. Cetane number measures diesel ignition quality (short ignition delay): n-hexadecane = 100. Straight chains tend to have low octane but high cetane.
- Tetraethyllead was the historic antiknock additive, now abandoned as a neurotoxic pollutant and catalyst poison. Modern blending uses branched alkylate, reformate, aromatics within limits and oxygenates such as ethanol; MTBE has groundwater-contamination concerns.
- Alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂) are tetrahedral, nonpolar and comparatively inert. Main reactions: combustion, free-radical halogenation, pyrolysis/cracking, isomerization and aromatization. Ethane burns, undergoes UV chlorination and cracks to ethene + H₂.
- Prepare alkanes by catalytic hydrogenation, Wurtz coupling (2 RX + 2 Na in dry ether; symmetrical products), decarboxylation of sodium carboxylates with soda lime (one carbon fewer) and reduction of alkyl halides.
- Alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ) are planar at C=C and show chain/position and E/Z isomerism. Prepare by alcohol dehydration, dehydrohalogenation with alcoholic KOH, Zn dehalogenation of vicinal dihalides and stereoselective partial hydrogenation of alkynes.
- Alkene additions: H₂/catalyst; X₂ (decolorized); HX by **Markovnikov** orientation through the more stable carbocation; HBr/peroxide by a radical anti-Markovnikov path; cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄ to a cis-diol; ozonolysis cleaves C=C to carbonyls; acid hydration gives Markovnikov alcohol.
- Alkynes (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂) are linear at C≡C. Prepare ethyne from CaC₂ + water or by double dehydrohalogenation. Lindlar H₂ gives a cis-alkene; Na/liquid NH₃ gives a trans-alkene. Hydration (HgSO₄/H₂SO₄) gives an enol that tautomerizes: ethyne → ethanal, a terminal alkyne → methyl ketone.
- A terminal alkyne C–H is weakly acidic (pKₐ ≈ 25) because the conjugate base holds its lone pair in an sp orbital (50% s). It forms metal acetylides with Na/NaNH₂, white Ag acetylide with ammoniacal AgNO₃ and red Cu(I) acetylide with ammoniacal Cu₂Cl₂.
- Benzene is a planar regular hexagon with six equal C–C bonds (≈139 pm) and a cyclic 6π cloud. Hückel aromaticity requires a cyclic, planar, fully conjugated system with 4n+2 π electrons. Benzene prefers electrophilic **substitution** over addition because substitution restores aromaticity. Key electrophiles: NO₂⁺ (nitration), polarized X₂/FeX₃ (halogenation), SO₃ (sulfonation), R⁺ or RCO⁺ equivalents with AlCl₃ (Friedel–Crafts).
:::

## 17.1 Cracking, pyrolysis and reforming; petrol quality, octane and cetane numbers and additives

Crude oil fractions seldom match market demand or engine design. Refineries therefore
perform molecular tailoring.

**Cracking** uses heat and/or a catalyst to split a long-chain hydrocarbon into shorter
molecules. A representative split is

C₁₆H₃₄ → C₈H₁₈ + C₈H₁₆.

Catalytic cracking over acidic zeolites gives branched, high-octane petrol components and
valuable propene/butenes. Hydrocracking adds H₂ over a catalyst and gives more saturated,
cleaner middle distillates.

**Pyrolysis** is decomposition by heat in the absence (or near-absence) of oxygen. In
petrochemistry, **steam cracking** of ethane, propane or naphtha at roughly 800–900 °C for a
fraction of a second maximizes ethene and propene for polymers. Even a simple alkane can be
described by several competing pyrolyses; for ethane the headline dehydrogenation is

C₂H₆ → C₂H₄ + H₂.

**Reforming** does not mainly shorten the chain. Over Pt/Re catalysts at high temperature,
straight-chain alkanes isomerize to branched alkanes and cyclize/dehydrogenate to aromatics.
For example,

n-C₇H₁₆ → C₆H₅CH₃ + 4H₂,

and n-hexane can be aromatized to benzene (also §17.3). Reformate has high octane; the H₂
co-product feeds refinery hydroprocessing. In older school language, “reforming” may also
describe converting straight-chain petrol hydrocarbons into branched ones — the goal is the
same shape change.

### Octane: resistance to unwanted self-ignition

In a petrol engine the spark should start a smooth flame front. **Knocking** is the
damaging, near-explosive autoignition of the unburned mixture ahead of that front. The
octane scale assigns **100** to very knock-resistant 2,2,4-trimethylpentane and **0** to
knock-prone n-heptane. A fuel rated 95 behaves, in the test engine, like a 95:5 blend of
those reference compounds. Laboratory RON and the harsher MON differ, but the examination
point is simple: **higher octane permits higher compression without knock; it does not
automatically mean more energy per litre**.

Branched alkanes, cycloalkanes, many aromatics and ethanol-rich blends resist knock better
than long straight chains. Tetraethyllead, Pb(C₂H₅)₄, once quenched radical pre-flame
chemistry at tiny concentration, but it spread lead through cities (Ch. 15), poisoned
catalytic converters and has been removed from road petrol worldwide. Present-day octane
comes from refinery streams and additives/blendstocks: alkylate, reformate, isomerate,
ethanol and other ether oxygenates where permitted. MTBE raises octane and oxygen content
but its taste/odour and mobility in groundwater led many jurisdictions to restrict it.
Detergents, antioxidants and metal deactivators are fuel additives too, but they manage
deposits and storage rather than the octane scale itself.

### Cetane: willingness to ignite on compression

A diesel engine has no spark: injected fuel must ignite promptly in hot compressed air.
**Cetane number** measures that readiness — higher cetane means shorter ignition delay,
smoother start and less diesel knock. n-Hexadecane (old name cetane) is 100. The structural
preference is opposite to petrol: a long straight chain autoignites readily, so it has high
cetane but poor octane. Cetane improvers such as 2-ethylhexyl nitrate decompose to radicals
that shorten ignition delay.

::: memory Two engines, opposite wishes
Spark engine: “wait for the spark” — high **octane**. Diesel: “ignite when squeezed, at
once” — high **cetane**. A straight-chain molecule obeys the diesel wish and disobeys the
petrol wish.
:::

::: example Pricing the pump
**Problem.** A petrol sample knocks exactly like a blend of 92 % iso-octane
and 8 % n-heptane by volume. State its octane number, and name two modern
additives that could raise it without lead. If a diesel matches 65 %
cetane with 35 % α-methylnaphthalene, what is its cetane number?
**Solution.** Octane number = the iso-octane percentage of the matching
blend = **92**; lead-free boosters include **ethanol and MTBE** (or
reformate aromatics). Cetane number = the cetane percentage = **65**. The
two scales run opposite temperaments: petrol wants reluctance to
auto-ignite, diesel wants eagerness — one barrel, two contradictory
virtues.
:::
## 17.2 Alkanes, alkenes and alkynes: isomerism, nomenclature and preparation; properties of ethane; additions and alkyne acidity

### Structures and isomerism at a glance

| Family | Bonding/geometry | Isomerism to watch |
|---|---|---|
| Alkane, CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | sp³, tetrahedral; rotation about σ bonds gives conformers, not new structural isomers | Chain isomerism begins at C₄; a substituted alkane frequent trap is missing a branched parent |
| Alkene, CₙH₂ₙ | sp², trigonal planar at C=C; π bond blocks rotation | Chain and position isomerism; cycloalkanes are ring–chain functional isomers; E/Z when each C=C carbon has two different groups |
| Alkyne, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ | sp, linear at C≡C; two π bonds | Chain/position isomerism; dienes and cycloalkenes share the formula; terminal vs internal controls acidity tests |

Nomenclature follows Ch. 16: longest chain including the multiple bond, lowest locant to the
multiple bond, ene before yne when a genuine tie must be broken, and E/Z descriptors when a
stereoisomer is specified. CH₃–C(CH₃)=CH–CH₃ is 2-methylbut-2-ene; CH≡C–CH(CH₃)₂ is
3-methylbut-1-yne.

### Preparation of alkanes and the chemistry of ethane

- **Catalytic hydrogenation:** an alkene or alkyne with H₂/Ni, Pt or Pd gives the alkane.
- **Wurtz reaction:** 2CH₃CH₂Br + 2Na →(dry ether) CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃ + 2NaBr. It joins two
  identical alkyl groups efficiently; two different halides give a mixture, and methane
  cannot be made this way.
- **Decarboxylation:** soda lime (NaOH + CaO) removes the carboxyl carbon:
  CH₃CO₂Na + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃. The alkane has one carbon fewer than the acid salt.
- **Reduction of alkyl halides:** Zn/acid and other reducing systems replace halogen by H.
- **Kolbe electrolysis** of a concentrated carboxylate can couple two alkyl radicals at the
  anode, a laboratory route to symmetrical even-carbon alkanes.

Ethane is a colourless, odourless, sparingly soluble gas (b.p. −89 °C). It burns completely,

2C₂H₆ + 7O₂ → 4CO₂ + 6H₂O,

and in oxygen-poor flames gives CO/soot. With Cl₂ in sunlight/UV it undergoes free-radical
substitution,

C₂H₆ + Cl₂ → C₂H₅Cl + HCl,

with further chlorination possible. The chain has initiation (Cl₂ → 2Cl•), propagation
(Cl• abstracts H, then C₂H₅• reacts with Cl₂) and termination (two radicals combine).
Bromination is slower and more selective in higher alkanes; direct iodination is
unfavourable because HI reduces the alkyl iodide back. Hot concentrated HNO₃ vapour can
nitrate alkanes (radical nitration), and strong heating cracks/pyrolyses them. Rotation
about ethane's C–C bond interconverts staggered (lower energy) and eclipsed conformations;
they are not separable isomers at room temperature.

Alkanes generally have low density, are insoluble in water and show boiling point rising
with chain length. Branching lowers boiling point by making molecules more compact and
reducing contact area/dispersion forces. Their old name **paraffins** (“little affinity”)
fits their indifference to acids, alkalis and ordinary oxidants: only strong σ bonds are
offered and there is no accessible π bond or lone pair.

### Preparation and addition chemistry of alkenes

Alkenes are made by **elimination**: dehydration of an alcohol with hot concentrated
H₂SO₄ or Al₂O₃; dehydrohalogenation of an alkyl halide with hot alcoholic KOH; and Zn
dehalogenation of a vicinal dihalide. When two β-hydrogens can be removed unequally, the
**Saytzeff (Zaitsev)** product — the more substituted, more stable alkene — usually
dominates. Partial hydrogenation of an alkyne with Lindlar catalyst adds H₂ syn to give the
cis-alkene; Na in liquid NH₃ gives the trans-alkene.

The π bond is the reactive handle. Typical electrophilic addition begins when the π electrons
attack E⁺, placing the new bond so that the *more stable carbocation* forms; a nucleophile
then completes the product. That mechanism is the reason behind **Markovnikov's rule** for
HX: hydrogen attaches to the alkene carbon already richer in hydrogen because protonation at
the other carbon gives the better carbocation. Thus propene + HCl gives 2-chloropropane.

| Reagent | Product pattern / examination hook |
|---|---|
| H₂, Ni/Pt/Pd | Alkane; syn delivery from the catalyst surface |
| Br₂ in CCl₄ | Vicinal dibromide; orange colour discharged — test for unsaturation. Addition is anti through a bromonium ion |
| HBr, no peroxide | Markovnikov bromide (ionic path) |
| HBr, peroxide/ROOR | Anti-Markovnikov bromide (radical path); the peroxide effect is special to HBr |
| Conc. H₂SO₄, then warm H₂O; or H₂O/H⁺ | Markovnikov alcohol by carbocation capture/rearrangement risk |
| 1. B₂H₆; 2. H₂O₂/OH⁻ (hydroboration–oxidation) | Anti-Markovnikov, syn alcohol without a free carbocation (developed again in Ch. 18) |
| Cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄ (Baeyer) | Syn vicinal diol; purple colour fades and brown MnO₂ appears — test for unsaturation |
| Hot concentrated KMnO₄ | Oxidative cleavage: =CH₂ → CO₂, =CHR → carboxylic acid, =CR₂ → ketone |
| O₃, then Zn/H₂O (reductive ozonolysis) | Cleaves C=C to aldehydes/ketones; joining the carbonyl carbons in reverse locates the original double bond |
| Heat/pressure or radical initiator; Ziegler–Natta for ethene | Addition polymer (polyethene etc.) |

::: example Worked example 17.1 — one alkene, two HBr answers
**Problem.** Give the major product when propene reacts with HBr (i) in the dark with no
peroxide and (ii) in the presence of a peroxide.

**Solution.** (i) Ionic addition: protonation that gives the secondary carbocation is
favoured, then Br⁻ attacks it → **2-bromopropane** (Markovnikov). (ii) Peroxide initiates
Br•. The bromine radical adds first so as to leave the more stable secondary carbon radical;
that radical abstracts H from HBr, placing Br on the terminal carbon →
**1-bromopropane** (anti-Markovnikov). Same reagents, different mechanism, opposite
orientation — and the trick does not transfer to HCl or HI.
:::

### Preparation, additions and acidity of alkynes

**Ethyne (acetylene)** is classically prepared by dropping water on calcium carbide:

CaC₂ + 2H₂O → C₂H₂↑ + Ca(OH)₂.

It is also obtained industrially by partial oxidation/high-temperature cracking of methane.
Alkynes in general come from double dehydrohalogenation of vicinal or geminal dihalides
(alcoholic KOH; the stronger NaNH₂ is often needed to finish a terminal alkyne), and from
Zn dehalogenation of vicinal tetrahalides. The old laboratory preparation
2CHCl₃ + 6Ag → C₂H₂ + 6AgCl illustrates reductive coupling of two one-carbon fragments.

Alkynes add many alkene reagents **twice** unless the conditions are tuned to stop halfway:

| Reagent | Outcome |
|---|---|
| H₂/Lindlar | cis-alkene (syn, poisoned catalyst) |
| Na or Li, liquid NH₃ | trans-alkene (dissolving-metal reduction) |
| H₂/Ni or Pt, excess | Alkane |
| Br₂ (2 equivalents) | Tetrabromoalkane; bromine is decolorized |
| HX (2 equivalents) | Geminal dihalide by two Markovnikov additions |
| H₂O, H₂SO₄/HgSO₄ (Kucherov hydration) | Enol → carbonyl: ethyne gives ethanal; propyne gives propanone; internal unsymmetrical alkynes may give ketone mixtures |
| O₃ then H₂O | Carboxylic acids from internal carbons; terminal ≡CH is driven to CO₂/formic-acid level |
| Red-hot iron tube (ethyne) | Cyclic trimerization to benzene (§17.3) |
| ammoniacal Cu(I) catalysis (ethyne) | Linear coupling/polymerization products such as vinylacetylene |

Terminal alkynes are carbon acids of modest strength: HC≡C–H has pKₐ ≈ 25, far more acidic
than ethene (≈44) or ethane (≈50) but still vastly weaker than water (≈15.7) or alcohols.
The trend follows s-character: an sp lone pair in ⁻C≡CR is held closer to the nucleus (50% s)
and is more stable than an sp² (33% s) or sp³ (25% s) lone pair. Therefore NaNH₂ and other
very strong bases form acetylides; aqueous NaOH does not meaningfully deprotonate a terminal
alkyne.

Diagnostic precipitations are coordination chemistry dressed as tests. With ammoniacal
AgNO₃, a terminal alkyne gives a **white silver acetylide**; with ammoniacal Cu₂Cl₂ it gives
a **red copper(I) acetylide**. Internal alkynes lack ≡C–H and give neither. Sodium metal
liberates H₂ from a terminal alkyne (and from alcohols/water too, so the sample must be
dry and alcohol-free before this is called a test). Do not store dry heavy-metal acetylides:
they can be shock-sensitive explosives.

Acetylene burns in oxygen at about 3100 °C, hot enough for welding/cutting; in air its flame
is smoky because of the high carbon fraction. Its compressed gas is stored dissolved in
acetone in a porous cylinder filling because free acetylene can decompose explosively under
pressure (Ch. 21 revisits the safety chemistry).

## 17.3 Aromaticity and resonance; preparation and properties of benzene

### Why benzene is not “cyclohexatriene”

Kekulé's alternating double-bond hexagon explains benzene's formula C₆H₆ but not its
behaviour. Experiment says all six C–C bonds are equal at about 139 pm (between C–C 154 pm
and C=C 134 pm), all angles are 120°, the molecule is planar, and it resists the addition
reactions that consume ordinary alkenes. Resonance theory draws two equivalent Kekulé
contributors; molecular-orbital language places three π bonding orbitals over the whole ring.
Either way, six π electrons occupy a cyclic cloud above and below the plane.

Thermochemistry measures the prize. Hydrogenating cyclohexene releases about 120 kJ mol⁻¹,
so a hypothetical 1,3,5-cyclohexatriene might be expected near 360 kJ mol⁻¹. Benzene
releases only about 208 kJ mol⁻¹ on complete hydrogenation: it starts about
**152 kJ mol⁻¹ lower in energy** than the invented triene. That difference is its empirical
resonance (aromatic stabilization) energy.

**Hückel's rule** generalizes the pattern for a monocyclic system: cyclic + planar + fully
conjugated + **4n+2** π electrons (n = 0, 1, 2, …) → aromatic. Benzene has 6 (n=1);
naphthalene's fused 10π perimeter fits the same count. The cyclopentadienyl anion (two π
bonds + a lone pair = 6) is aromatic, explaining cyclopentadiene's unusual acidity for a
hydrocarbon. Pyrrole's nitrogen lone pair is part of its sextet, whereas pyridine's lone
pair lies outside the π system and remains basic. A planar fully conjugated 4n π system is
antiaromatic; failure of planarity/conjugation makes a molecule simply non-aromatic.

### Preparation of benzene

- **Cyclic trimerization:** 3C₂H₂ passed through a red-hot iron/copper tube gives C₆H₆.
- **Decarboxylation:** sodium benzoate with soda lime gives benzene:
  C₆H₅CO₂Na + NaOH → C₆H₆ + Na₂CO₃.
- **From phenol:** distilling phenol with zinc dust removes oxygen:
  C₆H₅OH + Zn → C₆H₆ + ZnO.
- **Aromatization/reforming:** n-hexane over Cr₂O₃/Al₂O₃ or Pt at high temperature gives
  C₆H₁₄ → C₆H₆ + 4H₂; petroleum reformate and coal tar are industrial sources.

Benzene is a colourless, volatile, flammable liquid (b.p. 80.1 °C, m.p. 5.5 °C), lighter
than and almost insoluble in water, with a sweet odour. Modern safety practice treats it as
a proven human carcinogen associated with leukaemia; smelling it as a “test” belongs to
history. In air it burns with a very sooty flame:

2C₆H₆ + 15O₂ → 12CO₂ + 6H₂O.

### Chemical properties: substitution protects the sextet

If bromine simply added across one “double bond”, the product would surrender aromatic
stabilization. Instead benzene's π cloud attacks a strong electrophile, forming a
resonance-stabilized **arenium ion** (σ complex); a base then removes the proton from the
same carbon and the aromatic ring is restored. Overall this is **electrophilic aromatic
substitution**, not addition.

| Reaction | Conditions / electrophile | Product | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nitration | Conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄, about 50–55 °C; NO₂⁺ | Nitrobenzene | Gateway to aniline (Ch. 20); hotter mixed acid forces further substitution |
| Bromination/chlorination | Br₂/FeBr₃ or Cl₂/FeCl₃ (or Fe) in the dark; polarized X₂–Lewis acid | Bromobenzene/chlorobenzene | Benzene alone does not discharge Br₂ water — the catalyst is the point |
| Sulfonation | Fuming H₂SO₄ (SO₃) | Benzenesulfonic acid | Reversible; used as a removable blocking/solubilizing group |
| Friedel–Crafts alkylation | RCl/AlCl₃ | Alkylbenzene | Carbocation rearrangement and polyalkylation can spoil an otherwise easy plan |
| Friedel–Crafts acylation | RCOCl/AlCl₃ | Aryl ketone, e.g. phenylethanone | Product ring is deactivated, so reaction usually stops at one acyl group |

Under forcing conditions addition can be compelled: hydrogenation with Ni at high
temperature/pressure gives cyclohexane (C₆H₆ + 3H₂ → C₆H₁₂), and chlorine under UV adds
to give hexachlorocyclohexane isomers, C₆H₆Cl₆ (the γ-isomer is lindane, now heavily
restricted as a persistent pollutant). Ozonolysis attacks all three formal π bonds and,
after workup, glyoxal fragments are obtained — useful historically as evidence for the
ring, not a preparative method.

For a ring that already carries a substituent, the first group steers the second. The
school map (mechanism generalities from Ch. 16) is:

| Existing group type | Rate vs benzene | Main orientation |
|---|---|---|
| Strong/moderate donors: –NH₂, –OH, –OR, –NHCOR | Activating | ortho/para |
| Alkyl groups | Weakly activating | ortho/para |
| Halogens | Deactivating (−I stronger overall) | ortho/para (+R controls position) |
| Strong withdrawers: –NO₂, –CN, –CHO, –COR, –CO₂H, –SO₃H | Deactivating | meta |

::: caution Benzene and the bromine-water test
Benzene's formula is highly unsaturated (DU = 4), yet it does **not** decolorize bromine
water or cold alkaline KMnO₄ under alkene-test conditions. DU counts rings and π bonds; it
does not say those π electrons are available for ordinary addition. Aromatic stabilization
is exactly the energy that would be lost.
:::

::: caution
Five swaps the paper sets: **alcoholic KOH eliminates (alkene), aqueous KOH
substitutes (alcohol)** — the solvent chooses the path; **Markovnikov is
the ionic rule, the peroxide effect its radical exception, and the
exception works for HBr only** (HCl and HI chains fail energetically);
**ozonolysis with Zn/H₂O stops at aldehydes and ketones; oxidative workup
pushes on to acids**; **terminal alkynes answer silver and copper
ammoniates, internal ones stay silent** — and terminal alkynes are the
*acidic* hydrocarbons, not the reactive ones; and **benzene substitutes to
keep its resonance energy; addition (BHC, cyclohexane) needs light, heat
and excess reagent**.
:::

::: example Identifying an alkene from reductive ozonolysis products
**Problem.** Reductive ozonolysis ($\text{O}_3$ followed by $\text{Zn/H}_2\text{O}$) of an alkene $\text{C}_5\text{H}_{10}$ gives an equimolar mixture of acetone ($\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3$) and acetaldehyde ($\text{CH}_3\text{CHO}$). Deduce the structure and IUPAC name of the alkene.

**Solution.** Erase the two $\text{=O}$ oxygen atoms and join the carbonyl carbons by a double bond: $(\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C=O} + \text{O=CHCH}_3 \Longrightarrow (\text{CH}_3)_2\text{C=CHCH}_3$, which is **2-methylbut-2-ene**.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Refineries crack long chains, pyrolyse feedstocks to light alkenes and reform straight chains into branched/aromatic molecules that engines and polymer plants actually want.
- Octane rewards resistance to autoignition; cetane rewards prompt compression ignition. Molecular shape therefore wins opposite prizes in the two fuels.
- Alkanes are strong-σ, nonpolar and slow to react except by combustion and radical pathways; ethane exemplifies combustion, UV substitution and cracking.
- Alkenes spend a π bond in addition reactions. Carbocation stability explains Markovnikov orientation, while peroxide changes HBr addition into a radical anti-Markovnikov process.
- Alkynes can be stopped at a cis- or trans-alkene by choosing the reduction system; their terminal C–H is acidic because an sp conjugate base is comparatively stable.
- Benzene is stabilized by a six-electron cyclic π system (Hückel 4n+2), so electrophiles substitute a ring hydrogen rather than permanently adding and destroying aromaticity.
