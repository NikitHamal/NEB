Nitrogen appears in this **3-question** chapter in two opposite electronic costumes. In a
nitro group it is positively polarized, electron-withdrawing and reducible; in an amine it
carries a lone pair and behaves as a base/nucleophile. Organometallic reagents supply the
opposite carbon polarity — a nucleophilic carbon — and therefore complete many of the
syntheses begun in earlier chapters. Track oxidation level and carbon count, and most named
reactions in this unit stop being isolated facts.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- A **nitro compound** is R–NO₂ (bond through N); an alkyl nitrite is R–O–N=O. RX + AgNO₂ mainly gives the nitroalkane, while ionic KNO₂ mainly gives the alkyl nitrite — the classic ambident-nucleophile contrast.
- Primary/secondary nitroalkanes have acidic α-H (nitronate resonance) and dissolve in NaOH; tertiary nitroalkanes do not. Complete reduction (Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl, H₂/Ni) gives a primary amine.
- Nitrobenzene is made by benzene + conc. HNO₃/H₂SO₄ (NO₂⁺). Reduction menu: Sn/HCl → aniline; Zn/NH₄Cl (neutral) → N-phenylhydroxylamine; alkaline reducing couples → azoxybenzene/azobenzene/hydrazobenzene depending on conditions; strong-acid electrolytic route → p-aminophenol. –NO₂ is strongly deactivating and meta-directing.
- Amines are 1°, 2° or 3° by the number of carbon groups on **nitrogen**. C₃H₉N has four isomers: propan-1-amine, propan-2-amine, N-methyl­ethanamine and trimethylamine.
- Primary amines are prepared cleanly by reducing nitro compounds, nitriles (RCH₂NH₂) or amides (LiAlH₄); by Hofmann bromamide degradation of a primary amide (one carbon fewer); by Gabriel phthalimide synthesis (avoids over-alkylation); and less cleanly by ammonolysis of RX.
- Basicity: aliphatic amines > NH₃ >> aniline. In water, methylamines follow (CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > NH₃ because inductive donation competes with solvation/steric effects. Aniline is weak because its lone pair is delocalized into the ring; electron-withdrawing ring groups weaken it further.
- **Hoffmann separation** uses diethyl oxalate: 1° → solid oxamide derivative, 2° → liquid oxamic ester, 3° → no reaction; fractions are then hydrolysed back to amines. Hinsberg's benzenesulfonyl chloride gives the parallel diagnostic separation.
- Nitrous acid separates amine classes: 1° aliphatic → N₂ effervescence and alcohol mixture; aniline at 0–5 °C → benzenediazonium salt; 2° → yellow oily N-nitrosamine; 3° aliphatic → soluble nitrite salt with no N₂. Only primary amines give the carbylamine test.
- Aniline is acetylated to acetanilide; diazotized and coupled to azo dyes; and its ring is so activated that bromine water gives white 2,4,6-tribromoaniline. Protect –NH₂ as –NHCOCH₃ before controlled nitration.
- A Grignard reagent RMgX is made from RX + Mg in dry ether. It behaves as R⁻: acids/water → RH, CO₂ → RCO₂H, HCHO → 1° alcohol, other aldehyde → 2° alcohol, ketone → 3° alcohol, ester (2 equivalents) → 3° alcohol, nitrile → ketone after hydrolysis.
- Reactivity/selectivity: RLi is more reactive/basic than RMgX; Gilman reagents R₂CuLi and organocadmiums R₂Cd are softer/less reactive — both can convert RCOCl to a ketone without adding again, and Gilman reagents excel at coupling and conjugate addition.
:::

## 20.1 Nitroalkanes and nitrobenzene

### Nitroalkanes: a nitrogen group with an acidic α-carbon

The nitro group is best drawn as a resonance hybrid, R–N⁺(=O)O⁻ ↔ R–N⁺(O⁻)=O, with two
equal N–O bonds and powerful –I/–R electron withdrawal. Nitroalkanes are classified 1°, 2°
or 3° by the carbon attached to NO₂. They are distinct functional isomers of alkyl nitrites,
where oxygen is attached to carbon.

Laboratory preparation treats a primary (or suitable secondary) alkyl bromide/iodide with
silver nitrite in aqueous ethanol: C₂H₅Br + AgNO₂ → C₂H₅NO₂ + AgBr. AgNO₂ is appreciably
covalent, so nitrogen attack is favoured; ionic KNO₂ attacks mainly through oxygen and gives
ethyl nitrite. Industrially, alkanes undergo vapour-phase radical nitration with HNO₃ near
400 °C (useful mixtures of nitromethane, nitroethane and nitropropanes).

Nitroalkanes are polar, relatively high-boiling and sparingly soluble in water. Their
signature is the acidity of an α-hydrogen: nitromethane has pKₐ about 10 because the
conjugate **nitronate** anion is resonance-stabilized. Primary and secondary nitroalkanes
therefore dissolve in NaOH, form salts and can be α-halogenated or condensed with aldehydes
(the Henry/nitroaldol reaction); a tertiary nitroalkane has no α-H and fails these tests.
With nitrous acid, a primary nitroalkane gives a nitrolic acid whose alkali salt is red, a
secondary gives a blue pseudonitrol, and a tertiary gives no colour — the nitro counterpart
of the Victor Meyer distinction.

Acidic hydrolysis is powerful but must be worded carefully. Boiling a primary nitroalkane
with concentrated HCl gives a carboxylic acid and hydroxylamine. Under the milder **Nef
conditions** — form the nitronate salt in base, then acidify — a primary nitroalkane gives
an aldehyde and a secondary one a ketone. Reduction is the synthetic headline: Sn/HCl,
Fe/HCl or H₂/Ni carries RNO₂ to RNH₂, while Zn/NH₄Cl can stop at an N-alkylhydroxylamine.

### Nitrobenzene: preparation, reduction menu and ring behaviour

Benzene is nitrated below about 60 °C with concentrated HNO₃/H₂SO₄. The active electrophile
is nitronium, NO₂⁺. Nitrobenzene is a pale-yellow, bitter-almond-smelling oil; like several
nitroarenes it is toxic, oxidizing haemoglobin Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺ (methaemoglobinaemia).

Reduction can stop at several oxidation levels, and in alkaline solution partly reduced
molecules condense in pairs:

| Medium/reagent | Main product | Reading of the result |
|---|---|---|
| Sn/HCl, Fe/HCl or H₂/catalyst | Aniline | Complete six-electron reduction |
| Zn dust + NH₄Cl (neutral aqueous) | N-Phenylhydroxylamine | Reduction pauses before aniline |
| Alkaline glucose or arsenious oxide/NaOH | Azoxybenzene | Nitrosobenzene + phenylhydroxylamine condense |
| Zn/NaOH in methanol; also LiAlH₄ | Azobenzene | Azoxy compound reduced one stage further |
| Zn dust + aqueous NaOH | Hydrazobenzene | N–N single-bond stage |
| Electrolytic, strongly acidic | p-Aminophenol | Phenylhydroxylamine undergoes acid-catalysed rearrangement |

The school value of the table is the **medium**, not a claim that every industrial reactor
gives one textbook product regardless of stoichiometry. Nitrobenzene itself resists further
electrophilic substitution: –NO₂ withdraws by –I and –R, destabilizing the σ-complex
especially for ortho/para attack, so forced nitration gives m-dinitrobenzene and
Friedel–Crafts reactions essentially fail (nitrobenzene is even used as an inert solvent
for some of them). Nitro groups are explosophores — TNT is 2,4,6-trinitrotoluene — and
nitrobenzene's great constructive use is reduction to aniline for dyes, drugs and polymers.

::: example A Grignard by the mole
**Problem.** Methyl magnesium bromide is made from 0.3 mol of magnesium
with excess CH₃Br, then poured onto crushed dry ice (CO₂) and worked up
with acid. How many moles of acid form, and what mass of acetic acid is
that? (M of CH₃COOH = 60 g/mol.) Why must every trace of water be gone
before the CO₂ arrives?
**Solution.** One Mg gives one Grignard, and one Grignard fixes one CO₂:
**0.3 mol of CH₃COOMgBr**, hydrolysed to **0.3 mol acetic acid**, a mass
of 0.3 × 60 = **18 g**. Water must be absent because its O–H proton is
far more acidic than anything else present: it would protonate the
carbanion carbon instantly, giving methane and Mg(OH)Br — the reagent
suiciding on the solvent instead of carboxylating.
:::
## 20.2 Amines: classification, preparation, basicity, Hoffmann separation and aniline

### Classification and clean routes to primary amines

Count carbon groups attached to nitrogen: RNH₂ is primary, R₂NH secondary, R₃N tertiary and
R₄N⁺X⁻ a quaternary ammonium salt. “Aromatic amine” in the strict reactivity sense means N is
bonded directly to the ring (aniline); benzylamine has an insulating CH₂ and behaves as an
aliphatic amine. Amines with N–H bonds hydrogen-bond (less strongly than alcohols), smell
fishy at low molar mass and form water-soluble salts with acids — a purification handle used
throughout pharmaceutical chemistry.

| Route to a 1° amine | Carbon-count rule | Main caution |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce RNO₂ or ArNO₂ | Same carbon skeleton | Choose the reduction medium; alkaline conditions may dimerize nitrobenzene |
| RX + excess NH₃ (ammonolysis), then base | Same R | Product amine is also nucleophilic → 2°, 3° and R₄N⁺ mixtures unless NH₃ is in large excess |
| Reduce RCN (H₂/Ni, Na/EtOH Mendius, or LiAlH₄) | Nitrile C becomes CH₂NH₂; versus RX→RCN this is +1 C overall | A clean chain-extension route |
| Reduce RCONH₂ with LiAlH₄ | Same total C; carbonyl C becomes CH₂ | Strong hydride tolerates few other reducible groups |
| **Hofmann bromamide:** RCONH₂ + Br₂/NaOH | One carbon fewer (Ch. 19) | Only a primary amide gives the classic reaction |
| **Gabriel synthesis:** potassium phthalimide + RX, then hydrazinolysis/hydrolysis | Same R | RX must be SN2-competent (1°/methyl); aryl halides fail, so not a route to aniline |

Aniline is manufactured by catalytic reduction of nitrobenzene. Laboratory alternatives are
phenol with NH₃ under forcing catalytic conditions and chlorobenzene with NH₃ under
Cu-catalysed high-pressure amination.

### Basicity: donation, solvation and resonance compete

A base is compared by the pKₐ of its conjugate acid (pKₐH): the higher the pKₐH, the
stronger the base. Alkyl groups donate electron density and should give 3° > 2° > 1°, but in
water the ammonium ions are stabilized by hydrogen-bonding solvation best when they carry
more N–H bonds and less steric shielding. The observed aqueous methyl order is therefore
(CH₃)₂NH > CH₃NH₂ > (CH₃)₃N > NH₃; for ethyl groups Et₂NH > Et₃N > EtNH₂ > NH₃. In the gas
phase, where solvation vanishes, the polarizability/inductive order 3° > 2° > 1° emerges.
An examiner who gives no solvent usually expects the aqueous textbook order — read the stem.

Aniline is in another league altogether (pKₐH ≈ 4.6 versus about 10–11 for alkylamines).
Its lone pair is an integral part of the ring π system in the free base; protonation forms
anilinium, whose nitrogen has no lone pair left to conjugate, so the base is stabilized
relative to its conjugate acid. p-Methyl and p-methoxy groups donate and make aniline a
little stronger; p-NO₂ withdraws by –I/–R and makes p-nitroaniline feeble (pKₐH ≈ 1.0).
Benzylamine (pKₐH ≈ 9.3) shows how one CH₂ breaks the conjugation and restores an
alkylamine-like base.

### Separating and identifying 1°, 2° and 3° amines

**Hoffmann's separation** shakes the mixture with diethyl oxalate. A primary amine gives a
solid dialkyl oxamide; a secondary amine gives a liquid ethyl oxamate (an oxamic ester); a
tertiary amine does not react. Fractional distillation removes the tertiary amine first and
then the secondary amine's liquid derivative, leaving the primary derivative as a solid
residue. Hydrolysis of each derivative with KOH regenerates its amine.

**Hinsberg's test** uses benzenesulfonyl chloride in NaOH. A primary sulfonamide still has
an acidic N–H and dissolves in alkali; a secondary sulfonamide has no N–H and remains
insoluble; a tertiary amine forms no sulfonamide. Acidification reprecipitates the primary
derivative, and vigorous hydrolysis returns the amines.

Cold **nitrous acid** (NaNO₂ + dilute HCl) is the fastest classifier. A primary aliphatic
amine forms an unstable diazonium ion that loses **N₂ with brisk effervescence**, leaving
mainly alcohol-derived products. A secondary amine gives a yellow oily **N-nitrosamine**
(no gas). A tertiary aliphatic amine simply forms a soluble nitrite salt. Aniline is the
celebrated special case: its diazonium salt survives at 0–5 °C long enough to become
synthetic gold (next paragraph). The **carbylamine test** (CHCl₃ + alcoholic KOH → foul
isocyanide) is positive only for primary amines, aliphatic or aromatic.

### Aniline: a weak base with a fiercely reactive ring

Aniline is a colourless oil that darkens in air. It forms salts with acids, is alkylated
(usually to mixtures) by alkyl halides and is acylated smoothly: acetic anhydride gives
**acetanilide**, in which the amide nitrogen is a tamer, still ortho/para-directing donor.
Benzoyl chloride in aqueous NaOH gives benzanilide under Schotten–Baumann conditions.

Diazotization at 0–5 °C with NaNO₂/HCl gives benzenediazonium chloride. Warming with water
replaces N₂⁺ by OH (phenol); CuCl or CuBr gives chloro-/bromobenzene (**Sandmeyer**); KI
gives iodobenzene; hypophosphorous acid replaces the group by H; and coupling with phenol in
mild alkali or with β-naphthol gives orange to red **azo dyes**. The diazonium group is thus
a removable steering wheel fixed to the ring through aniline.

Free –NH₂ activates the ring so strongly that bromine water instantly precipitates white
**2,4,6-tribromoaniline**. Direct nitration is messy: acid oxidizes aniline and converts
much of it to meta-directing anilinium. The examination solution is protection —
acetylate to acetanilide, nitrate (para major), then hydrolyse. Concentrated H₂SO₄ and heat
ultimately give sulfanilic acid (p-aminobenzenesulfonic acid, an internal salt important in
dye and drug history), while Friedel–Crafts reactions fail because the basic nitrogen binds
the Lewis-acid catalyst.

::: example Worked example 20.1 — classify C₃H₉N without a spectrum
**Problem.** Four isomeric amines C₃H₉N are available. Sample A gives brisk N₂ with cold
HNO₂ and a foul carbylamine odour. Sample B gives a yellow oil with HNO₂ and no gas. Sample
C gives neither result. Classify A–C.

**Solution.** A is a **primary** amine (propan-1-amine or propan-2-amine; the two tests fix
the class, not the skeleton). B is the only **secondary** isomer, N-methylethanamine. C is
**trimethylamine**, tertiary. One gas syringe and one unforgettable smell have done the work
of a spectrum — provided the class logic was learned, not the odour by experience.
:::


::: example Worked example — the aqueous basicity order of ethylamines
Predict the order of basic strength **in water**: ethylamine, diethylamine, triethylamine, ammonia.

Three effects compete: (a) the **+I (inductive) effect** of alkyl groups pushes electrons toward nitrogen: 3° > 2° > 1°; (b) **solvation** of the conjugate acid by hydrogen bonding is best for the least crowded ion: 1° > 2° > 3°; (c) **steric hindrance** also increases with substitution. Balancing them for ethylamines in water gives **(C₂H₅)₂NH > (C₂H₅)₃N > C₂H₅NH₂ > NH₃**.

The traps: in the **gas phase** (no solvation) the order is purely inductive, 3° > 2° > 1° > NH₃; and **aniline** is far weaker than all of these because its lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring.
:::

::: caution Trap — three reductions/amines tests that swap in MCQs
The **carbylamine** (foul-smelling isocyanide) test is given by **primary amines only**; **Hoffmann bromamide** converts an amide into an amine with **one carbon fewer** (the carbonyl carbon is lost as carbonate); and aniline's diazotisation must be done at **0–5 °C** — the diazonium salt decomposes (to phenol, releasing N₂) if the mixture warms up.
:::

## 20.3 Organolithium, organocopper and organocadmium compounds; the Grignard reagent

An **organometallic compound** contains a direct carbon–metal bond. Because carbon is more
electronegative than Li, Mg, Cu or Cd, that bond is polarized Cδ−–Mδ+, making carbon behave
as a nucleophile/strong base. The art is choosing the metal to tune ferocity.

**Organolithium reagents (RLi)** are made from an organic halide and lithium metal in dry
ether or hydrocarbon, or by halogen–lithium exchange. They often exist as aggregates and are
more reactive and more strongly basic than Grignard reagents. They are protonated instantly
by water/alcohols to RH, add to carbonyl compounds and CO₂, deprotonate weak C–H acids and
exchange with aryl/vinyl halides. n-BuLi solutions can ignite in air; “dry and inert
atmosphere” is a safety instruction, not a style preference.

**Organocopper reagents** used at this level are chiefly lithium diorganocuprates,
**R₂CuLi (Gilman reagents)**, from 2 RLi + CuI. Copper makes carbon softer and more selective:
a Gilman reagent couples with an alkyl/aryl/vinyl halide to form a C–C bond (**Corey–House
synthesis**, one route to unsymmetrical alkanes that Wurtz cannot manage cleanly), converts
an acid chloride to a ketone and stops, and adds 1,4 (conjugate) to an α,β-unsaturated
carbonyl where a Grignard reagent often adds 1,2 to C=O.

**Organocadmium reagents (R₂Cd)** are made by transmetallation, 2 RMgX + CdCl₂ → R₂Cd +
2 MgXCl. They are still less reactive: acid chlorides are converted to ketones, but the
ketone/ester/nitrile functions are largely left alone. That selectivity made them textbook
favourites; cadmium's severe toxicity (Ch. 15) means modern laboratories prefer cuprates or
organozinc reagents, a good example of methodology changing under toxicology.

### The Grignard reagent

Magnesium turnings react with RX in rigorously dry ether or THF to give **RMgX**. Iodides and
bromides form most readily; aryl/vinyl bromides work, chlorides may need THF/initiation. The
solution contains solvated species in Schlenk equilibrium (2 RMgX ⇌ R₂Mg + MgX₂), but the
school formula RMgX captures its chemistry: treat R as a carbanion equivalent.

| Grignard partner | After aqueous workup | Carbon-count lesson |
|---|---|---|
| H₂O, ROH, RCO₂H, terminal alkyne (any acidic H) | RH (the reagent is destroyed) | Why solvents/substrates must be dry and proton-free |
| D₂O | RD | A clean way to label one C–H position |
| CO₂ (dry ice), then H₃O⁺ | RCO₂H | +1 carbon |
| Methanal (HCHO) | RCH₂OH, primary alcohol | +1 carbon |
| Any other aldehyde | Secondary alcohol | New C–C bond at carbonyl carbon |
| Ketone | Tertiary alcohol | Three carbon groups on carbinol C |
| Ester with 2 RMgX | Tertiary alcohol (formate ester gives secondary) | The ketone formed first is attacked again |
| Nitrile, then hydrolysis | Ketone | Addition stops at an imine salt until workup |
| Epoxide (oxirane) | Primary alcohol after ring opening at the less hindered carbon | +2 carbons from ethylene oxide |
| Sulfur, then workup | Thiol RSH | Electrophilic heteroatoms also accept R |

Two limitations decide many synthesis questions. A Grignard reagent cannot be prepared from
a halide that also carries an acidic proton (–OH, –NH, –CO₂H, terminal –C≡CH) or a carbonyl/
nitro group, because the reagent would attack its own precursor. And because acid chlorides
and esters pass through a ketone stage, excess RMgX adds twice; use R₂Cd or R₂CuLi when the
ketone itself is the target.

::: memory Choose the metal by stopping power
Want maximum force/basicity? **RLi**. General carbon nucleophile? **RMgX**. Need to stop at a
ketone or couple two carbons? **R₂CuLi** (or the older, toxic **R₂Cd** of the textbooks).
:::


::: example Worked example — predicting Grignard products by the carbonyl type
CH₃–MgBr reacts first by **addition** to the carbonyl carbon; water (work-up) then protonates the alkoxide. So ask what the carbonyl compound is:

- With **formaldehyde** (HCHO): CH₃–CH₂–OH — a **primary** alcohol (ethanol), one carbon added.
- With any **other aldehyde** (e.g. ethanal): a **secondary** alcohol, CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₃.
- With a **ketone** (e.g. propanone): a **tertiary** alcohol, (CH₃)₃C–OH.
- With **CO₂** (dry ice): after hydrolysis, a carboxylic acid with one extra carbon — CH₃–COOH.

One mechanism (nucleophilic addition of the carbanion-like CH₃⁻) predicts all four answers — and also why the reagent is destroyed by water, alcohol or any –OH/–NH compound (it simply grabs the proton, giving methane).
:::

::: caution
Five swaps the paper loves here: **AgNO₂ gives the nitroalkane, KNO₂ the
alkyl nitrite** — same anion, two mouths; **Sn/HCl gives aniline but
Zn/NaOH gives azobenzene and Zn/NH₄Cl the hydroxylamine** — the medium
steers the reduction; **amine classes count carbons on nitrogen, amide
classes count carbonyls on nitrogen**; **the carbylamine and diazotisation
tests need a primary amine's N–H hydrogens**, which secondary and
tertiary lack; and **any acidic hydrogen — water, alcohol, terminal
alkyne — kills a Grignard**, so dry ether is not a preference but a
condition of existence.
:::

## Chapter summary

- The nitro group withdraws electrons, acidifies an α-C–H and can be reduced all the way to an amine or stopped/dimerized at intermediate oxidation levels by choosing the medium.
- Nitrobenzene's ring is deactivated and meta-directing, in complete contrast to the aniline produced from it.
- Primary-amine synthesis is an exercise in carbon bookkeeping: nitrile reduction extends, Hofmann degradation shortens, and Gabriel protects nitrogen from over-alkylation.
- Aqueous basicity balances alkyl donation against solvation; resonance makes aniline and especially p-nitroaniline feeble bases.
- Hoffmann and Hinsberg separations exploit the presence or absence of N–H bonds; nitrous acid and the carbylamine reaction identify amine class in test tubes.
- Diazotization converts aniline into a versatile leaving group for phenols, haloarenes and azo dyes, while ring protection tames aniline for controlled substitution.
- Organometallic carbon is nucleophilic: lithium gives force, magnesium gives generality, and copper/cadmium give the selectivity needed to stop at ketones or form chosen C–C bonds.
