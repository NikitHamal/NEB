Analytical chemistry is the detective work of the subject: qualitative tests ask **what is
present**, separations ask **how can it be isolated**, and titrations ask **how much**. It
carries **about 3 questions**, usually as a colour, precipitate, gas, separation choice or a
short titration calculation. The winning habit is to treat every observation as evidence with
a chemical reason — a white precipitate is only useful when you also know where it dissolves,
what gas came before it and which rival ion could imitate it.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- Dilute-acid anion gases: carbonate/bicarbonate → CO₂ (lime water milky, excess clears); sulfite → SO₂ (acidified dichromate turns green); sulfide → H₂S (lead-ethanoate paper blackens); nitrite → NO browning in air; ethanoate → vinegar smell on warming.
- Confirmatory anions: sulfate = BaCl₂/HCl white ppt insoluble in acid; nitrate = brown-ring test; phosphate = yellow ppt with ammonium molybdate/HNO₃ on warming; halides with AgNO₃: AgCl white (soluble in dilute NH₄OH), AgBr pale yellow (sparingly soluble in conc. NH₄OH), AgI yellow (insoluble).
- Cation groups: I dil. HCl (Ag⁺, Hg₂²⁺, Pb²⁺; PbCl₂ dissolves in hot water, AgCl in NH₄OH, Hg₂Cl₂ blackens with NH₄OH); II H₂S/dil. HCl; III NH₄OH/NH₄Cl (Fe³⁺ brown ppt, Al³⁺ white, Cr³⁺ green); IV H₂S/NH₄OH (ZnS white, MnS flesh, NiS/CoS black); V (NH₄)₂CO₃ (Ba²⁺/Sr²⁺/Ca²⁺ white carbonates); VI Mg²⁺/Na⁺/K⁺ remain. NH₄⁺ is tested in the original sample (Group 0).
- Spot tests: Fe³⁺ + SCN⁻ blood-red; Cu²⁺ + excess NH₄OH deep-blue [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺; Ni²⁺ + dimethylglyoxime rosy-red ppt; flames Na golden-yellow, K lilac (cobalt glass), Ca brick-red, Sr crimson, Ba apple-green, Cu blue-green.
- Organic unsaturation: Br₂/CCl₄ decolorized and cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄ (Baeyer) discharged — but benzene is negative, and phenol/aniline decolorize bromine water by substitution with a precipitate.
- Functional-group anchors: –CO₂H fizzes with NaHCO₃; phenol violet with FeCl₃; aldehyde Tollens/Fehling positive, ketone only 2,4-DNP; CH₃CO– iodoform positive; terminal alkyne gives white Ag(I)/red Cu(I) acetylide; 1° amine carbylamine positive.
- **Lassaigne:** sodium fusion converts organically bound N → CN⁻, S → S²⁻, halogen → X⁻ (N+S may give SCN⁻). Nitrogen → Prussian blue; sulfur → violet with sodium nitroprusside or black PbS; N+S → blood-red Fe³⁺ thiocyanate colour; halogens → AgX after boiling the extract with HNO₃.
- Food tests: Molisch = carbohydrate (violet ring); Benedict/Fehling = reducing sugar (brick-red Cu₂O); iodine = starch (blue-black); Biuret = peptide bonds (violet); xanthoproteic = aromatic amino-acid residues (yellow → orange in alkali); grease spot/Sudan dyes = fat.
- Separations follow a property difference: filtration (particle size), sublimation (vapour pressure of a solid), evaporation/crystallization (solubility), precipitation (selective insolubility), distillation (volatility; fractional for close boiling points, steam for heat-sensitive immiscibles, vacuum to lower b.p.), paper chromatography (partition; $R_f$ = spot distance ÷ solvent-front distance), atmolysis (Graham diffusion, rate ∝ 1/√M).
- Indicator choice follows the pH at equivalence: strong acid–weak base → methyl orange/red; weak acid–strong base → phenolphthalein; strong–strong → either. Endpoint (observed) must be matched to equivalence point (stoichiometric).
- KMnO₄ titrations are self-indicating, use H₂SO₄ (not HCl), and in acid MnO₄⁻ takes 5 e⁻ to Mn²⁺. **Iodimetry** titrates a reductant directly with I₂; **iodometry** lets an oxidant liberate I₂ from I⁻ and titrates it with thiosulfate, adding starch only near the pale-straw end point.
- EDTA binds metal ions 1:1. At pH 10 with Eriochrome Black T, wine-red → blue marks total Ca²⁺+Mg²⁺ hardness; results are reported as mg L⁻¹ CaCO₃ equivalent.
:::

## 22.1 Tests for acid and basic radicals; unsaturation and functional groups; distinction tests; Lassaigne's test; fats, proteins and carbohydrates

### Acid radicals (anions): let the gas speak first

Qualitative salt analysis begins with the sample's appearance and solubility, but the
systematic anion hunt starts with dilute H₂SO₄, warm if needed:

| Observation with dil. H₂SO₄ | Ion indicated | Confirmation |
|---|---|---|
| Brisk effervescence; gas turns lime water milky, excess gas clears it (soluble bicarbonate) | CO₃²⁻ / HCO₃⁻ | CO₂ has no smell and no effect on dichromate paper |
| Choking gas of burning sulfur; turns acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ paper green | SO₃²⁻ | SO₂ also decolorizes dilute KMnO₄ |
| Rotten-egg gas; lead-ethanoate paper turns black (PbS) | S²⁻ | Sodium nitroprusside on alkaline extract → violet |
| Colourless gas browning at the mouth of the tube (NO → NO₂ in air); brown fumes | NO₂⁻ | FeSO₄ gives a dark brown/black ring even with dilute acid |
| Vinegar smell, stronger on warming | CH₃CO₂⁻ | Neutral FeCl₃ gives a deep-red colour; esterification gives fruity ethyl ethanoate |
| No gas | Try conc. H₂SO₄ tests and specific tests below | — |

Concentrated H₂SO₄ (handle as the dehydrating oxidant of Ch. 13) interrogates a second tier:
chlorides fume HCl (white smoke with an NH₃ rod; with MnO₂, greenish Cl₂); bromides give
HBr partly oxidized to orange Br₂ vapour; iodides give violet I₂ vapour; nitrates with Cu
turnings give brown NO₂; oxalates evolve CO + CO₂ without charring; fluorides generate HF,
which etches glass. These are old but logically ordered tests — the strong acid first
expels a weaker/more volatile acid, then may oxidize it.

Independent confirmations settle the common ions:

- **Sulfate:** BaCl₂ to an acidified solution gives white BaSO₄ that stays insoluble in dilute
  HCl — acid solubility separates it from BaCO₃/BaSO₃ impostors.
- **Nitrate (brown ring):** fresh FeSO₄ solution, then conc. H₂SO₄ poured down the wall; a
  brown [Fe(H₂O)₅(NO)]²⁺ ring forms at the junction. Do not shake the layers away.
- **Halides:** AgNO₃ after acidifying with dilute HNO₃ — AgCl white and soluble in dilute
  NH₄OH, AgBr pale yellow and only sparingly soluble in concentrated NH₄OH, AgI yellow and
  insoluble. The organic-layer test agrees: chlorine water liberates Br₂ (orange) or I₂
  (violet) into CHCl₃/CCl₄.
- **Phosphate:** ammonium molybdate in conc. HNO₃, warmed, gives a canary-yellow precipitate
  of ammonium phosphomolybdate.
- **Chromate:** yellow solution; BaCl₂ gives yellow BaCrO₄ soluble in strong acid; acidifying
  shifts CrO₄²⁻ (yellow) toward Cr₂O₇²⁻ (orange), Ch. 7 in colour.

### Basic radicals (cations): separation by controlled precipitation

The classical scheme precipitates groups in order, each reagent exploiting a solubility
cliff. Ammonium is hunted first (**Group 0**) because later reagents add it: warm the
original solid with NaOH — NH₃ turns moist red litmus blue and gives a brown precipitate
with Nessler's reagent.

| Group reagent | Ions precipitated | Discriminating observations |
|---|---|---|
| I: dilute HCl | Ag⁺, Hg₂²⁺, Pb²⁺ as white chlorides | PbCl₂ dissolves in hot water; AgCl dissolves in NH₄OH; Hg₂Cl₂ turns black with NH₄OH |
| II: H₂S in dilute HCl | Cu²⁺/Pb²⁺/Hg²⁺ (black sulfides), Cd²⁺ (yellow CdS), As³⁺ (yellow), Sb³⁺ (orange), Sn²⁺ (brown) | Acid suppresses S²⁻ (common-ion logic), so only the least soluble sulfides fall |
| III: NH₄OH + NH₄Cl | Fe³⁺, Al³⁺, Cr³⁺ as hydroxides | Fe(OH)₃ reddish-brown, Al(OH)₃ white gelatinous (amphoteric), Cr(OH)₃ green |
| IV: H₂S in NH₄OH | Zn²⁺, Mn²⁺, Ni²⁺, Co²⁺ as sulfides | ZnS white, MnS flesh/pink, NiS and CoS black |
| V: (NH₄)₂CO₃ + NH₄Cl/NH₄OH | Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺ as white carbonates | Flame: Ba apple-green, Sr crimson, Ca brick-red |
| VI: filtrate | Mg²⁺, Na⁺, K⁺ | Mg²⁺ white crystalline MgNH₄PO₄ with Na₂HPO₄/NH₄OH; flames settle Na (golden) and K (lilac through cobalt glass) |

Spot tests finish what groups begin: Fe³⁺ with KSCN gives an intense blood-red complex;
Fe³⁺ with K₄[Fe(CN)₆] gives Prussian blue; Cu²⁺ turns deep blue in excess NH₄OH after a
pale-blue Cu(OH)₂ stage (Ch. 14); Ni²⁺ with dimethylglyoxime in faintly ammoniacal solution
gives a rosy-red chelate precipitate; Ca²⁺ gives white calcium oxalate with ammonium
oxalate. Modern laboratories generate H₂S in situ from thioacetamide or avoid the classical
scheme altogether, but its solubility logic remains superb equilibrium teaching.

### Organic qualitative analysis: unsaturation and functional groups

**Unsaturation.** Shaking with bromine in CCl₄ discharges the orange colour when a C=C/C≡C
adds Br₂; cold dilute alkaline KMnO₄ (Baeyer's reagent) loses its purple colour to brown
MnO₂. Two cautions earn marks: benzene, with DU = 4, passes neither test (Ch. 17); and
phenol/aniline decolorize bromine *water* by electrophilic substitution, throwing a white
tribromo precipitate — evidence about the ring, not about an alkene.

| Suspected group | First-line test(s) | Positive sign |
|---|---|---|
| Carboxylic acid | NaHCO₃ solution | Brisk CO₂ effervescence (phenol fails) |
| Phenol | Neutral FeCl₃; bromine water | Violet colour; white 2,4,6-tribromophenol |
| Alcohol | Na metal (dry sample); Lucas reagent; oxidation behaviour | H₂ evolved; 3° > 2° > 1° cloudiness in Lucas; see Ch. 18 |
| Aldehyde | Tollens; Fehling; Schiff | Silver mirror; red Cu₂O (aliphatic); magenta restored |
| Ketone | 2,4-DNP positive but Tollens negative; NaHSO₃ for methyl ketones | Orange hydrazone without a mirror |
| CH₃CO– or CH₃CH(OH)– | I₂/NaOH, warm | Yellow CHI₃, antiseptic smell |
| Terminal alkyne | ammoniacal AgNO₃; ammoniacal Cu₂Cl₂ | White Ag acetylide; red Cu(I) acetylide |
| Primary amine | CHCl₃/alcoholic KOH; HNO₂ | Foul isocyanide; aliphatic N₂↑, aromatic diazonium coupling |
| Amide | Boil with NaOH | NH₃ evolved (damp red litmus → blue) |
| Ester | Hydroxamic-acid test (NH₂OH, then FeCl₃) | Wine-red/violet ferric hydroxamate; fruity smell supports |
| Alkyl halide | Warm with alcoholic AgNO₃; sodium fusion then AgNO₃ | AgX precipitate (reactive halides faster) |

High-yield **distinctions** are one-reagent decisions: ethanol vs methanol by iodoform
(ethanol +); propanal vs propanone by Tollens; pentan-2-one vs pentan-3-one by iodoform
(only the methyl ketone); formaldehyde vs acetaldehyde by iodoform (acetaldehyde +);
benzaldehyde vs acetophenone by Tollens; formic vs acetic acid by Tollens/KMnO₄ (formic
reduces); phenol vs benzoic acid by NaHCO₃ (acid fizzes); ethylamine vs aniline by cold
HNO₂ then β-naphthol (aniline couples to a dye; ethylamine just releases N₂); glucose vs
sucrose by Benedict before and after acid hydrolysis.

### Lassaigne's test: make covalent elements ionic

Nitrogen, sulfur and halogens locked in covalent molecules do not answer ionic reagents.
**Sodium fusion** solves the problem violently and elegantly: a small piece of sodium is
melted in an ignition tube, the organic compound added and heated to redness, and the hot
tube plunged into distilled water. Boiling and filtering gives the alkaline **sodium fusion
extract**, in which the elements now sit as ions:

Na + C + N → NaCN;  2Na + S → Na₂S;  Na + X → NaX;  and if N and S coexist with insufficient
carbon/sodium balance, NaSCN may form instead.

- **Nitrogen:** add fresh FeSO₄, boil, cool, then FeCl₃ and acidify. A **Prussian-blue**
  colour/precipitate, Fe₄[Fe(CN)₆]₃, confirms N. (Fe²⁺ first traps CN⁻ as ferrocyanide;
  Fe³⁺ then precipitates it.)
- **Sulfur:** sodium nitroprusside gives an intense **violet** thionitroprusside colour, or
  lead ethanoate in acetic acid gives black PbS.
- **Nitrogen + sulfur together:** FeCl₃ alone gives a **blood-red** thiocyanate colour —
  and the Prussian-blue test then fails unless excess sodium was used in fusion.
- **Halogens:** acidify a separate portion with dilute HNO₃ and **boil** to expel HCN/H₂S if
  N or S is present (otherwise AgCN/Ag₂S contaminate the result), cool and add AgNO₃. The
  AgX colours/solubilities match the inorganic table above. Fluorine escapes this test
  because AgF is soluble.

The test fails when the compound is too volatile, when nitrogen is present without carbon
(no CN⁻ can form, as in hydrazine), or when N₂ is lost from diazo-type compounds. Carbon and
hydrogen are detected separately by combustion with CuO: CO₂ turns lime water milky and
water turns white anhydrous CuSO₄ blue.

### Tests for carbohydrates, proteins and fats

| Test | Detects | Positive result / chemistry |
|---|---|---|
| Molisch (α-naphthol, then conc. H₂SO₄ down the wall) | Essentially all carbohydrates | Violet ring at the junction (furfural derivatives condense with α-naphthol) |
| Fehling/Benedict, heat | Reducing sugars (free anomeric carbon: glucose, fructose, maltose, lactose) | Blue Cu²⁺ → brick-red Cu₂O; sucrose negative until acid-hydrolysed |
| Barfoed (weakly acidic Cu²⁺) | Monosaccharides faster than disaccharides | Red Cu₂O within a couple of minutes |
| Seliwanoff (resorcinol/HCl) | Ketoses such as fructose | Rapid cherry-red colour |
| Iodine solution | Starch (amylose helix) | Blue-black complex; colour fades on heating, returns on cooling |
| Biuret (NaOH + very dilute CuSO₄) | Two or more peptide bonds (proteins/peptides) | Violet Cu²⁺–peptide complex; free amino acids essentially negative |
| Ninhydrin, heat | α-Amino acids (and protein N-termini) | Purple (Ruhemann) colour; proline gives yellow |
| Xanthoproteic (conc. HNO₃, then alkali) | Aromatic amino-acid residues | Yellow nitro products turning orange in alkali — the yellow stain when acid touches skin |
| Millon | Tyrosine phenolic group | Brick-red colour on warming |
| Grease spot; Sudan III/IV | Fats/oils | Translucent spot on paper; red-stained oil droplets |
| Acrolein (heat fat/glycerol with KHSO₄) | Glycerol backbone of fats | Pungent acrolein vapour (do this only as a demonstration) |


::: example Worked example — identifying four white powders with tests
Un-labelled bottles contain glucose, sucrose, starch and a protein (e.g. egg albumin). Design the identifications:

- **Iodine solution** → blue-black colour only with **starch**: identified first.
- Warm the others with **Fehling's/Benedict's solution** → brick-red precipitate only with **glucose** (a reducing sugar: free aldehyde group). **Sucrose** does not react — it is non-reducing because both anomeric carbons are locked in the glycosidic bond; boil it with dilute acid first (hydrolysis to glucose + fructose) and it then reduces Fehling's.
- **Biuret test** (NaOH + a drop of CuSO₄) → violet colour identifies the **protein** (peptide bonds).

The structure of every "identify" answer is the same: one test, one unique positive, one structural reason.
:::

::: caution Trap — indicator choice is decided by the salt formed
The equivalence-point pH decides the indicator: **strong acid–weak base** (acidic salt) → **methyl orange**; **weak acid–strong base** (basic salt) → **phenolphthalein**; **strong–strong** → either (steep jump covers both ranges). Weak acid–weak base has no sharp jump and *neither* indicator works well — the option students rarely expect.
:::

::: example A normality by the balance
**Problem.** A 20.0 mL portion of sodium hydroxide solution is exactly
neutralised by 40.0 mL of 0.1 N sulphuric acid. Find the normality of
the base, and name the indicator you would trust for this pair.
**Solution.** N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ gives N(base) × 20.0 = 0.1 × 40.0, so
N(base) = 4.0/20.0 = **0.2 N**. Strong acid against strong base ends at
pH 7, where **either phenolphthalein or methyl orange** changes within
the steep jump — the rare titration that forgives the indicator choice.
:::
## 22.2 Separation techniques: filtration, sublimation, evaporation, precipitation, crystallization, distillation, paper chromatography and atmolysis

Every separation buys purity by spending a **difference in a physical property**:

| Technique | Property exploited | Choose it when… | School example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Filtration (gravity/vacuum) | Particle size vs pore size | An insoluble solid must leave a liquid | Sand from salt solution; the filtrate still needs evaporation for the salt |
| Sublimation | A solid with high vapour pressure below its melting point | One solid sublimes, the rest does not | NH₄Cl, iodine, camphor or naphthalene away from sand/salt; collect on a cold surface |
| Evaporation | Solvent volatility | The dissolved solid is heat-stable and the solvent is not wanted | NaCl from seawater (identity yes, high purity no) |
| Crystallization | Solubility varies with temperature/solvent | A purer solid is required; impurities stay in mother liquor | Benzoic acid from hot water; impure CuSO₄ recrystallized. Fractional crystallization separates salts of differing solubility curves |
| Precipitation | Selective formation of a sparingly soluble compound | One ion/component can be thrown down quantitatively | BaSO₄ gravimetry; softening water by precipitating CaCO₃ |
| Simple distillation | Volatility/boiling point | Volatile liquid + non-volatile impurity, or boiling points far apart (roughly more than 25–30 K) | Pure water from salt water; ethanol from wash (partly) |
| Fractional distillation | Small boiling-point difference; repeated condense–revaporize in a column | Close-boiling miscible liquids | Benzene/toluene; petroleum fractions; ethanol–water stops at the 95.6% azeotrope |
| Steam distillation | Immiscible liquids boil when their vapour pressures sum to atmospheric | A heat-sensitive, water-immiscible organic must distil below 100 °C | Aniline, essential oils (eucalyptus, clove) |
| Vacuum distillation | Lowering external pressure lowers boiling point | High-boiling or heat-sensitive liquids | Glycerol purification; vacuum evaporation of milk/sugar liquors |
| Paper chromatography | Partition between water held on cellulose (stationary) and a moving solvent | Tiny, coloured or colourless mixtures must be fingerprinted | Amino acids (ninhydrin spray), plant pigments, ink dyes |
| Atmolysis | Graham's law: diffusion/effusion rate ∝ 1/√M through a porous barrier | Gases of different molar mass, classically isotopes | H₂ escapes 4× faster than O₂; historical UF₆ isotope enrichment |

In **paper chromatography** the sample is spotted on a pencil baseline above the solvent
level (ink would run; submerging the spot would wash it away). As solvent climbs by
capillarity, components partition repeatedly and travel different distances. The retardation
factor,

$$
R_f=\frac{\text{distance moved by spot}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front}},
$$

is characteristic only for the stated paper, solvent, temperature and saturation — which is
why standards run on the same sheet convict or acquit an unknown. Colourless spots are
located with UV light, iodine vapour or a spray reagent.

**Atmolysis** deserves its one calculation: at the same temperature and pressure, Graham's
law gives rate(H₂)/rate(O₂) = √(32/2) = 4. A porous pot therefore enriches the faster,
lighter gas on the far side; repeating the stage multiplies a tiny enrichment — historically
the hard road to ²³⁵UF₆/²³⁸UF₆ separation, now largely replaced by gas centrifuges.

## 22.3 Acid–base, redox and complexometric titrations; choice of indicator

A titration delivers a standard solution (the **titrant**, in the burette) to a measured
volume of analyte until the **equivalence point** — exact stoichiometric consumption. The
**end point** is what the indicator shows; good method design makes the two coincide within a
drop. A **primary standard** can be weighed and trusted (pure, stable, non-hygroscopic,
ideally high molar mass: anhydrous Na₂CO₃, oxalic acid, potassium hydrogen phthalate,
K₂Cr₂O₇). NaOH, KMnO₄ and Na₂S₂O₃ solutions are secondary standards and must be standardized.
At equivalence, equivalents of titrant = equivalents of analyte (N₁V₁ = N₂V₂), or work in
moles with the equation ratio — the safer modern habit.

### Acid–base indicators follow the equivalence pH

| Titration | Salt at equivalence | Equivalence pH | Indicator (range, colour change) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strong acid–strong base | Neutral | 7, with a huge vertical jump | Phenolphthalein (8.2–10, colourless→pink) or methyl orange (3.1–4.4, red→yellow/orange end point) |
| Strong acid–weak base (for example HCl vs NH₄OH) | Acidic (NH₄⁺ hydrolyses) | About 5 | Methyl orange or methyl red (4.2–6.3) |
| Weak acid–strong base (CH₃CO₂H vs NaOH) | Basic (CH₃CO₂⁻ hydrolyses) | About 8.7 | Phenolphthalein |
| Weak acid–weak base | Depends on Kₐ/K_b | No steep jump | No satisfactory single visual indicator |

Sodium carbonate titrated with HCl shows **two** end points: phenolphthalein fades when
CO₃²⁻ has become HCO₃⁻ (half-neutralization), and methyl orange then marks HCO₃⁻ → CO₂ + H₂O.
Comparing the two volumes analyses NaOH/Na₂CO₃ mixtures — an old examination favourite built
from Ch. 7's hydrolysis.

### Permanganometric titrations

In acid, permanganate is a five-electron oxidant:

MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O.

Its own purple colour is the indicator: the end point is the first permanent pale pink that
survives about 30 seconds of swirling. The medium must be dilute **H₂SO₄** — HCl would itself
be oxidized to Cl₂, and HNO₃ is an oxidant in its own right. Oxalate titrations are run at
60–70 °C and start slowly until Mn²⁺ autocatalyses them:

2KMnO₄ + 5H₂C₂O₄ + 3H₂SO₄ → K₂SO₄ + 2MnSO₄ + 10CO₂ + 8H₂O.

Fe²⁺, oxalate and H₂O₂ are standard analytes; KMnO₄ is standardized against sodium oxalate or
oxalic acid. Read the **upper** meniscus of the dark solution, and remember that in neutral
or alkaline solution permanganate stops at MnO₂ — a different electron count and a different
equivalent weight.

::: example Worked example 22.1 — standardizing KMnO₄ in moles, not memory
**Problem.** 25.0 mL of 0.0500 mol L⁻¹ oxalic acid required 20.0 mL of acidified KMnO₄.
Find the KMnO₄ concentration.

**Solution.** Moles of oxalic acid = 0.0250 × 0.0500 = 1.25×10⁻³ mol. The equation ratio is
2 MnO₄⁻ : 5 H₂C₂O₄, so moles of KMnO₄ = (2/5)(1.25×10⁻³) = 5.00×10⁻⁴ mol.
Concentration = 5.00×10⁻⁴ / 0.0200 = **0.0250 mol L⁻¹**. Writing the balanced molecular
equation first makes normality arithmetic unnecessary.
:::

### Iodimetry versus iodometry — similar names, opposite directions

**Iodimetry (direct)** titrates a reducing analyte with a standard iodine solution; starch
turns blue at the first excess of I₂. Vitamin C and sulfite are classic analytes.
**Iodometry (indirect)** is far commoner: an oxidizing analyte liberates I₂ from excess KI,
and that iodine is titrated with standard sodium thiosulfate,

I₂ + 2S₂O₃²⁻ → 2I⁻ + S₄O₆²⁻.

Copper estimation is the model: 2Cu²⁺ + 4I⁻ → 2CuI↓ + I₂; the I₂ found equals the Cu²⁺ taken,
one-to-one in moles of electrons. Dichromate, chlorine/bleaching powder and dissolved oxygen
(Winkler method) are handled the same way. Add starch only when the iodine colour has faded
to pale straw: added early, the starch–iodine complex forms too strongly/adsorbs I₂ and the
end point drags and overshoots. The end point is blue → colourless; in copper work a little
thiocyanate near the end frees iodine adsorbed on CuI.

### Complexometric (EDTA) titrations

EDTA (used as its disodium salt) is a hexadentate chelator that binds metal ions in a strict
**1:1 ratio** regardless of the metal's charge: M²⁺ + H₂Y²⁻ → MY²⁻ + 2H⁺. Because protons are
released, the solution is buffered. At pH 10 (NH₃/NH₄Cl buffer) with **Eriochrome Black T**,
Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ first hold the indicator in a wine-red complex; EDTA strips the metals away and the
free indicator turns **blue** — the standard total-hardness titration. At pH about 12,
Mg(OH)₂ precipitates and murexide lets Ca²⁺ alone be found; Mg²⁺ follows by difference.
Interfering Fe³⁺/Cu²⁺ can be masked (triethanolamine, cyanide where permitted) — masking is
selectivity by competitive complexation. Results are conventionally reported as mg L⁻¹ of
equivalent CaCO₃. The same chelation principle, scaled up, is water softening, detergent
building and the lead-poisoning therapy of Chapters 14–15.

::: caution
Five traps in the analytical room: **limewater turns milky and then
clear again in excess CO₂**, the clearing as telling as the cloud;
**group II runs H₂S in acid and group IV in alkaline solution** — the
same gas, opposite pH, different families; **Lassaigne's blood red means
nitrogen and sulphur together**, not sulphur alone; **iodometric
titrations add starch near the end point**, not at the start, lest the
complex trap the iodine; and **phenolphthalein is colourless in acid and
pink in base** — the pink appears on the basic side of the jump.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Anion analysis begins with acid-evolved gases and ends with precipitate confirmations whose acid/ammonia solubilities separate look-alikes.
- Cation groups are solubility cliffs climbed in order; group reagents work because pH controls S²⁻, OH⁻ and CO₃²⁻ availability.
- Organic tests are the functional-group reactions of Chapters 17–20 read backwards from product to structure, with benzene and phenol as the standing bromine-test traps.
- Lassaigne's fusion converts covalent N, S and halogens into CN⁻, S²⁻ and X⁻ so inorganic colour chemistry can see them.
- Molisch, Benedict, iodine, Biuret, ninhydrin and grease-spot tests identify the three great food classes by their reactive groups.
- Separation choice is property choice: volatility for distillation, solubility for crystallization, partition for chromatography and √M for atmolysis.
- An indicator is chosen from the pH of the salt at equivalence, not from habit; the end point is an observation that must be engineered onto the equivalence point.
- Permanganate counts five electrons in acid and announces itself; iodine methods split into direct iodimetry and indirect iodometry with late starch.
- EDTA's 1:1 chelation plus a metal-sensitive dye turns water hardness into a clean wine-red-to-blue measurement.
