This short chapter carries **about 2 questions**, but it joins the periodic table directly
to physiology and medicine. The same principles used throughout inorganic chemistry —
oxidation state, ligand binding, ionic size, solubility and redox reactivity — decide why a
metal ion can be an enzyme cofactor at one dose and a poison at another. Learn each element
through four hooks: **where it is concentrated, what molecule or enzyme uses it, what breaks
in deficiency, and what breaks in excess**. That pattern will also serve you in Zoology,
physiology and pharmacology.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- **Macrominerals** are needed in relatively large daily amounts. Among the elements in this chapter: Na, K, Mg and Ca. **Microminerals/trace elements** here are Zn, Cu, Co, Ni, Fe and Cr.
- Na⁺ is the dominant **extracellular** cation; K⁺ is the dominant **intracellular** cation. Their gradients control fluid volume, membrane potential and excitability.
- Mg²⁺ is the central ion of chlorophyll and the usual partner of ATP in cells (MgATP). Ca²⁺ builds bone mineral and also acts in clotting, muscle contraction, secretion and signalling.
- Zn²⁺ is a redox-inert Lewis acid in carbonic anhydrase, carboxypeptidase and zinc-finger proteins. Cu and Fe are useful because they change oxidation state readily; the same redox activity makes free Fe/Cu dangerous.
- Cobalt is biologically useful to humans as the centre of **vitamin B₁₂ (cobalamin)**; inorganic cobalt in food cannot be converted by human cells into B₁₂.
- Nickel is a true cofactor in plant and microbial enzymes such as urease, but a specific essential biochemical function in humans is not yet established. Cr(III) was traditionally linked with insulin action, but modern reviews also find human essentiality unproved; Cr(VI), by contrast, is highly toxic and carcinogenic.
- The **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** uses one ATP to move **3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in**. It is primary active transport and is inhibited by ouabain and cardiac glycosides such as digoxin.
- A sodium–glucose transporter (SGLT) is not an ATP-burning pump. It is a **secondary-active Na⁺ symporter**: intestinal/renal SGLT1 generally couples **2 Na⁺ to 1 glucose**, whereas early proximal-tubule SGLT2 couples **1 Na⁺ to 1 glucose**. Oral rehydration solution exploits intestinal SGLT1.
- Toxic metals often work by **ionic mimicry and thiol binding**: arsenate imitates phosphate; As(III), Hg and Pb bind –SH groups; Pb²⁺ imitates Ca²⁺/Zn²⁺; Cd²⁺ imitates Zn²⁺/Ca²⁺.
- Disease hooks: iron overload → liver, heart and endocrine damage by hydroxyl-radical chemistry; arsenic → skin changes, neuropathy and cancers; methylmercury → Minamata-type brain injury; lead → impaired neurodevelopment and haem synthesis; cadmium → renal tubular injury and painful bone disease (Itai-itai).
:::

## 15.1 Micro- and macronutrients and the biological roles of metal ions

An **essential element** is not merely an element found in tissue. Strictly, removing it
should reproducibly impair a biological function, restoring it should repair that function,
and ideally a direct biochemical role should be known. This strict definition is why the
status of chromium and nickel in human nutrition remains more cautious than in many older
textbooks.

Mineral nutrients are often divided by the amount required. **Macrominerals** are needed at
roughly hundreds of milligrams or more per day; **microminerals** are needed in milligram or
microgram amounts. The division says nothing about importance: a trace of cobalt inside
vitamin B₁₂ is indispensable for blood formation, while a tiny excess of potassium in
plasma can stop the heart. Biology also cares about **speciation** — the exact chemical
form. Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ differ in transport, Cr(III) and Cr(VI) differ dramatically in hazard,
and methylmercury crosses membranes far more effectively than inorganic mercury salts.

Metal ions perform four recurring chemical jobs. First, they provide **charge and osmotic
balance** (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺). Second, they act as **Lewis acids** that polarize bonds in
enzyme active sites, especially Zn²⁺ and Mg²⁺. Third, Fe and Cu perform **electron transfer
and oxygen chemistry** because neighbouring oxidation states are accessible. Fourth, ions can
be **structural or signalling agents**: Ca²⁺ in bone and cytosolic signals, Zn²⁺ in
zinc-finger domains that hold transcription factors in the correct shape for DNA binding.

| Element | Chemical/biological role | Deficiency or imbalance hook |
|---|---|---|
| **Sodium (Na⁺)** | Main extracellular cation; sets effective plasma osmolality and extracellular-fluid volume; Na⁺ influx produces the upstroke of many action potentials; its gradient drives nutrient and ion cotransport | Losses with vomiting, diarrhoea or diuretics cause volume depletion; abnormal plasma Na⁺ mainly signals water imbalance and can produce confusion, seizures or coma |
| **Potassium (K⁺)** | Main intracellular cation; K⁺ leak through membrane channels is the main immediate reason the resting membrane is negative inside; needed for nerve conduction, muscle contraction and several enzymes, including pyruvate kinase | Hypokalaemia causes weakness and cardiac arrhythmias; hyperkalaemia is an emergency because cardiac excitability and conduction fail |
| **Magnesium (Mg²⁺)** | Cofactor in hundreds of reactions; most intracellular ATP is present as MgATP; stabilizes DNA, RNA and ribosomes; required by kinases and by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase; central atom of chlorophyll in plants | Deficiency gives tremor, cramps, arrhythmias and resistant K⁺/Ca²⁺ abnormalities; most body Mg is in bone and cells, so a normal plasma value can hide depletion |
| **Calcium (Ca²⁺)** | About 99% is in bone and teeth as hydroxyapatite, Ca₁₀(PO₄)₆(OH)₂; ionized Ca²⁺ is clotting factor IV, triggers neurotransmitter and hormone release, enables troponin-mediated muscle contraction and binds calmodulin in signalling | Childhood deficiency contributes to rickets; adult bone loss gives osteomalacia/osteoporosis; acute hypocalcaemia increases excitability and can cause tetany |

| **Cobalt (Co)** | Central ion of cobalamin (vitamin B₁₂). | **Nickel (Ni)** | Established cofactor in microbial/plant urease and several hydrogenases; occurs in human tissues and may influence iron handling, but no definite human Ni-dependent enzyme is known | No recognized human nickel-deficiency syndrome. | **Iron (Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺)** | In haemoglobin, myoglobin, cytochromes and catalase; in iron–sulfur clusters of respiratory-chain proteins; and in ribonucleotide reductase for DNA synthesis. The iron and copper rows reveal the chapter's central chemical bargain. A redox-active metal
is an excellent catalyst, but the cell must never leave much of it “free”. Iron is therefore
handed between proteins and locked in ferritin; copper is escorted by chaperones. Zinc can
occupy delicate structural sites more safely because Zn²⁺ has a filled 3d¹⁰ subshell and does
not cycle between oxidation states under biological conditions.

Absorption is controlled most tightly for iron because humans have no regulated pathway for
excreting a large iron excess. In the duodenum, Fe³⁺ is reduced to Fe²⁺ for uptake; iron then
leaves intestinal and storage cells through **ferroportin**. The liver hormone **hepcidin**
blocks ferroportin when iron is plentiful or infection is present. This is elegant
bio-inorganic control: the body regulates the gate, not the drain.

::: caution “Present in the body” does not prove “essential”
Nickel and chromium illustrate why examination answers should follow established biochemical
roles rather than supplement advertisements. Nickel unquestionably serves in plant and
microbial urease, and chromium(III) has been linked experimentally with carbohydrate
metabolism, but neither has a firmly demonstrated, specific essential function in humans.
That caution does not make their toxic forms safe: nickel allergy and carcinogenic
chromium(VI) are real occupational hazards.
:::

::: example The pump's hourly bill
**Problem.** A resting nerve cell hydrolyses 1.0 × 10⁹ ATP molecules per
minute on its Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase alone. How many sodium ions leave and
potassium ions enter per minute, and what net charge crosses outward?
**Solution.** Per ATP: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in. Sodium out = 3 × 10⁹ per
minute; potassium in = 2 × 10⁹ per minute; net outward charge = (3 − 2) ×
10⁹ = **1.0 × 10⁹ positive charges per minute** — the electrogenic leak
the resting potential leans on. One protein, three-to-two bookkeeping, a
billion times a minute: bio-inorganic arithmetic in miniature.
:::
## 15.2 The sodium–potassium pump and sodium–glucose cotransport

Almost every animal cell keeps Na⁺ high outside and K⁺ high inside. Typical plasma Na⁺ is
near 145 mmol L⁻¹ while cytosolic Na⁺ is only about 10–15 mmol L⁻¹; cytosolic K⁺ is about
140 mmol L⁻¹ while plasma K⁺ is near 4–5 mmol L⁻¹. These gradients are batteries. They store
energy that powers electrical signalling, cell-volume control, pH regulation, Ca²⁺ handling
and the uptake of glucose and amino acids.

### The Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase: primary active transport

The **sodium–potassium pump** is a P-type ATPase embedded in the plasma membrane. In each
cycle it exports **three Na⁺ ions**, imports **two K⁺ ions** and hydrolyses **one ATP**.
The cycle can be remembered as a two-door airlock:

1. With the transporter open to the cytosol, 3 Na⁺ bind.
2. ATP phosphorylates the pump; its shape changes and Na⁺ is released outside.
3. In the outward-facing form, 2 K⁺ bind.
4. Loss of phosphate returns the pump to the inward-facing shape, releasing K⁺ into the cytosol.

Because three positive charges leave for every two that enter, the pump is slightly
**electrogenic**: it contributes a few millivolts to the negative interior. Its larger role is
indirect — it maintains the K⁺ gradient, and K⁺ leaking out through resting K⁺ channels makes
the inside much more negative. The pump also keeps cells from swelling: impermeant proteins
inside the cell attract ions and water, but continuous Na⁺ export balances that osmotic pull.

**Ouabain** and the cardiac glycosides **digoxin/digitoxin** inhibit Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase from the
extracellular side. In heart muscle, partial inhibition raises cytosolic Na⁺ slightly. The
Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger then expels less Ca²⁺, so more Ca²⁺ is available for contraction. Too much
inhibition disturbs rhythm — a narrow boundary between drug and poison. Hypokalaemia increases
digoxin toxicity because K⁺ normally competes at the pump's extracellular side.

### Sodium–glucose cotransport: secondary active transport

A **sodium–glucose cotransporter (SGLT)** uses the downhill entry of Na⁺ to drag glucose
uphill into a cell. It does not hydrolyse ATP itself. The energy bill is paid afterwards by
Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, which pumps the entering Na⁺ back out. Thus SGLT is **secondary active
transport**, whereas the Na⁺/K⁺ pump is **primary active transport**.

| Feature | SGLT1 | SGLT2 |
|---|---|---|
| Main sites | Small-intestinal brush border; later proximal renal tubule (S3) | Early proximal renal tubule (S1/S2) |
| Coupling | 2 Na⁺ : 1 glucose | 1 Na⁺ : 1 glucose |
| Character | High affinity, lower capacity; also transports galactose | Lower affinity, high capacity; responsible for most renal glucose reabsorption |
| Clinical hook | Basis of glucose-containing oral rehydration solution; genetic loss causes glucose–galactose malabsorption | Blocked by gliflozin drugs, increasing urinary glucose excretion in type 2 diabetes |

After glucose enters an intestinal or renal epithelial cell through apical SGLT, it leaves
across the basolateral membrane mainly through a facilitative GLUT transporter. Water follows
the absorbed solute osmotically. This explains a life-saving treatment developed from pure
transport chemistry: in cholera and other secretory diarrhoeas, the intestine secretes
profusely, but SGLT1 remains functional. A correctly balanced solution containing glucose and
sodium therefore pulls Na⁺ and water back into the body. Plain glucose without salts, or a
very sugary drink, does not reproduce that effect and may worsen osmotic loss.

::: example Worked example 15.1 — who pays the ATP bill?
**Problem.** An intestinal cell takes up 300 glucose molecules through SGLT1. Assuming every
co-transported Na⁺ is subsequently exported by Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, how many ATP molecules are used
by the pump, and how many K⁺ ions enter during those pump cycles?

**Solution.** SGLT1 couples 2 Na⁺ to each glucose, so Na⁺ entry is
300 × 2 = 600 Na⁺. One pump cycle exports 3 Na⁺, requiring 600 ÷ 3 = 200 cycles and therefore
**200 ATP**. Each cycle imports 2 K⁺, so K⁺ entry is 200 × 2 = **400 K⁺**. SGLT itself uses no
ATP; Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pays to restore the gradient.
:::

::: tip A two-line memory lock
**“3–2–1 pump; 2–1 gut.”** The pump moves 3 Na⁺ and 2 K⁺ per 1 ATP; intestinal SGLT1 moves
2 Na⁺ with 1 glucose. Renal SGLT2 is the exception to complete: 1 Na⁺ with 1 glucose.
:::

## 15.3 Toxicity of iron, arsenic, mercury, lead and cadmium

Toxicity is governed by dose, chemical form, route, age and nutritional state. A useful
mechanistic map has four roads to injury. **Ionic mimicry** lets a toxic ion use another
ion's transporter or binding site. **Thiol binding** disables enzymes whose cysteine –SH
groups are chemically vital. **Redox cycling** generates reactive oxygen species. Finally,
**storage and biomagnification** make exposure last: lead and cadmium are warehoused in the
body, while methylmercury becomes more concentrated at higher levels of an aquatic food chain.

Chelation therapy follows the same coordination chemistry. A multidentate ligand wraps a
metal ion and forms a more excretable complex. It is never a general “detox”: the ligand may
also remove essential metals or redistribute a toxic metal to a more sensitive organ. The
first treatment is always to identify and stop exposure, with urgent supportive and
specialist toxicology care.

### Iron: essential redox power without a safety valve

Iron overload occurs through inherited increases in absorption (notably HFE-related
haemochromatosis), repeated blood transfusion, or acute ingestion — classically a child eating
adult iron tablets. Once transferrin becomes highly saturated, poorly controlled
non-transferrin-bound iron enters liver, heart and endocrine cells. Inside cells, ferrous iron
can drive the **Fenton reaction**:

Fe²⁺ + H₂O₂ → Fe³⁺ + OH⁻ + •OH

The hydroxyl radical (•OH) attacks lipids, proteins and DNA almost where it is formed.
Chronic injury produces hepatic fibrosis/cirrhosis, diabetes, skin pigmentation,
cardiomyopathy, arthropathy and hypogonadism. Hereditary overload is commonly treated by
removing blood (venesection), which forces stored iron into new red cells; transfusion overload
requires iron chelators such as deferoxamine or oral agents. Acute poisoning begins with
corrosive gastrointestinal injury and may progress to shock, acidosis and liver failure.

### Arsenic: phosphate mimic and thiol poison

Arsenic is a metalloid whose inorganic forms occur naturally in some groundwater, including
parts of Nepal's Terai aquifers. The WHO provisional drinking-water guideline is 10 µg L⁻¹;
concentrations above a national operational limit call for an alternative source or effective
removal, not reassurance that “a little poison is a nutrient”.

- **Arsenate, As(V)**, resembles phosphate. It can substitute in phosphorylation reactions, but the arsenate esters hydrolyse readily, wasting metabolic energy (arsenolysis).
- **Arsenite, As(III)**, binds paired thiol groups, notably lipoamide in pyruvate dehydrogenase and α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase. Blocking these gateways restricts aerobic ATP production.

Acute poisoning causes severe vomiting, abdominal pain, watery diarrhoea and shock. Chronic
exposure is associated with mottled pigmentation and thickened palms/soles
(hyperkeratosis), peripheral neuropathy, vascular disease and cancers of skin, bladder and
lung. Dimercaprol (BAL) was historically designed around arsenic's thiol chemistry; water-soluble
chelators such as succimer (DMSA) or DMPS may be used in selected cases. Public-health control
— testing wells and switching to safe water — prevents far more injury than late chelation.

### Mercury: the form picks the target organ

Metallic mercury liquid is poorly absorbed from a healthy gut, but its invisible **vapour** is
readily inhaled, crosses the blood–brain barrier and is oxidized and trapped in the nervous
system. Chronic vapour poisoning classically gives tremor, gum and kidney problems and
erethism — irritability, shyness and emotional instability. Inorganic mercury salts such as
HgCl₂ are corrosive and especially injure gut and kidney.

Microorganisms in aquatic sediments convert inorganic mercury to **methylmercury**, which binds
protein thiols, enters food chains and **biomagnifies** in predatory fish. A methylmercury–
cysteine complex resembles methionine closely enough to be carried across the blood–brain and
placental barriers. The Minamata disaster in Japan showed the result: numbness, unsteady gait,
constricted visual fields, speech and hearing disturbance, and devastating fetal brain injury.
“Mercury poisoning” is therefore incomplete as an examination phrase unless the chemical form is
specified.

### Lead: a calcium/zinc impostor with no safe childhood threshold

Pb²⁺ exploits pathways meant for Ca²⁺, Fe²⁺ and Zn²⁺ and binds thiol and other ligand groups
in proteins. It inhibits **δ-aminolaevulinic acid dehydratase** and **ferrochelatase** in haem
synthesis, contributing to anaemia and basophilic stippling of red cells. More importantly, lead
disturbs neuronal development, synaptic signalling and behaviour. No safe blood-lead
concentration has been identified for children; a reference value is a screening statistic,
not a biological safety line.

Sources include deteriorating lead paint, battery recycling, contaminated dust and soil, some
traditional remedies/cosmetics, industrial emissions and plumbing components. Gastrointestinal
absorption is greater in children and increases when iron or calcium nutrition is poor. Lead is
stored for years in bone and can be released during pregnancy, lactation or bone loss. Clinical
hooks include developmental delay, abdominal colic, constipation, kidney injury, hypertension,
peripheral neuropathy such as wrist drop and, at very high exposure, encephalopathy. Severe cases
may receive CaNa₂EDTA, succimer or dimercaprol combinations under specialist direction; removing
the source is indispensable because chelation cannot undo established neurodevelopmental injury.

### Cadmium: the kidney's long memory

Cadmium enters through tobacco smoke, contaminated rice and other foods, phosphate-fertilizer
impurities, nickel–cadmium batteries, pigments and metal work. In blood and liver it induces
and binds **metallothionein**. The small Cd–metallothionein complex is filtered by the kidney
and taken up by proximal tubules, where cadmium accumulates with a biological half-life measured
in decades. Tubular injury leaks low-molecular-mass proteins and wastes phosphate and calcium.
Bone then demineralizes, producing osteomalacia/osteoporosis; severe environmental poisoning in
Japan was named **Itai-itai** (“ouch-ouch”) disease because of intense bone pain and fractures.
Inhaled cadmium compounds are also lung carcinogens. Unlike acute lead or arsenic poisoning,
routine chelation is generally not the answer for chronic cadmium burden because mobilized
cadmium may increase renal exposure; prevention and exposure removal dominate management.

::: caution
Five swaps the paper sets: **Na⁺ is the extracellular cation and K⁺ the
intracellular one** — the pump maintains, never reverses, that order;
**the Na⁺/K⁺ pump is primary active (spends ATP) while SGLT is secondary

**chelation matches the metal: EDTA for lead, BAL or penicillamine for
arsenic and mercury**, never interchangeably.

::: example Stoichiometry and ATP budget of the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase pump
**Problem.** A resting neuron hydrolyses $2.0 \times 10^6$ ATP molecules via its plasma-membrane $\text{Na}^+/\text{K}^+$ pump. How many $\text{Na}^+$ ions are extruded, how many $\text{K}^+$ ions are imported, and what is the net charge moved out of the cell?

**Solution.** Each ATP exports $3\text{ Na}^+$ and imports $2\text{ K}^+$, moving $+1$ net elementary charge outward. Thus $6.0 \times 10^6\text{ Na}^+$ leave, $4.0 \times 10^6\text{ K}^+$ enter, and $+2.0 \times 10^6\,e$ of net positive charge is pumped out (making the interior negative).
:::

## Chapter summary

- Macro- and microminerals differ in required amount, not in importance; chemical form and dose decide benefit or harm.
- Na⁺ dominates extracellular fluid, K⁺ dominates cytosol, Mg²⁺ partners ATP and chlorophyll, and Ca²⁺ combines a structural role in bone with rapid signalling roles.
- Zn²⁺ is a redox-inert catalytic/structural ion; Fe and Cu handle oxygen and electrons but must be protein-bound; cobalt reaches human metabolism through microbially made vitamin B₁₂.
- Nickel has established roles in plants and microbes, while chromium(III)'s supposed essential role in humans remains unproved; chromium(VI) is an important carcinogen.
- Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase is the primary-active 3-out/2-in pump. SGLTs spend the Na⁺ gradient, not ATP directly, to import glucose.
- Oral rehydration works because intestinal SGLT1 links glucose uptake to Na⁺ and water absorption.
- Iron overload produces hydroxyl radicals; arsenic blocks thiol enzymes and imitates phosphate; organic mercury biomagnifies and crosses into brain and fetus.
- Lead injures the developing nervous system without an identified safe childhood threshold, while cadmium accumulates in kidney and secondarily destroys bone.
