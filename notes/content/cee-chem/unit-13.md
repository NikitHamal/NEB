This is one of the two heavyweight inorganic chapters — **about 4 questions** — and it is
unashamedly factual: preparations, properties, tests and *which reagent does what to whom*.
The winning strategy is to file each species under three hooks — **how it is made, what
kind of agent it is (oxidant, reductant, acid, dehydrator…), and its one diagnostic test** —
and to know the trends that bind families (halogen reactivity falls down the group, HX
acidity rises). Every equation below is balanced; in the examination hall, write the same
way: charge and atoms both counted.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- **Hydrogen:** nascent [H] (freshly generated, e.g. Zn + HCl) reduces more strongly than molecular H₂; atomic H from an electric arc is fiercer still (atomic hydrogen torch). Isotopes: ¹H (protium), ²H/D (deuterium), ³H/T (tritium, β⁻, t½ 12.3 y). D₂O (heavy water): denser (1.106 g cm⁻³), b.p. 101.4 °C — a neutron **moderator** and tracer.
- **Oxides:** acidic (CO₂, SO₂, NO₂), basic (Na₂O, CaO), amphoteric (Al₂O₃, ZnO), neutral (CO, NO, N₂O, H₂O), peroxides (Na₂O₂: O at −1), superoxides (KO₂: −½), mixed (Fe₃O₄, Pb₃O₄).
- **Ozone:** 3O₂ ⇌ 2O₃ by **silent electric discharge**; bent (116.8°), resonance-equal bonds, strong oxidant (starch–iodide turns blue; Hg loses its meniscus — "tailing of mercury"). CFC chlorine radicals catalytically destroy stratospheric O₃ → UV shield thinning (Montreal Protocol 1987).
- **NH₃:** Haber (450 °C, 200 atm, Fe/Mo); pyramidal lone-pair base & ligand ([Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ deep blue); Nessler's test (brown). **PH₃:** Ca₃P₂ + water; rotten-fish, poisonous, far less basic/soluble than NH₃.
- **HNO₃:** Ostwald (NH₃/Pt gauze → NO → NO₂ → absorb); oxidizing acid — Cu + dil. → NO, Cu + conc. → NO₂; Fe/Al go **passive** in conc. HNO₃; aqua regia (1 HNO₃ : 3 HCl) dissolves Au/Pt; brown-ring test for NO₃⁻.
- **Halogens:** reactivity F > Cl > Br > I; colours pale-green/red-brown/violet; each higher halogen displaces lower halides. HX acidity HF ≪ HCl < HBr < HI; reducing power runs the other way; HF etches glass (→ SiF₄).
- **Carbon:** diamond (sp³, hardest, insulator), graphite (sp² layers, conductor, lubricant), fullerene C₆₀, graphene. **CO:** from formic acid + conc. H₂SO₄; neutral oxide, strong reductant, binds haemoglobin ~250× O₂ (poison); water gas = CO + H₂, producer gas = CO + N₂.
- **H₂S:** FeS + dil. H₂SO₄ (Kipp); rotten eggs, weak dibasic acid, reductant; blackens lead-acetate paper; the group-analysis precipitant (Ch. 22). **SO₂:** acidic oxide, reducing bleach (temporary!), turns acidified dichromate **green**. **H₂SO₄:** Contact process (V₂O₅, oleum), dehydrating + oxidizing (hot conc.) — "king of chemicals".
:::

## 13.1 Atomic, molecular and nascent hydrogen; isotopes; heavy water; types of oxides

Ordinary hydrogen is the **molecular** diatomic gas. Two more reactive forms are exam
staples: **nascent hydrogen** [H], the just-born atoms liberated *in situ* by a reaction
such as Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + 2[H], which reduce things (e.g. acidified KMnO₄, Fe³⁺ salts,
KClO₃) that bubbling molecular H₂ through the same solution leaves untouched; and
**atomic hydrogen**, made by passing H₂ through an electric arc — its atoms recombine on
a surface releasing so much heat (≈ 4000 °C, the atomic hydrogen torch for welding
refractory metals) and it is the strongest reducing form of all:
reducing power: atomic H > nascent [H] > molecular H₂. (Modern chemistry treats
"nascent" effects as adsorbed hydrogen atoms/electron transfer at the metal surface —
the exam keeps the classical name and ranking.)

| Isotope | Nucleus | Abundance/behaviour | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Protium ¹H | 1 p | 99.985 % | — |
| Deuterium ²H (D) | 1 p + 1 n | 0.015 %; stable | D₂O, tracers, NMR solvents, fusion fuel |
| Tritium ³H (T) | 1 p + 2 n | radioactive β⁻, t½ 12.3 y | luminous signs, biological labelling (Ch. 12) |

**Heavy water, D₂O**, is obtained in practice by prolonged electrolysis of ordinary water
(containing alkali) — H₂ is evolved preferentially, concentrating D₂O behind — or by the
Girdler-sulfide exchange industrially. Its molecules are heavier, so its constants crawl
up (m.p. 3.8 °C, b.p. 101.4 °C, density 1.106 g cm⁻³, maximum density at 11.6 °C) and its
reactions run slower; organisms tolerate only dilution (high fractions are toxic because
enzyme kinetics and mitotic spindles depend on those very rates). Its headline job is the
**neutron moderator** in CANDU-type reactors, where its low neutron absorption lets
natural (unenriched) uranium burn.

**Types of oxides** (memory scheme: the partner's character decides):

| Type | Examples | Behaviour/test |
|---|---|---|
| Acidic (mostly non-metals, high oxidation states) | CO₂, SO₂, SO₃, NO₂, P₂O₅, CrO₃ | turn moist litmus red; +base → salt + water; many are acid anhydrides |
| Basic (metals) | Na₂O, CaO, MgO, CuO | +acid → salt + water |
| Amphoteric | Al₂O₃, ZnO, PbO, SnO | react with both acid and alkali |
| Neutral | CO, NO, N₂O, H₂O | neither acidic nor basic to litmus |
| Peroxides (O = −1) | Na₂O₂, BaO₂, H₂O₂ | give H₂O₂ with dilute acid |
| Superoxides (O = −½) | KO₂, RbO₂ | large alkali metals in excess O₂; used to regenerate O₂ in spacecraft/submarines |
| Mixed | Fe₃O₄ (FeO·Fe₂O₃), Pb₃O₄ (2PbO·PbO₂) | two formal oxides in one lattice |

::: example Volumes of ozone and oxygen
**Problem.** 90 mL of oxygen is converted to ozone in a silent discharge,
and the conversion reaches 60 % by volume of oxygen consumed. Later the
ozone fully decomposes. What volumes of ozone form and of oxygen return?
**Solution.** Ozonisation runs 3O₂ → 2O₃: 60 % of 90 mL = 54 mL of O₂
reacts, giving 2/3 × 54 = **36 mL of O₃** (with 36 mL O₂ left over,
72 mL total gas). Decomposition runs 2O₃ → 3O₂: 36 mL of O₃ returns
3/2 × 36 = **54 mL of O₂**, restoring the original 90 mL. Volume
arithmetic of gases is stoichiometry at constant T and P — Gay-Lussac
doing the counting.
:::
## 13.2 Ozone: preparation, structure and tests; depletion of the ozone layer

**Preparation.** A slow, silent (sparkless) electric discharge through dry oxygen in an
ozonizer (Siemens/Brodie) gives up to ~10 % of the pale-blue, pungent gas:

3O₂ ⇌ 2O₃  (ΔH = +285 kJ — endothermic; heat destroys the product, hence the silent, cold discharge)

Pure ozone is condensed out as a deep-blue, dangerously explosive liquid. **Structure:**
the molecule is **bent** (bond angle 116.8°, O–O 127 pm — between single (148) and double
(121)), a resonance hybrid of two forms with an sp² central oxygen carrying a lone pair;
the delocalized π system makes both bonds equal (bond order 1.5). **Properties:** a
powerful oxidant — stronger than O₂, because it sheds an atom of "nascent oxygen",
O₃ → O₂ + [O]: it oxidizes I⁻ to I₂ (starch–iodide paper turns **blue** — the standard
test), black PbS to white PbSO₄, and mercury suffers **tailing** (oxidized to Hg₂O, it
loses its meniscus and sticks to glass — the classic distinguishing test); it bleaches
dyes and disinfects drinking water without chlorine's taste. Its quantity is estimated
iodometrically (O₃ + 2KI + H₂O → I₂ + O₂ + 2KOH).

**The ozone layer** (stratosphere, 20–35 km) absorbs 97–99 % of the Sun's medium UV
(200–315 nm), shielding DNA from its mutagen. **Depletion:** chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs —
old refrigerants/aerosols) survive to that altitude, where UV frees chlorine atoms that
catalytically destroy ozone:

Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂, and ClO• + O → Cl• + O₂ (the Cl• is regenerated: one atom kills ≈ 10⁵ O₃ molecules)

with the Antarctic spring "ozone hole" worsened by polar stratospheric clouds. The
**Montreal Protocol (1987)** phased CFCs out worldwide — atmospheric chlorine is falling
and the layer is on a slow (mid-century) mend, the most successful environmental treaty to
date; NO from jets and N₂O are also catalytic offenders. Consequences of thinning: more
UV-B → skin cancer, cataracts, phytoplankton and crop damage. (Do not confuse *good*
stratospheric ozone with *bad* ground-level ozone, a photochemical-smog pollutant — Ch. 21.)

## 13.3 Ammonia, phosphine and nitric acid

**Ammonia NH₃.** Laboratory: heat NH₄Cl with slaked lime,
2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2NH₃↑ + 2H₂O, and dry over quicklime (never conc. H₂SO₄ or
CaCl₂ — both absorb it). Industrial: **Haber**, N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ at 450 °C, 200 atm,
Fe/Mo (Ch. 7's compromise). A colourless, pungent gas, fantastically soluble in water
(the fountain experiment: ~700 volumes in 1), giving the weak base NH₄OH. Its trigonal-
**pyramidal** molecule (sp³ N, lone pair at the apex) explains both faces: it is a base
(turns litmus blue; dense white fumes of NH₄Cl with HCl vapour — a field test), and a
**ligand**, donating the pair to metal ions: pale-blue Cu(OH)₂ dissolves in excess NH₃ to
the deep-blue tetraammine [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ (Schweizer's reagent chemistry, Ch. 14). Tests:
**Nessler's reagent** (alkaline K₂[HgI₄]) gives a brown colour/precipitate with even
traces of NH₃/NH₄⁺. Uses: fertilizers (urea, ammonium salts), HNO₃ manufacture,
refrigeration, and (aqueous) as a laboratory base and complexing agent.

**Phosphine PH₃.** From calcium phosphide and water, Ca₃P₂ + 6H₂O → 3Ca(OH)₂ + 2PH₃↑
(companion: white phosphorus boiled with NaOH gives PH₃ + sodium hypophosphite). A
colourless, **rotten-fish/garlic-smelling, very poisonous** gas, sparingly soluble,
only feebly basic (PH₄⁺ salts form only with strong acids like HI; basicity
NH₃ ≫ PH₃ because the lone pair sits in a diffuse 3s-ish orbital on the bigger atom) and
a stronger reductant than NH₃. Impure PH₃ (with P₂H₄ vapour) ignites spontaneously in
air — the basis of Holmes' signals (sea flares: Ca₃P₂ + CaC₂ cannisters) and of marsh
"will-o'-the-wisp" tales.

**Nitric acid HNO₃.** Laboratory: distil KNO₃/NaNO₃ with conc. H₂SO₄ in an all-glass
retort (KNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HNO₃↑). Industrial: **Ostwald's process** —
4NH₃ + 5O₂ →(Pt–Rh gauze, ~800 °C) 4NO + 6H₂O; 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂;
2NO₂ + H₂O → HNO₃ + HNO₂ (practically 3NO₂ + H₂O → 2HNO₃ + NO, the NO recycled).
Pure HNO₃ is colourless but commercial acid is yellow-brown from dissolved NO₂ (made by
its photodecomposition — store shaded). It is both a strong acid and a fierce
**oxidizing agent** whose reduction product tracks concentration:

| Reaction | Product gas | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cu + dilute HNO₃: 3Cu + 8HNO₃ → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO↑ + 4H₂O | NO (browns in air) | the standard equation |
| Cu + conc. HNO₃: Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂↑ + 2H₂O | NO₂ (brown) | |
| Zn + very dilute/cold HNO₃ | even NH₄NO₃ (N reduced to −3) | deeper reduction with active metals |
| Fe, Al, Cr + conc. HNO₃ | no attack — **passivity** | dense oxide film; hence conc. HNO₃ ships in aluminium |
| Non-metals + hot conc.: C → CO₂, S → H₂SO₄, P → H₃PO₄, I₂ → HIO₃ | — | HNO₃ plays pure oxidant |
| Aqua regia: HNO₃ + 3HCl (1 : 3) | dissolves Au, Pt | nascent Cl₂ and NOCl do the work |

Test: the **brown ring** — FeSO₄ solution + conc. H₂SO₄ down the side gives a brown ring
of [Fe(NO)]SO₄ at the junction (NO₃⁻/NO₂⁻ confirm; Ch. 22).

## 13.4 Chlorine, bromine and iodine; HCl, HBr and HI

The group-17 elements are diatomic, coloured, and hangrier for electrons at the top:
**oxidizing power F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂**, so the higher halogen displaces the lower from
its salts (Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂; Br₂ will not displace Cl⁻ in return). Laboratory Cl₂:
warm MnO₂ with conc. HCl (MnO₂ + 4HCl → MnCl₂ + Cl₂↑ + 2H₂O, similarly KMnO₄ acts in the
cold); industry gets it from brine electrolysis (Ch. 10) and historically by Deacon's
4HCl + O₂ → 2Cl₂ + 2H₂O (over CuCl₂ catalyst). Chlorine water bleaches by
*oxidation* (via HOCl) and its bleaching is **permanent** (contrast SO₂ below); with cold
dilute NaOH it gives NaCl + NaClO (bleach liquor), with hot concentrated, NaCl + NaClO₃;
over slaked lime it makes **bleaching powder**, Ca(OCl)Cl. Bromine (red-brown fuming
liquid) is won from sea-water/brines by chlorine displacement; iodine (violet vapour,
sublimes, starch turns **deep blue**) from seaweed ash and Chilean nitrate deposits;
both dissolve in KI solution (as I₃⁻) and in organic solvents with characteristic colours
(Br₂ orange in CCl₄; I₂ violet in CCl₄, brown in water/ethanol — a living qualitative
test).

**Hydrogen halides** are made by displacing from salts with a non-volatile acid — with a
crucial exception:

| HX | Preparation | Acid strength in water | Signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| HF | CaF₂ + conc. H₂SO₄ (in lead/Pt, not glass!) | **weak** (strong H–F bond, H-bonding) | etches glass: SiO₂ + 4HF → SiF₄↑ + 2H₂O — used for marking glassware, fluoridation |
| HCl | NaCl + conc. H₂SO₄; direct synthesis H₂ + Cl₂ (industry) | strong | white fumes with NH₃; AgCl white ppt, soluble in NH₄OH |
| HBr | **not** conc. H₂SO₄ (it oxidizes HBr → Br₂); use H₃PO₄, or PBr₃ + water | stronger | reducing agent |
| HI | H₃PO₄ route or PI₃ hydrolysis; conc. H₂SO₄ would give I₂ | strongest | strongest reductant of the four; even reduces conc. H₂SO₄ to H₂S/S |

Trends in one breath: bond strength HF > HCl > HBr > HI, so thermal stability falls and
**acidity and reducing power rise** down the group; silver halide tests run AgCl (white,
NH₄OH-soluble) → AgBr (pale yellow, sparingly soluble) → AgI (yellow, insoluble) as
Ksp falls (Ch. 7 patterns again).

## 13.5 Allotropes of carbon and carbon monoxide; H₂S, SO₂ and sulphuric acid

**Allotropes.** **Diamond:** every C sp³-bonded in a 3-D tetrahedral lattice — the hardest
natural substance, electrical insulator, superb heat conductor, transparent, very high
melting. **Graphite:** sp² sheets (each C bonded to three, the fourth electron delocalized)
held by weak forces — soft, slippery (pencil "lead", lubricant), conducts electricity
along the planes (electrodes, brushes). **Fullerenes** (C₆₀ buckminsterfullerene,
soccer-ball cages), **graphene** (a single graphite sheet — Physics Ch. 22), nanotubes,
and the amorphous family (coke, charcoal — adsorptive, gas masks; lampblack/carbon black —
inks, tyres). Whichever the costume, the chemistry (combustion to CO₂ 393.5 kJ per mole
for graphite) is carbon's.

**Carbon monoxide CO.** Laboratory: dehydrate formic (or oxalic) acid with hot conc.
H₂SO₄: HCOOH → CO↑ + H₂O. Industrial: **producer gas** (air over red-hot coke: CO + N₂)
and **water gas** (steam over coke: CO + H₂, endothermic — alternated with air blasts).
Colourless, odourless, almost insidiously poisonous: it binds haemoglobin
(carboxyhaemoglobin) some 200–250 times more avidly than O₂ — faulty room heaters kill
silently, and cherry-red blood is the post-mortem clue. Chemically a **neutral oxide**
(yet it reduces Fe₂O₃ in the blast furnace, Ch. 14), burns with a blue flame
(2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂), and is a strong ligand (Ni + 4CO ⇌ Ni(CO)₄, the Mond refining step).

**Hydrogen sulphide H₂S.** Kipp's apparatus, FeS + dil. H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂S↑ (never
conc. acid or HNO₃ — they oxidize it). Rotten-egg gas, denser than air, very poisonous
(and treacherously paralyses the sense of smell). A weak **dibasic acid**
(→ HS⁻ → S²⁻) and a **reductant** (decolorizes Br₂ water and acidified KMnO₄, depositing
sulfur). Its kingdom is analysis: with controlled [S²⁻] (acidic vs alkaline medium, the
common-ion machinery of Ch. 7) it precipitates metal sulphides group by group (Ch. 22).
Test: **lead acetate paper turns black** (PbS).

**Sulphur dioxide SO₂.** Made by burning sulfur, roasting sulphides
(4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂), or Cu + hot conc. H₂SO₄. Pungent, denser than air, very
soluble; an **acidic oxide** (H₂SO₃ in water; salts sulphites/bisulphites) — a major
acid-rain precursor. It is a **reducing agent** (turns acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ paper **green**,
Cr³⁺ — its distinguishing test; decolorizes KMnO₄) and its bleaching, by *reduction* of
coloured matter to colourless, is **temporary** (air slowly re-oxidizes the fabric — the
eternal pair with chlorine's permanent oxidative bleach).

**Sulphuric acid H₂SO₄** — the "king of chemicals". **Contact process**: purify SO₂;
2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ over **V₂O₅** at ~450 °C and modest pressure (exothermic: low T favoured
but rate demands compromise, Ch. 7); absorb SO₃ not in water (violent mist) but in conc.
H₂SO₄ to give **oleum** H₂S₂O₇, then dilute to order. Properties to file in threes:
(1) *strong dibasic acid* (first proton complete); (2) *dehydrating agent* when
concentrated — chars sugar to black carbon (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O), dries gases (not
NH₃, HBr, H₂S), and demands respect on dilution — **always acid into water**, the mixing
is fiercely exothermic; (3) *oxidizing agent* when hot and concentrated
(Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂↑ + 2H₂O; C → CO₂ + SO₂). Tests: BaCl₂ gives a white precipitate
of BaSO₄ insoluble in acids. Industrial fingerprints: fertilizers ((NH₄)₂SO₄,
superphosphate), detergents, petroleum refining, lead-acid batteries (density of the
acid even gauges their charge, Ch. 10).

::: example Worked example 13.1 — identify the gas
**Problem.** A colourless gas G (i) turns limewater milky slowly, (ii) turns acidified
potassium dichromate solution green, (iii) decolorizes bromine water, and (iv) bleaches a
coloured flower, whose colour returns in a few days. Identify G and the chemistry of
(ii).

**Solution.** G is **SO₂**: a reducing, acidic gas with temporary reductive bleaching
(CO₂ would fail (ii)–(iv); Cl₂'s bleaching is permanent and it is greenish).
(ii) SO₂ reduces orange Cr₂O₇²⁻ to green Cr³⁺:
K₂Cr₂O₇ + H₂SO₄ + 3SO₂ → K₂SO₄ + Cr₂(SO₄)₃ + H₂O (Cr +6 → +3; S +4 → +6).
:::

::: caution The three not-with-conc.-H₂SO₄ cases
Concentrated sulphuric acid fails as a maker/drier whenever the other partner reduces it
or is a base: HBr and HI (oxidized to Br₂, I₂), H₂S (oxidized to S), NH₃ (acid–base).
Examiners ask for the workarounds (H₃PO₄ for HBr/HI; CaO to dry NH₃; dil. acid for H₂S).
:::

::: caution
Six swaps the paper sets: **nascent hydrogen is atomic hydrogen in
statu nascendi**, not a different element; **D₂O moderates neutrons but
poisons eukaryotic life in bulk** — the same property list; **chlorine
bleaches by oxidation (permanent), SO₂ by reduction (temporary)**;
**aqua regia is 3 HCl : 1 HNO₃, not the reverse**; **HF is weak despite
fluorine's electronegativity**, because the H–F bond and hydrogen bonding
hold the proton; and **SO₃ is absorbed into acid as oleum, never into
water**, which would fog it into an unusable mist.
:::

::: example Disproportionation of chlorine in cold vs hot alkali
**Problem.** Write the balanced ionic equations and oxidation-state changes when $\text{Cl}_2$ reacts with (a) cold, dilute $\text{NaOH}$ and (b) hot, concentrated $\text{NaOH}$.

**Solution.** (a) Cold dilute alkali gives chloride ($-1$) and hypochlorite ($+1$): $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}^- + \text{ClO}^- + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. (b) Hot concentrated alkali disproportionates $\text{Cl}_2$ ($0$) into chloride ($-1$) and chlorate ($+5$) in a $5 : 1$ molar ratio: $3\text{Cl}_2 + 6\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 5\text{Cl}^- + \text{ClO}_3^- + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Hydrogen's personas scale in reducing violence (H₂ < [H] < atomic H); its isotopes give
  heavy water (a moderator) and tritium (a tracer).
- Oxides sort by character — acidic, basic, amphoteric, neutral, peroxide, superoxide,
  mixed — and ozone is oxygen's bent, resonating, oxidizing allotrope, whose
  stratospheric veil CFC chemistry taught us to defend.
- Ammonia is base and ligand in one lone pair; phosphine its feebler, fouler sibling;
  nitric acid an oxidizing acid whose product gas advertises its concentration.
- Halogens weaken downwards as oxidants while their hydrides strengthen as acids and
  reductants; HF alone is weak — and alone eats glass.
- Carbon reinvents itself from diamond to graphene; CO poisons quietly and refines
  metals; the sulphur trio H₂S/SO₂/H₂SO₄ escalates from analytical precipitant to the
  dehydrating, oxidizing king of chemicals.
