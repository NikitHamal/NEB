## 14.1 Animal behaviour: reflex actions, taxes and migration

**Behaviour** is an animal's observable response to internal or external
stimuli. Its scientific study is **ethology**. Innate behaviour is inherited
and stereotyped; learned behaviour is modified by experience. Most real
behaviour combines genetic predisposition with learning.

### Reflexes, taxes and kineses

A **reflex action** is a rapid, involuntary and stereotyped response mediated
by a reflex arc:

**receptor → sensory neuron → CNS → motor neuron → effector**

Withdrawal from a hot object, blinking and the knee jerk are examples. A reflex
usually moves only part of the body and is fast because the pathway is short.

A **taxis** (plural **taxes**) is *directed locomotion of the whole organism*
relative to a stimulus. Movement toward it is positive and away from it is
negative:

| Taxis | Stimulus | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Phototaxis | light | moth toward a lamp; cockroach away from light |
| Chemotaxis | chemical | mosquito toward CO₂/body odours; sperm toward egg signals |
| Geotaxis | gravity | many soil invertebrates move downward |
| Rheotaxis | water current | fish orient/swim against current |
| Thermotaxis | temperature | animals move toward a preferred temperature |
| Thigmotaxis | touch/contact | cockroaches keep contact with walls/crevices |

A **kinesis** is *undirected*: stimulus intensity changes speed or turning
rate, but the animal does not steer toward a source. Woodlice move and turn
more in dry air and less in humid air, so they accumulate in damp places by
differential activity rather than navigation.

A **fixed action pattern** is a more complex innate sequence released by a sign
stimulus and usually carried to completion, such as egg-retrieval in geese.
**Instinct** is a broader inherited programme such as web spinning or nest
building. Habituation, conditioning, imitation and insight are forms of
learning.

### Migration

**Migration** is regular, often seasonal, two-way movement between breeding
and non-breeding/feeding areas. It tracks food, water, climate and breeding
sites, at the cost of energy and exposure to predators. Birds may migrate
latitudinally or altitudinally; bar-headed geese and demoiselle cranes cross or
pass through the Himalayan region. Animals navigate using the sun, stars,
polarised light, landmarks, odours and sensitivity to Earth's magnetic field,
with an internal clock correcting for time.

Fish terminology is a frequent trap:

- **Anadromous:** adults live mainly at sea and migrate into fresh water to
  spawn—salmon.
- **Catadromous:** adults live mainly in fresh water and migrate to the sea to
  spawn—freshwater eels.
- **Potamodromous:** migration entirely within fresh water.
- **Oceanodromous:** migration entirely within the sea.


::: example Worked example — taxis, kinesis or reflex?
Classify three observations: (i) a moth flies *towards* a lamp; (ii) woodlice move faster in dry air and settle when they reach damp air, with no set direction; (iii) a finger is jerked off a hot pan before any pain is felt.

- (i) **Positive phototaxis** — locomotion *directed* towards the light stimulus.
- (ii) **Kinesis** — the *rate* of movement changes with humidity but direction is random; the animals simply accumulate where movement slows.
- (iii) A **reflex** — a fixed, involuntary, spinal response; the conscious pain registers slightly later.

The deciding test: is the movement aimed at/away from the stimulus (**taxis**), merely faster or slower (**kinesis**), or an automatic neural arc (**reflex**)?
:::

::: caution Trap — biomagnification is about concentration, not amount eaten
A persistent pollutant such as DDT becomes **more concentrated at each higher trophic level** (water → plankton → small fish → large fish → bird) because it is fat-soluble and neither excreted nor broken down; the famous end-result is thin-shelled eggs in birds of prey. It is different from **eutrophication** (nutrient enrichment → algal bloom → oxygen crash) — MCQs like to blur the two.
:::

::: example Mark–recapture: counting animals you cannot line up
**Problem.** In a Chitwan grassland, 40 spotted deer are captured, marked and
released. Weeks later 50 are captured, and 10 of them carry marks. Estimate
the population. A second survey marks 60, recaptures 80, finds 12 marked —
what estimate now?
**Solution.** Lincoln–Petersen: N = (marked first × captured second) ÷
recaptured marked = (40 × 50) ÷ 10 = **200 deer**. Second survey:
(60 × 80) ÷ 12 = **400**. The method assumes marks neither help nor hurt
survival and that mixing is complete — break either assumption and the
estimate lies, which is why real censuses repeat the capture many times over.
:::
## 14.2 Air, water and soil pollution; pesticides

**Pollution** is an undesirable change in the physical, chemical or biological
characteristics of air, water or soil that harms organisms, ecosystems or human
use. A pollutant may be **primary** (emitted in harmful form, such as CO or
SO₂) or **secondary** (formed in the environment, such as tropospheric ozone,
PAN or acid-rain acids). Sources may be point (a factory pipe) or non-point
(fertiliser/pesticide run-off from many fields).

### Air pollution

| Pollutant/process | Source and effect |
|---|---|
| **Particulate matter (PM₂.₅/PM₁₀)** | combustion, dust, brick kilns; penetrates airways/alveoli, aggravates respiratory and cardiovascular disease |
| **Carbon monoxide** | incomplete combustion; binds haemoglobin much more strongly than O₂, reducing oxygen delivery |
| **SO₂ and NOₓ** | fossil fuels/vehicles/industry; respiratory irritation and conversion to sulphuric/nitric acids |
| **Acid rain** | precipitation below the natural pH of about 5.6; acidifies lakes/soils, mobilises toxic aluminium, damages leaves/buildings |
| **Ground-level ozone/PAN** | secondary products of NOₓ + volatile organics in sunlight (photochemical smog); injure lungs and crops |
| **CFCs** | old refrigerants/aerosols; UV releases chlorine that catalytically destroys stratospheric ozone |
| **Greenhouse gases** | CO₂, CH₄, N₂O and fluorinated gases absorb outgoing infrared, enhancing warming |

Do not merge the three atmospheric problems: greenhouse gases warm by trapping
outgoing infrared mainly in the troposphere; ozone depletion admits more
incoming UV through the stratosphere; acid rain is wet/dry deposition of acids
formed from SO₂ and NOₓ.

### Water pollution and eutrophication

Sewage, industrial effluents, fertiliser/pesticide run-off, oil, heated water,
plastics and pathogens pollute water. **Eutrophication** follows nutrient
enrichment, especially nitrogen and phosphorus:

**nutrients → algal/cyanobacterial bloom → light blocked and plants die →
decomposition increases BOD → dissolved O₂ crashes → fish/invertebrates die**

Clean water has low BOD and high dissolved oxygen. Heavy metals such as lead,
cadmium, chromium and mercury do not “rot away”; they persist, bind proteins
and may enter food chains. Excess nitrate in drinking water and geogenic
arsenic/fluoride are chemical hazards distinct from microbial contamination.

### Soil pollution and pesticides

Soil is polluted by excessive fertiliser/pesticide, industrial sludge, mine
waste, leaking fuel, plastics and unsegregated municipal waste. Consequences
include loss of soil microbiota/earthworms, nutrient imbalance, salinity,
groundwater leaching and uptake of toxins by crops.

A **pesticide** is used against a pest group—such as insecticides, herbicides,
fungicides or rodenticides. Its environmental behaviour matters as much as its
immediate kill:

- **Organochlorines** such as DDT are persistent and fat-soluble, making them
  prone to bioaccumulation/biomagnification.
- **Organophosphates/carbamates** inhibit acetylcholinesterase; generally less
  persistent but can be acutely toxic.
- **Pyrethroids** alter nerve sodium channels and can be highly toxic to fish
  and aquatic invertebrates.
- **Neonicotinoids** are systemic neuroactive insecticides of concern for
  pollinators and aquatic insects.

**Bioaccumulation** is build-up within one organism over time.
**Biomagnification** is rising concentration at successive trophic levels
because predators consume many contaminated prey while retaining most of the
persistent chemical.

Integrated pest management combines monitoring/thresholds, crop rotation and
resistant varieties, habitat for natural enemies, biocontrol/mechanical
methods and targeted chemicals only when needed, reducing resistance and
non-target damage.

## 14.3 Aquatic, terrestrial and volant adaptations

An **adaptation** is an inherited morphological, physiological or behavioural
trait shaped by natural selection that improves survival/reproduction in a
particular environment. It differs from **acclimatisation**, a reversible
adjustment within an individual's lifetime.

### Aquatic adaptations

Water is dense, buoyant, thermally buffering and oxygen-poor compared with
air. Typical fish solutions are:

- fusiform/streamlined body, backward-pointing scales and mucus to reduce drag;
- caudal fin for thrust, dorsal/anal fins against rolling and paired fins for
  steering/braking;
- large, thin gill lamellae with counter-current blood/water flow;
- a swim bladder for neutral buoyancy in many bony fishes;
- lateral-line detection of vibration/current and often countershading.

Osmoregulation reverses between media. A freshwater bony fish gains water and
loses salts, so it excretes abundant dilute urine and actively absorbs ions at
the gills. A marine bony fish loses water to the sea, drinks seawater, excretes
excess salt at the gills and produces scant urine. Secondary aquatic mammals
such as dolphins breathe air with lungs and use flippers/blubber; they are not
fish despite a convergent outline.

### Terrestrial adaptations

Land removes buoyant support and creates desiccation and temperature stress.
Terrestrial animals evolve strong limbs/skeletons, waterproof skin or cuticle,
internal lungs/tracheae, water-conserving excretory products and internal
fertilisation or shelled eggs. By locomotor habit:

| Habit | Design | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Cursorial** (running) | long limbs, reduced digits, digitigrade/unguligrade stance, elastic tendons | horse, chital, blackbuck |
| **Arboreal** (trees) | grasping/opposable digits, claws or adhesive pads, prehensile tail in some, binocular vision | langur, red panda, many geckos |
| **Fossorial** (burrowing) | compact/spindle body, strong forelimbs/claws, reduced eyes/external ears | mole, pangolin |
| **Desert/xeric** | nocturnality, burrows, concentrated urine/dry faeces, metabolic water, heat tolerance | camel, kangaroo rat |
| **Cold/high-altitude** | insulation, compact extremities, counter-current limb heat exchange, high oxygen-carrying capacity | yak, snow leopard, marmot |

**Bergmann's rule** predicts larger body size in colder populations of related
endotherms; **Allen's rule** predicts shorter appendages in cold climates.
Both reduce surface-area-to-volume heat loss and are tendencies, not laws.

### Volant (flight) adaptations

True powered flight evolved independently in insects, birds and bats (and in
extinct pterosaurs). In birds, the examination list is:

- streamlined body and feathers forming smooth aerofoils;
- forelimbs transformed into wings with fused/reduced hand bones;
- **pneumatic bones** that are hollow but internally strutted;
- a deep **keeled sternum (carina)** anchoring huge pectoral flight muscles;
- **air sacs** connected to lungs, supporting highly efficient, largely one-way
  airflow and cooling;
- four-chambered heart/double circulation and high metabolic rate;
- weight economy: beak instead of teeth/jaw heaviness, no urinary bladder,
  semi-solid uric-acid excretion and compact reproductive organs outside the
  breeding season.

Bats instead fly on a skin **patagium** stretched over greatly elongated finger
bones. Gliders such as flying squirrels descend on membranes but do not sustain
powered flapping flight.

## 14.4 Conservation biology: biodiversity, protected areas, hotspots, Ramsar sites, IUCN categories and endangered species of Nepal

**Biodiversity** is variety at genetic, species and ecosystem levels. Species
diversity has both **richness** (how many species) and **evenness** (how equally
abundant they are). A major threat mnemonic is **HIPPO**: Habitat loss,
Invasive species, Pollution, Population/consumption pressure and
Overexploitation—now all amplified by climate change.



::: example Measuring evenness with Simpson's index
A sample contains four species with counts 50, 25, 15 and 10, so $N=100$.
Simpson's dominance is

$$D=\frac{\sum n_i(n_i-1)}{N(N-1)}
=\frac{50(49)+25(24)+15(14)+10(9)}{100(99)}
=\frac{3350}{9900}=0.338$$

Simpson's diversity is $1-D=0.662$, and the reciprocal $1/D=2.96$ is the
effective number of equally common species. It is below the richness of 4
because one species dominates.
:::

### In-situ and ex-situ conservation; protected areas

**In-situ** conservation protects a species in its natural ecosystem—national
parks, wildlife reserves, conservation areas, buffer zones/community forests—
so ecological interactions and natural selection continue. **Ex-situ**
conservation protects components outside the habitat—zoos, botanical gardens,
seed/gene banks, cryopreservation and captive breeding. Ex-situ methods are
insurance and a source for reintroduction; they do not by themselves replace a
functioning wild ecosystem.

Nepal's network under the National Parks and Wildlife Conservation Act is
described in current departmental summaries as 13 national parks, one wildlife
reserve, one hunting reserve and seven conservation areas, in addition to
buffer zones. Management categories differ:

```photo id="bio11/red-panda" caption="The red panda, *Ailurus fulgens*, a threatened species of the forests of eastern and central Nepal." width="68"
```


| Category | Nepal example | Defining management |
|---|---|---|
| National park | Chitwan (first, 1973); Sagarmatha; Bardiya | strict ecosystem protection; resource extraction/hunting prohibited |
| Wildlife reserve | Koshi Tappu (only one) | protection of particular species/habitats, especially arna and wetland birds |
| Hunting reserve | Dhorpatan (only one) | tightly licensed, quota-based trophy hunting |
| Conservation area | Annapurna (largest); Kanchenjunga; Manaslu | multiple use with resident communities participating in management |
| Buffer zone | around Chitwan/Bardiya and other parks | regulated use and revenue-sharing to build local support/reduce conflict |

### Biodiversity hotspots and Ramsar sites

A Conservation International **biodiversity hotspot** must contain at least
1,500 endemic vascular plant species and have lost at least 70% of its original
habitat. Nepal's mountain and foothill ecosystems form part of the
**Himalaya hotspot**, one reason the country holds exceptional species richness
over a steep elevation gradient.

**Ramsar sites** are wetlands of international importance designated under the
Ramsar Convention (Iran, 1971). Nepal has ten: Koshi Tappu, Beeshazari and
associated lakes, Ghodaghodi Lake area, Jagadishpur Reservoir, Gokyo and
associated lakes, Gosaikunda and associated lakes, Phoksundo Lake, Rara Lake,
Mai Pokhari and the Lake Cluster of Pokhara Valley. Ramsar designation is a
commitment to “wise use”, not automatically the same legal category as a
national park.

### IUCN categories and threatened animals of Nepal

The IUCN Red List orders evaluated species as:

**EX Extinct → EW Extinct in the Wild → CR Critically Endangered → EN
Endangered → VU Vulnerable → NT Near Threatened → LC Least Concern**

DD (Data Deficient) and NE (Not Evaluated) sit outside that risk ranking.
A species is **threatened** only if it is CR, EN or VU. Status is quantitative
and can change after reassessment; “endangered” in ordinary speech is not the
same as the formal EN category.

| White-rumped vulture | *Gyps bengalensis* | **Critically Endangered** |
| Bengal florican | *Houbaropsis bengalensis* | **Critically Endangered** |
| Bengal tiger | *Panthera tigris* | **Endangered** |

| Asian elephant | *Elephas maximus* | **Endangered** |

flagship mammals. Their increase also creates new management duties: prey and
habitat carrying capacity, genetic mixing and prevention/compensation of
human–wildlife conflict.

Four pairs the paper swaps: **taxis is directed, kinesis is not** (a moth
steering to light vs a woodlouse merely slowing in damp); **bioaccumulation**
is build-up within one body, **biomagnification** is the climb up the food

## Formula and fact sheet

- Reflex = receptor → sensory neuron → CNS → motor neuron → effector.
- Taxis = directed whole-organism orientation; kinesis = undirected rate change.
- Anadromous = sea to river; catadromous = river to sea.
- SO₂/NOₓ → acid rain; CFC-Cl → stratospheric ozone depletion; greenhouse gases → infrared trapping.
- Eutrophication: nutrient ↑ → bloom ↑ → decomposition/BOD ↑ → dissolved O₂ ↓.
- Bioaccumulation is within an organism; biomagnification is across trophic levels.
- Freshwater fish: water in, dilute urine out, salts absorbed. Marine bony fish: drinks, salt excreted at gills, scant urine.
- Cursorial = running; arboreal = trees; fossorial = burrowing; volant = flying.
- Bird flight: feathers, pneumatic bones, keeled sternum, pectorals, air sacs.
- Simpson diversity $=1-D$, where $D=\sum n_i(n_i-1)/[N(N-1)]$; $1/D$ = effective species number.
- Ramsar = wetlands of international importance; Nepal has 10 listed sites.
- IUCN: EX, EW, CR, EN, VU, NT, LC; threatened = CR+EN+VU.

## Chapter summary

- Reflexes, taxes and kineses differ by pathway, body involvement and whether
  movement is directed.
- Migration trades travel costs for seasonal access to food and breeding
  habitat and relies on multiple navigational cues.
- Air pollution separates into particles/toxic gases, smog, acid deposition,
  ozone depletion and greenhouse warming.
- Water and soil pollutants may be biodegradable, nutrient-like or persistent;
  persistence plus fat solubility creates biomagnification.
- Aquatic and terrestrial animals solve opposite mechanical/osmotic problems,
  while flight imposes the strictest combined demands for lift, power and
  low mass.
- Biodiversity includes genes, species and ecosystems, and evenness matters as
  well as species count.
- In-situ protected areas preserve whole systems; ex-situ collections are a
  complementary safety net.
- Hotspots and Ramsar sites prioritise exceptional endemic-rich and wetland
  ecosystems under threat.
- IUCN categories make extinction risk comparable, and Nepal's rhino, tiger
  and vulture programmes show that specific threat removal can work.
