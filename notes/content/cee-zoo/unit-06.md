The respiratory system carries **2 of the 40 Zoology questions**. The paper asks
for the **organs and their lining**, the **mechanics of breathing** (which muscle
does what, and which way the pressure moves), the **lung volumes**, the **partial
pressures** and the **two transport routes** for O₂ and CO₂, and the **control of
breathing**. The applied questions are almost always one of five diseases. The
traps are the same every year: **most CO₂ travels as bicarbonate, not as
carbaminohaemoglobin**, and **the respiratory centre responds to CO₂, not to O₂**.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- The **route** nostrils → nasal cavity → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli, and the **epithelium** of each part (pseudostratified ciliated columnar in trachea and bronchi; simple squamous in the alveolus).
- **Trachea**: 10–12 cm, **C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings** open behind; the **right bronchus** is wider, shorter and more vertical, so foreign bodies lodge there.
- **Alveoli**: 300–500 million, total surface about **70–80 m²**, wall **0.5 μm** thick; **type II cells** make **surfactant**.
- **Breathing**: inspiration is **active** (diaphragm + external intercostals), expiration **passive**; intrapleural pressure is **always negative**.
- **Volumes**: TV 500, IRV 3000, ERV 1100, RV 1200 mL; **VC = TV + IRV + ERV**; TLC = VC + RV; FRC = ERV + RV.
- **Partial pressures**: alveolar pO₂ **104**, arterial 95–100, venous **40** mmHg; pCO₂ 40 arterial, **45** venous.
- **O₂** travels **97 % on haemoglobin**; **CO₂** travels **70 % as bicarbonate**, 23 % carbamino, 7 % dissolved.
- **Control**: the **medulla** sets the rhythm and answers a **rise in CO₂/H⁺**; the carotid and aortic bodies answer a **fall in O₂** below about 60 mmHg.
:::

## 6.1 Respiratory organs and the mechanism of breathing

**Respiration** is the whole process by which an animal takes in O₂, uses it to
release energy from food and gives out CO₂. It has three levels:

| Level | Where | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| **External respiration** (breathing) | lungs | exchange of O₂ and CO₂ between alveolar air and blood |
| **Internal respiration** | tissues | exchange between blood and tissue cells |
| **Cellular respiration** | mitochondria | C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy (about 38 ATP) |

### The organs, from nostril to alveolus

The airway is divided into a **conducting part** (nostrils to terminal
bronchioles), which carries, warms, moistens and filters the air, and a
**respiratory part** (respiratory bronchioles, alveolar ducts and alveoli), where
exchange happens. The conducting part holds about **150 mL** of air — the **dead
space** — which takes no part in exchange.

| Organ | Structure | Epithelium | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nostrils and nasal cavity** | vestibule with hairs; three conchae; richly supplied with blood | **stratified squamous** at the vestibule, **ciliated columnar with goblet cells** above | filters, warms to about 37 °C, moistens, and smells |
| **Pharynx** (naso-, oro-, laryngo-) | muscular tube behind the mouth | **stratified squamous** (ciliated columnar in the nasopharynx) | common passage for air and food |
| **Larynx** (voice box) | thyroid, cricoid and arytenoid cartilages; **vocal cords**; glottis guarded by the **epiglottis** | stratified squamous over the cords, ciliated columnar elsewhere | produces sound; keeps food out of the airway |
| **Trachea** (windpipe) | **10–12 cm** long, 2.5 cm wide; **16–20 C-shaped hyaline cartilage rings** open behind, closed by the trachealis muscle | **pseudostratified ciliated columnar with goblet cells** | conducts air; mucus traps dust, cilia sweep it up |
| **Primary bronchi** | one to each lung; the **right** is wider, shorter and more vertical | same as the trachea | conducts air |
| **Bronchioles** | no cartilage; smooth muscle in the wall | ciliated **cuboidal**, then simple cuboidal | control airflow (bronchoconstriction, bronchodilation) |
| **Alveoli** | 300–500 million air sacs; wall **0.5 μm** thick | **simple squamous** (type I cells), **type II cells**, macrophages | gas exchange; surfactant |

The **mucociliary escalator** is the defence of the conducting zone: goblet cells
and submucosal glands make mucus, and the cilia beat upwards about 1,000 times a
minute to carry trapped dust and bacteria to the pharynx, where they are
swallowed. Cigarette smoke paralyses the cilia, which is why smokers cough.

### The larynx and the trachea

The **larynx** is a box of cartilages at the top of the trachea. The **thyroid
cartilage** forms the Adam's apple; the **cricoid** is a complete ring below it;
the **arytenoid** cartilages move the **vocal cords**, and air passing between
them produces sound. The **glottis** is the opening; the **epiglottis** folds over
it during swallowing.

The **trachea** runs down the neck in front of the oesophagus and divides at the
**carina** (level of the **fourth to fifth thoracic vertebra**) into the two
primary bronchi. Its cartilage rings are **C-shaped**, incomplete at the back, so
that a bolus of food in the oesophagus can bulge into it.

::: caution Why the right bronchus matters
The **right primary bronchus** is **wider, shorter and more vertical** than the
left, so an inhaled peanut or a coin usually lodges in the **right** lung. This is
a standard application question, and the distractor is always "the left bronchus
because it is longer".
:::

### The lungs and the pleura

Each lung lies in its own **pleural cavity**. The **visceral pleura** sticks to
the lung, the **parietal pleura** lines the chest wall, and a thin film of
**pleural fluid** between them holds the two surfaces together by surface tension.
This is what keeps the lung inflated.

| Feature | Right lung | Left lung |
|---|---|---|
| Lobes | **three** (upper, middle, lower) | **two** (upper, lower) |
| Fissures | oblique and horizontal | oblique only |
| Notch | none | **cardiac notch** (room for the heart) |
| Size | shorter and wider (the liver pushes it up) | longer and narrower |

### The alveolus

An alveolus is a thin-walled sac about 0.2–0.3 mm across, surrounded by a network
of capillaries. Its wall has three cell types:

- **Type I (squamous) cells** — about 95 % of the surface; only 0.1–0.5 μm thick;
  they form the **respiratory membrane** with the capillary endothelium.
- **Type II (septal) cells** — cuboidal; they secrete **surfactant**, a mixture
  rich in **dipalmitoyl lecithin**, which **lowers surface tension** and stops the
  alveoli collapsing as they empty. A premature baby born before about 28–30 weeks
  may lack surfactant and develop **respiratory distress syndrome**.
- **Alveolar macrophages** — engulf dust and bacteria.

The respiratory membrane (alveolar epithelium + fused basement membrane +
capillary endothelium) is only **0.5 μm** thick — about 1/100 of the thickness of
this page — so diffusion is very fast.

```

### The mechanism of breathing

**Inspiration (inhalation) is active.** The diaphragm contracts and flattens
(descending about 1.5 cm), and the **external intercostal muscles** contract and
lift the ribs up and out. The thoracic cavity gets bigger in all three
dimensions, so the pressure inside the lungs (**intrapulmonary pressure**) falls
about **1 mmHg below atmospheric**, and air flows in.

**Expiration (exhalation) at rest is passive.** The muscles relax, the elastic
lung tissue and the surface tension of the alveoli recoil, the thoracic volume
falls, intrapulmonary pressure rises about **1 mmHg above atmospheric**, and air
flows out. **Forced expiration** uses the **internal intercostals** and the
**abdominal muscles**.

| Pressure | Value | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **Atmospheric** | 760 mmHg | the reference |
| **Intrapulmonary (alveolar)** | −1 mmHg in inspiration, +1 mmHg in expiration | the small difference that moves air |
| **Intrapleural** | **−4 mmHg** at rest, −6 to −8 mmHg in inspiration | always subatmospheric; keeps the lung stretched against the chest wall |

The **intrapleural pressure is always negative** because the growing chest pulls
the elastic lung outward, and the fluid film resists separation. If air enters the
pleural cavity — a **pneumothorax** from a stab wound or a burst bleb — the
negative pressure is lost, the lung's own elasticity makes it collapse, and
breathing on that side fails.

**Compliance** is the ease with which the lungs stretch: a healthy lung inflates
easily because surfactant lowers surface tension and the elastic fibres are
arranged to recoil gently. **Elasticity** is the tendency to spring back, and it
is what drives quiet expiration. In **emphysema** elasticity is lost and
compliance rises, so the lung inflates easily but empties badly; in **fibrosis**
compliance falls and the lung is stiff, so breathing costs much more work.

| Muscle | Nerve | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Diaphragm** | **phrenic nerve (C3, C4, C5)** | flattens, increases vertical diameter — about 75 % of quiet inspiration |
| **External intercostals** | intercostal nerves | raise the ribs, increase the transverse diameter |
| Sternocleidomastoid, scalenes | cervical nerves | lift the sternum and first ribs in forced inspiration |
| **Internal intercostals, abdominal muscles** | intercostal nerves | pull the ribs down and squeeze the abdomen in **forced expiration** |

### Lung volumes and capacities

| Name | Definition | Typical value (young adult man) |
|---|---|---|
| **Tidal volume (TV)** | air moved in or out in one quiet breath | **500 mL** |
| **Inspiratory reserve volume (IRV)** | extra air taken in after a normal inspiration | **3000 mL** |
| **Expiratory reserve volume (ERV)** | extra air forced out after a normal expiration | **1100 mL** |
| **Residual volume (RV)** | air left in the lungs that cannot be breathed out | **1200 mL** |
| **Inspiratory capacity (IC)** | TV + IRV | **3500 mL** |
| **Functional residual capacity (FRC)** | ERV + RV | **2300 mL** |
| **Vital capacity (VC)** | TV + IRV + ERV — the largest breath possible | **4600 mL** |
| **Total lung capacity (TLC)** | VC + RV | **5800 mL** |

Two rules answer almost every volume question: **vital capacity never includes
the residual volume**, and **the residual volume cannot be measured by a simple
spirometer** — it needs helium dilution or body plethysmography.

### Worked examples

::: example Minute ventilation and alveolar ventilation
**Problem.** A resting student has a tidal volume of 500 mL and breathes 12 times
a minute. The dead space is 150 mL. Find the minute ventilation and the alveolar
ventilation.

**Solution.** Minute ventilation $= 500 \times 12 = 6000$ mL = **6.0 L min⁻¹**.

Alveolar ventilation $= (500 - 150) \times 12 = 4200$ mL = **4.2 L min⁻¹**.

So 1.8 L of every 6 L breathed only fills the conducting airways. During exercise
the depth of breathing matters more than the rate, because the dead space is
fixed.
:::

::: example Oxygen carried by the blood
**Problem.** A person has 15 g of haemoglobin per 100 mL of blood, and 1 g of
haemoglobin binds 1.34 mL of O₂ when saturated. How much O₂ does 5 L of arterial
blood carry?

**Solution.** Per 100 mL: $15 \times 1.34 = 20.1$ mL. Per litre: 201 mL. In 5 L:
$201 \times 5 \approx 1005$ mL ≈ **1.0 L of O₂**.

This is why anaemia causes breathlessness even when the lungs are normal: the
volume of blood is fine, but there is too little haemoglobin to fill it.
:::

::: example Checking the volumes add up
**Problem.** A spirometer gives TV = 500 mL, IRV = 3000 mL and ERV = 1100 mL. The
residual volume is measured separately as 1200 mL. Find VC and TLC.

**Solution.** VC $= 500 + 3000 + 1100 = 4600$ mL. TLC $= 4600 + 1200 = 5800$ mL.
A student who writes 4600 mL for TLC has forgotten the residual volume — the
commonest error in this topic.
:::

## 6.2 Exchange and transport of gases; regulation of respiration

### Partial pressures

Gases move from a region of **higher partial pressure** to one of **lower partial
pressure**. That single sentence explains all gas exchange.

```photo id="bio12/alveoli" caption="Alveoli in a lung lobule. Each alveolar sac is a cluster of alveoli at the end of an alveolar duct, wrapped in a network of capillaries where gas exchange takes place." width="72"
```


| Site | pO₂ (mmHg) | pCO₂ (mmHg) |
|---|---|---|
| Atmospheric air (dry) | 159 | 0.3 |
| **Alveolar air** | **104** | **40** |
| **Arterial blood** | **95–100** | **40** |
| **Venous blood** | **40** | **45** |
| Tissue cells | below 40 | above 45 |

Alveolar pO₂ is lower than atmospheric because the air is saturated with water
vapour (which adds 47 mmHg) and because O₂ is constantly being absorbed.

### Exchange across the respiratory membrane

- **In the lungs**: alveolar pO₂ (104) > venous pO₂ (40), so O₂ diffuses into the
  blood; venous pCO₂ (45) > alveolar pCO₂ (40), so CO₂ diffuses out.
- **In the tissues**: arterial pO₂ (95) > tissue pO₂ (<40), so O₂ leaves the
  blood; tissue pCO₂ (>45) > arterial pCO₂ (40), so CO₂ enters it.

Diffusion is by **simple diffusion** — no energy, no carrier. It is fast because
the membrane is **0.5 μm** thick, the area is **70–80 m²**, and the gradients are
steep. **CO₂ diffuses about 20 times faster than O₂** because it is far more
soluble in the membrane, which is why hypoxia comes before hypercapnia in most
lung disease.

### Transport of oxygen

About **97 %** of the O₂ in blood is carried by **haemoglobin** and **3 %** is
dissolved in the plasma. Each haemoglobin molecule has four haem groups, each with
one Fe²⁺, and binds four O₂ molecules:

$$\text{Hb} + 4\text{O}_2 \rightleftharpoons \text{Hb(O}_2\text{)}_4$$

Oxyhaemoglobin is bright red; deoxyhaemoglobin is dark red-purple, which is why
venous blood looks darker. **Carboxyhaemoglobin** — haemoglobin with carbon
monoxide — cannot carry O₂ at all, and CO binds about **200–250 times** more
strongly than O₂, so even a little CO is dangerous.

### The oxygen dissociation curve and the Bohr effect

Plot the percentage saturation of haemoglobin against pO₂ and you get a
**sigmoid (S-shaped) curve**. Its shape matters:

- At the **lungs** (pO₂ about 100 mmHg) haemoglobin is about **98 % saturated** —
  it picks up O₂ easily.
- At the **tissues** (pO₂ about 40 mmHg) it is about **75 % saturated** — it lets
  O₂ go.
- The **P₅₀** is the pO₂ at which haemoglobin is 50 % saturated: about
  **26–27 mmHg**. A low P₅₀ means high affinity.

The curve shifts **to the right** (affinity falls, O₂ is released more easily)
when any of these rise:

| Factor | Why |
|---|---|
| **pCO₂** | more CO₂ → more H⁺ (carbonic acid) |
| **H⁺ (lower pH)** | the **Bohr effect** proper |
| **Temperature** | exercising muscle is warmer |
| **2,3-BPG** | made in red cells; rises at altitude and in anaemia |

A shift **to the left** (higher affinity) happens with a **fall** in CO₂, H⁺,
temperature and 2,3-BPG, and in **fetal haemoglobin**, which must take O₂ from
the mother's blood across the placenta.

::: caution The Bohr effect is about unloading, not loading
The Bohr effect says that in active tissue, where CO₂ and H⁺ are high, haemoglobin
**releases** O₂ more readily. Students often write that it "helps haemoglobin pick
up oxygen" — that is the opposite. The same shift helps **loading** in the lung,
where CO₂ is low, but the effect is named for the tissue side.
:::

### Transport of carbon dioxide

| Form | Percentage | Where / how |
|---|---|---|
| **Bicarbonate (HCO₃⁻)** | **70 %** | CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ → H⁺ + HCO₃⁻, catalysed by **carbonic anhydrase** inside the red cell; HCO₃⁻ leaves into the plasma and Cl⁻ enters the cell (**chloride shift**) |
| **Carbaminohaemoglobin** | **23 %** | CO₂ binds the globin (not the haem) — HbCO₂ |
| **Dissolved in plasma** | **7 %** | simple solution; this is the part that sets the pCO₂ |

The **Haldane effect** is the mirror of the Bohr effect: **deoxygenated
haemoglobin carries more CO₂**. In the tissues, where Hb has just given up O₂, it
takes up CO₂ and H⁺ easily; in the lungs, oxygenation pushes CO₂ out. The two
effects together make gas transport far more efficient than either alone.

::: caution The commonest wrong answer in the whole chapter
"Most CO₂ is carried as carbaminohaemoglobin" is wrong. **70 % travels as
bicarbonate**, 23 % as carbamino compounds and 7 % dissolved. A related trap:
the enzyme that makes bicarbonate is **carbonic anhydrase**, and it is inside the
**red cell**, not in the plasma.
:::

### Regulation of respiration

Breathing is rhythmic and automatic, but it can be overridden for a while by the
will — which is why we can hold our breath, and why speech is possible.

| Centre | Position | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Dorsal respiratory group** | **medulla oblongata** | sets the basic rhythm — inspiration for about 2 s, then relaxation |
| **Ventral respiratory group** | medulla | active only in forced breathing; drives expiration |
| **Pneumotaxic centre** | upper **pons** | switches inspiration off, so it controls the rate and depth |
| **Apneustic centre** | lower pons | prolongs inspiration |
| **Central chemoreceptors** | ventral medulla | respond to **H⁺** in the cerebrospinal fluid, which rises when blood **CO₂** rises |
| **Peripheral chemoreceptors** | **carotid bodies** and **aortic bodies** | respond to a **fall in O₂** (strongly below about 60 mmHg) and to a rise in H⁺ |
| Stretch receptors | lungs | **Hering–Breuer reflex** — a big inflation stops inspiration |
| Cerebral cortex | cerebrum | voluntary control, speech, breath-holding |

The **main drive to breathe is CO₂, not O₂**. A rise in arterial pCO₂ of only a
few mmHg increases ventilation greatly. CO₂ crosses the blood–brain barrier easily
and forms H⁺ in the cerebrospinal fluid, and the central chemoreceptors respond to
that H⁺. Oxygen lack is only a back-up stimulus, and it becomes important only
when arterial pO₂ falls below about 60 mmHg — which is why a patient breathing
oxygen may lose the drive to breathe if the disease has made him depend on
hypoxic drive.

::: caution Two traps in the control of breathing
(1) The **respiratory centre is in the medulla**, not the pons, cerebellum or
cortex; the pons only modifies it. (2) The centre responds chiefly to **CO₂/H⁺**.
A stem that says "the respiratory centre is stimulated mainly by a fall in oxygen"
is the standard wrong option.
:::

### Exercise and high altitude

In **exercise**, ventilation may rise from 6 to 100 L min⁻¹. The depth of each
breath increases first, because the dead space is fixed and deep breathing is more
efficient than fast shallow breathing. More CO₂ and H⁺ from the muscles stimulate
the respiratory centre, and the Bohr shift delivers more O₂ where it is needed.

At **high altitude** the partial pressure of oxygen in the air falls, so arterial
pO₂ falls and the carotid bodies increase ventilation. The person also makes more
**2,3-BPG**, which shifts the dissociation curve right, and over weeks produces
more red cells (erythropoietin from the kidney). This is why climbers need days to
acclimatise, and why acute mountain sickness — headache, breathlessness, nausea —
strikes those who go up too fast.

## 6.3 Respiratory disorders

| Disorder | What happens | Cause | Key clinical point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Asthma** | reversible narrowing of the bronchioles — smooth-muscle spasm, mucosal oedema, thick mucus | allergy (house dust, pollen), cold air, exercise, stress; histamine and leukotrienes; eosinophils | **wheezing**, difficulty in **expiration**, attacks; treated with bronchodilators and steroids |
| **Bronchitis** (chronic) | inflammation of bronchi with too much mucus; goblet cells enlarge | **smoking**, dust, pollution | **cough with sputum for at least 3 months in 2 successive years** |
| **Emphysema** | **alveolar walls destroyed**, air spaces enlarge, elastic recoil lost | smoking, α₁-antitrypsin deficiency | **barrel chest**, pursed-lip breathing, **↑ residual volume**, ↓ surface area, "pink puffer" |
| **Pneumonia** | alveoli filled with fluid and pus — consolidation | ***Streptococcus pneumoniae*** most often; viruses | fever, cough, **rusty sputum**, crackles; a **diffusion** problem |
| **Tuberculosis** | caseous (cheese-like) necrosis and cavities, usually at the lung apex | ***Mycobacterium tuberculosis***, droplet infection | chronic cough, evening fever, weight loss; **Ghon complex**; BCG vaccine; DOTS treatment for 6 months |
| **Lung cancer** | malignant growth of the bronchial epithelium | **smoking** (tar), asbestos, radon | persistent cough, blood in sputum, weight loss |

**Emphysema and chronic bronchitis together are called COPD** (chronic obstructive
pulmonary disease). In both, air is trapped, so **residual volume rises and vital
capacity falls**; the chest becomes barrel-shaped and expiration is prolonged
through pursed lips, which keeps the small airways from collapsing.

| Feature | **Obstructive** (asthma, bronchitis, emphysema) | **Restrictive** (pneumonia, TB, fibrosis) |
|---|---|---|
| Main problem | air cannot get **out** | lung cannot fill **in** |
| FEV₁ | **reduced** | reduced |
| Residual volume | **increased** | normal or reduced |
| Vital capacity | normal or slightly reduced | **reduced** |
| Expiration | prolonged, wheeze | rapid, shallow breathing |

**Effects of smoking.**

- **Nicotine** — addictive; raises heart rate and blood pressure; constricts
  vessels.
- **Tar** — a mixture of carcinogens; causes lung cancer and paralyses the cilia.
- **Carbon monoxide** — binds haemoglobin about 200–250 times more strongly than
  O₂, forming **carboxyhaemoglobin**, so less O₂ is carried.
- **Irritants** — make the mucosa swell and the glands secrete more mucus, while
  the paralysed cilia cannot clear it: the smoker's cough, then chronic
  bronchitis, then emphysema.

::: tip Answering a respiratory disease question
Ask three things. **Obstructive or restrictive?** Asthma, bronchitis and emphysema
obstruct the airway (air trapping, ↑ RV); pneumonia, TB and fibrosis restrict the
lung (↓ VC, ↓ compliance). **Reversible or not?** Asthma is reversible; emphysema
is not. **Infective or not?** Pneumonia and TB are infective; asthma, emphysema
and bronchitis (chronic) are not.
:::

**Traps**

1. **Most CO₂ is carried as bicarbonate** (70 %), not carbamino (23 %).

## Chapter summary

- Air passes nostrils → nasal cavity → pharynx → larynx → trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli; the conducting part is lined by **pseudostratified ciliated columnar** epithelium, the alveolus by **simple squamous**.
- The **trachea** has 16–20 **C-shaped hyaline** rings and divides at the carina; the **right bronchus** is wider, shorter and more vertical, so it takes inhaled objects.
- The **right lung has three lobes, the left two**; the pleura holds the lung against the chest wall with a **negative intrapleural pressure**.
- **Alveoli** number 300–500 million, give **70–80 m²** of surface and a **0.5 μm** barrier; **type II cells** make **surfactant**, without which premature babies get RDS.
- **Inspiration is active** (diaphragm, phrenic C3–C5, plus external intercostals); **quiet expiration is passive**.
- Volumes: **TV 500, IRV 3000, ERV 1100, RV 1200 mL**; **VC = TV + IRV + ERV = 4600**; **TLC = VC + RV = 5800**; FRC = ERV + RV.
- Gas exchange is by **simple diffusion** down partial-pressure gradients: alveolar pO₂ **104**, venous **40**; venous pCO₂ **45**, alveolar **40**; CO₂ diffuses about **20×** faster than O₂.
- **O₂**: 97 % on haemoglobin, 3 % dissolved; the **sigmoid curve** has **P₅₀ ≈ 27 mmHg** and shifts **right** with CO₂, H⁺, heat and 2,3-BPG (**Bohr effect**).
- **CO₂**: **70 % bicarbonate** (carbonic anhydrase, chloride shift), 23 % carbamino, 7 % dissolved; **Haldane effect**.
- The **medulla** sets the rhythm and responds mainly to **CO₂/H⁺**; the carotid and aortic bodies respond to a **fall in O₂**.
- **Asthma** is reversible obstruction, **chronic bronchitis** is cough with sputum, **emphysema** destroys alveolar walls (↑ RV), **pneumonia** consolidates alveoli and **TB** causes apical caseous cavities; **smoking** paralyses cilia, adds CO and tar.

### Model answer D1: Respiratory organs and breathing mechanism (10 marks)

**Introduction.** **Respiration** includes breathing (external), gas exchange and the use of oxygen in cells (cellular respiration). The human respiratory tract carries air from the nose to the lungs.

**Respiratory organs.**

1. **Nose and nasal cavity:** hairs and mucus filter dust; the lining warms and moistens the air.
2. **Pharynx and larynx:** the larynx (**voice box**) has the vocal cords; the **epiglottis** covers it while swallowing.
3. **Trachea (about 12 cm):** held open by **C-shaped cartilage rings** and lined by ciliated epithelium that sweeps mucus upward.
4. **Bronchi and bronchioles:** the trachea divides into two bronchi, which branch like a tree into bronchioles.
5. **Alveoli:** about **300 million** thin-walled air sacs surrounded by capillaries. Their total surface is about **70–100 m²**, so exchange is efficient.
6. **Lungs and pleura:** the right lung has three lobes and the left two. A double **pleural membrane** with fluid reduces friction.

**Mechanism of breathing.**

| Step | Inspiration | Expiration |
|---|---|---|
| Diaphragm | Contracts, flattens | Relaxes, dome-shaped |
| External intercostal muscles | Contract, ribs up and out | Relax, ribs down and in |
| Thoracic volume | Increases | Decreases |
| Lung pressure | Falls below atmospheric | Rises above atmospheric |
| Air movement | Into the lungs | Out of the lungs |

Inspiration is active and expiration is normally passive. The **respiratory rate** is 12–16 per minute in an adult.

**Lung volumes (adult male, about).** Tidal volume (TV) 500 mL; inspiratory reserve volume (IRV) 3000 mL; expiratory reserve volume (ERV) 1100 mL; residual volume (RV) 1200 mL. Then **vital capacity = TV + IRV + ERV = 4600 mL** and **total lung capacity = VC + RV = 5800 mL**. **Pulmonary ventilation** = TV × rate = 500 × 12 = **6000 mL/min**. Of each breath about 150 mL fills the dead space, so the alveolar ventilation is (500 − 150) × 12 = 4200 mL/min.

**Conclusion.** A large, moist, thin surface and pressure changes caused by the diaphragm and rib muscles make breathing effective.

### Model answer D2: Gas exchange, transport and regulation (10 marks)

**Introduction.** Gases move by **diffusion** from a region of higher partial pressure (pp) to lower pressure, across the **respiratory membrane** (alveolar wall + capillary wall, about 0.5 μm thick).

**Exchange of gases.**

| Gas | Alveolar air (mm Hg) | Deoxygenated blood | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| O₂ | 104 | 40 | Alveolus → blood |
| CO₂ | 40 | 45 | Blood → alveolus |

In the tissues the process is reversed: pO₂ in the cells is lower than in blood, and pCO₂ is higher, so O₂ leaves the blood and CO₂ enters it.

**Transport of oxygen.** About **97%** is carried as **oxyhaemoglobin** (HbO₂), as each haemoglobin molecule binds four O₂ molecules, and 3% is dissolved in plasma. One gram of Hb binds 1.34 mL of O₂, so blood with 15 g Hb per 100 mL carries about 15 × 1.34 = **20 mL O₂ per 100 mL**. The **oxygen dissociation curve** is S-shaped. A high pCO₂, low pH or high temperature shifts it to the right, so Hb releases more O₂ to active tissues (**Bohr effect**).

**Transport of carbon dioxide.**

1. **About 70% as bicarbonate:** in red cells carbonic anhydrase speeds CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H⁺ + HCO₃⁻. Bicarbonate moves into plasma while chloride enters the cell (**chloride shift**).
2. **20–25% as carbamino-haemoglobin** (CO₂ attached to Hb).
3. **About 7% dissolved** in plasma.

**Regulation of breathing.** The **respiratory rhythm centre** in the **medulla oblongata** sets the basic rhythm. The **pneumotaxic centre** in the pons limits inspiration and smooths the rhythm. A rise in **CO₂ or H⁺** is detected by the **chemosensitive area** of the medulla and raises the rate; low O₂ affects the **carotid and aortic bodies**. This is why breathing speeds up during exercise and at altitude, such as in trekkers above 4000 m in Nepal.

**Disorders.** **Asthma** (airway narrowing), **bronchitis**, **emphysema** (alveolar walls break), **pneumoconiosis** (dust in quarry workers), and **tuberculosis** are common; smoke from firewood and cigarettes raises the risk.

**Conclusion.** Pressure differences drive the exchange, haemoglobin and bicarbonate carry the gases, and the medulla keeps the blood gases steady.
