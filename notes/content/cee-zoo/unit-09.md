The nervous system and sense organs are rated at **2 questions** in this
40-question Zoology paper. The examinable pattern is
mostly “which structure does this job?”: **medulla** for vital centres,
**cerebellum** for coordination, **hypothalamus** for homeostasis, nodes of
Ranvier for saltatory conduction, rods and cones for vision, and the organ of
Corti for hearing. The second pattern is directional logic — sensory fibres
enter by the **dorsal root**, motor fibres leave by the **ventral root**, and
chemical synapses transmit in one direction only.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- **CNS = brain + spinal cord**. **PNS = 12 pairs of cranial nerves + 31 pairs of spinal nerves**.
- Cerebrum: conscious thought, memory and voluntary movement; **cerebellum**: balance and coordination; **hypothalamus**: temperature, hunger, thirst and pituitary control; **medulla**: breathing, heart and blood-pressure centres.
- Sympathetic outflow is **thoracolumbar**, usually adrenergic and “fight or flight”; parasympathetic outflow is **craniosacral**, cholinergic and “rest and digest”.
- Resting potential is about **−70 mV**; threshold about **−55 mV**; depolarisation is Na⁺ entry, repolarisation is K⁺ exit; the Na⁺/K⁺ pump moves **3 Na⁺ out for 2 K⁺ in**.
- Myelin and nodes of Ranvier give **saltatory conduction** — faster and cheaper in ATP. At a chemical synapse, Ca²⁺ entry releases transmitter vesicles.
- Eye: **rods** for dim light, **cones** for colour and detail, **fovea** for sharpest vision, **blind spot** where the optic nerve leaves.
- Ear: **malleus → incus → stapes → oval window → cochlea**; semicircular canals detect rotation, utricle and saccule detect gravity and linear acceleration.

## 9.1 Central, peripheral and autonomic nervous systems

The nervous system is the body's rapid communication network. Its signals
travel as electrical impulses along neurons and act within milliseconds.
Unlike hormones, whose effects may persist for minutes or hours, a nervous
signal is brief, precisely addressed and quickly switched off.

### The neuron: the signalling cell

A **neuron** is the structural and functional unit of the nervous system. Its
**cell body (cyton/perikaryon)** contains the nucleus and Nissl substance
(rough endoplasmic reticulum) for protein synthesis. Short branched
**dendrites** receive signals and conduct them toward the cell body. A single
long **axon** conducts the action potential away from the cell body to axon
terminals or synaptic knobs. Most mature neurons have lost the capacity for
ordinary cell division, so their metabolic cell body must maintain processes
that may be more than a metre long.

Neurons are classified functionally as:

- **Sensory (afferent) neurons** — receptors to CNS.
- **Motor (efferent) neurons** — CNS to muscles or glands.
- **Interneurons/relay neurons** — connect neurons within the CNS and are
  responsible for integration.

Supporting **neuroglial cells** protect and maintain neurons. In the PNS,
**Schwann cells** form myelin; in the CNS, **oligodendrocytes** do so.
Astrocytes help maintain the blood–brain barrier and extracellular ion
balance, while microglia act as immune/phagocytic cells.

### Organisation at a glance

| Division | Main parts | Basic role |
|---|---|---|
| **Central nervous system (CNS)** | brain and spinal cord | receives, integrates and stores information; issues commands |
| **Peripheral nervous system (PNS)** | cranial and spinal nerves | carries impulses between CNS, receptors and effectors |
| **Somatic nervous system** | sensory input and motor output to skeletal muscle | voluntary movement and conscious sensation |
| **Autonomic nervous system (ANS)** | sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions | involuntary control of viscera, glands, cardiac and smooth muscle |
| **Enteric nervous system** | nerve plexuses in gut wall | largely local control of digestion, modulated by ANS |

### Brain: protection and major centres

The human brain weighs about 1.3–1.4 kg and lies in the cranium. It is
surrounded by three meninges: tough outer **dura mater**, middle
**arachnoid mater**, and delicate inner **pia mater**. Cerebrospinal fluid
(CSF), formed mainly by the **choroid plexuses** in the ventricles, cushions
the CNS, reduces its effective weight and helps maintain a stable chemical
environment.

| Brain region | High-yield functions |
|---|---|
| **Cerebrum/cerebral cortex** | intelligence, learning, memory, language, conscious sensation and voluntary movement |
| **Frontal lobe** | motor cortex, planning, personality; Broca's area for speech production |
| **Parietal lobe** | touch, pressure, pain, temperature and body position |
| **Temporal lobe** | hearing; Wernicke's area for language comprehension |
| **Occipital lobe** | visual processing |
| **Corpus callosum** | large commissural tract joining the two cerebral hemispheres |
| **Thalamus** | major relay and sorting station for sensory pathways to cortex |
| **Hypothalamus** | body temperature, hunger, thirst, sleep–wake rhythm, autonomic control and regulation of pituitary secretion |
| **Midbrain** | visual/auditory reflexes, eye movements and relay pathways |
| **Pons** | relay between cerebrum and cerebellum; helps regulate breathing |
| **Cerebellum** | posture, balance, muscle tone and fine coordination of voluntary movement |
| **Medulla oblongata** | cardiac, respiratory, vasomotor, swallowing, coughing, sneezing and vomiting centres |

The cerebrum does not itself “feel” every sensation in a simple point-to-point
way; perception is constructed by networks. Nevertheless, for school and CEE
purposes the lobe associations above are the tested first approximation.
Damage to the **medulla** is immediately life-threatening because breathing and
cardiovascular reflexes fail; cerebellar damage characteristically produces
an unsteady gait, poor coordination and intention tremor rather than paralysis
or loss of consciousness.

### Spinal cord and reflexes

The spinal cord extends from the medulla through the vertebral canal to about
the level of the first or second lumbar vertebra in adults. In cross-section,
**grey matter** is central and H-shaped, containing neuron cell bodies,
dendrites and synapses; **white matter** lies outside and consists mainly of
myelinated ascending and descending tracts. This is the reverse of the
cerebrum, where grey matter forms the outer cortex.

Each spinal nerve is mixed. Its **dorsal root** carries sensory fibres into
the cord and bears the dorsal-root ganglion containing sensory cell bodies.
Its **ventral root** carries motor fibres out of the cord. A useful memory is
**SAME DAVE**: Sensory Afferent, Motor Efferent; Dorsal Afferent, Ventral
Efferent.

A **reflex arc** is the shortest functional pathway of an involuntary reflex:

**receptor → sensory neuron → CNS integration → motor neuron → effector**

In withdrawal from a hot object, nociceptors excite sensory neurons; spinal
interneurons activate flexor motor neurons and inhibit antagonistic extensors;
the hand moves before ascending signals produce conscious pain. The knee-jerk
is a simpler **monosynaptic** stretch reflex: muscle-spindle sensory neurons
synapse directly on quadriceps motor neurons. Reflexes are rapid, stereotyped
and protective, but higher centres can modify many of them.

### Cranial and spinal nerves

Humans have **12 pairs of cranial nerves** and **31 pairs of spinal nerves**
(8 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral and 1 coccygeal). Especially
testable cranial nerves are:

| Nerve | Main school-level association |
|---|---|
| I Olfactory | smell |
| II Optic | vision |
| III Oculomotor | most eye movements; parasympathetic pupil constriction and accommodation |
| V Trigeminal | facial sensation and muscles of mastication; largest cranial nerve |
| VII Facial | facial expression, taste from anterior tongue, salivary/lacrimal glands |
| VIII Vestibulocochlear | hearing and balance |
| X Vagus | extensive parasympathetic supply to thoracic and abdominal viscera |
| XII Hypoglossal | tongue movements |

### Autonomic nervous system

Autonomic pathways usually contain two neurons in series: a **preganglionic
neuron** from CNS to an autonomic ganglion and a **postganglionic neuron** from
ganglion to effector. Both divisions often innervate the same organ and act in
a balanced, frequently antagonistic manner.

| Feature | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
|---|---|---|
| CNS origin | thoracic and upper lumbar cord (**thoracolumbar**) | brainstem cranial nerves III, VII, IX, X and sacral cord (**craniosacral**) |
| Preganglionic fibres | usually short | usually long |
| Postganglionic fibres | usually long | usually short; ganglia near or in target organ |
| Main postganglionic transmitter | noradrenaline (norepinephrine), with notable cholinergic exceptions such as most sweat glands | acetylcholine |
| General state | emergency, exercise, “fight or flight” | recovery, digestion, “rest and digest” |
| Heart | rate and force increase | rate decreases, mainly through vagus |
| Pupil | dilates | constricts |
| Bronchial smooth muscle | relaxes; airways widen | contracts; airways narrow |
| Gut motility/secretion | decreases | increases |
| Urinary bladder | detrusor relaxes; storage favoured | detrusor contracts; emptying favoured |

The **adrenal medulla** is functionally a modified sympathetic ganglion: its
chromaffin cells release adrenaline and noradrenaline into blood, prolonging
and generalising sympathetic effects. Most systemic blood vessels receive
important sympathetic vasoconstrictor tone but little or no parasympathetic
innervation, so “every organ receives equal and opposite supply” is too simple.

::: caution The vagus is not the whole parasympathetic system
The vagus supplies much of the thorax and abdomen, but the eye, glands of the
head and pelvic viscera depend on other cranial or sacral parasympathetic
pathways. Likewise, acetylcholine is not exclusively parasympathetic: it is
the transmitter at autonomic ganglia, at the neuromuscular junction and in
many CNS synapses.
:::

## 9.2 The nerve impulse and the synapse

### Resting potential and action potential

An unstimulated axon maintains a **resting membrane potential** of about
**−70 mV** inside relative to outside. The sodium–potassium ATPase pumps
**3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in**, while leakage of K⁺ through potassium channels makes
the inside negative. Large intracellular anions that cannot cross the membrane
also contribute.

When a stimulus depolarises the membrane to threshold, roughly **−55 mV**,
voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open regeneratively. Na⁺ rushes in and the inside
becomes positive, peaking around +30 to +40 mV. This is
**depolarisation**. Na⁺ channels then inactivate and voltage-gated K⁺ channels
open; K⁺ leaves, restoring negativity in **repolarisation**. A brief excess
K⁺ outflow may cause **hyperpolarisation**. The pump maintains long-term ionic
gradients; it does not create each individual spike beat by beat.

### All-or-none behaviour, refractoriness and coding

Below threshold, only a local graded potential occurs. At or above threshold,
a full action potential of characteristic amplitude is produced: the
**all-or-none law**. Increasing stimulus intensity therefore does not make a
single impulse larger; it increases its **frequency** and recruits additional
sensory fibres.

During the **absolute refractory period**, inactivated Na⁺ channels cannot
reopen, so no second action potential can be triggered. This limits maximum
firing frequency and helps ensure forward propagation. During the following
**relative refractory period**, a stronger-than-usual stimulus may succeed.

### Conduction and saltatory transmission

Local currents depolarise adjacent membrane, propagating the action potential
without decrement. Diameter and myelination determine speed. Large myelinated
mammalian fibres may conduct at about 100 m s⁻¹; thin unmyelinated pain fibres
may conduct below 2 m s⁻¹. In a myelinated axon, voltage-gated channels are
concentrated at the **nodes of Ranvier**; the signal is regenerated from node
to node in **saltatory conduction**. Myelin also reduces ion leakage across
internodes, saving pump ATP.

::: example Reading a nerve-conduction study
A motor nerve is stimulated at the wrist and then at the elbow. The muscle
responds after 3.2 ms and 9.2 ms respectively. The stimulation sites are
42 cm apart. The neuromuscular delay is common to both trials, so subtract:

$$v=\frac{0.42\ \text{m}}{(9.2-3.2)\times10^{-3}\ \text{s}}=70\ \text{m s}^{-1}$$

This is characteristic of a myelinated motor fibre. The subtraction is the
examinable trick: do not divide by either raw latency alone.
:::

### Chemical synaptic transmission

At a chemical synapse, the presynaptic terminal and postsynaptic membrane are
separated by a synaptic cleft of roughly 20 nm. Transmission follows a strict
sequence:

1. An action potential depolarises the presynaptic knob.
2. Voltage-gated **Ca²⁺ channels** open and Ca²⁺ enters.
3. Synaptic vesicles fuse and release neurotransmitter by exocytosis.
4. Transmitter diffuses across the cleft and binds postsynaptic receptors.
5. Ion channels open or intracellular signalling changes, producing an
   excitatory or inhibitory postsynaptic potential.
6. Transmitter is removed by enzymatic breakdown, reuptake or diffusion.

An **EPSP** depolarises the postsynaptic cell toward threshold; an **IPSP**
hyperpolarises it or stabilises it away from threshold. EPSPs may add by
**temporal summation** (rapid signals from one terminal) or **spatial
summation** (signals from several terminals). Major transmitters include
acetylcholine, glutamate, GABA, glycine, dopamine, noradrenaline and
serotonin. The same molecule can excite one target and inhibit another because
the **receptor**, not the transmitter alone, determines the response.

Because vesicles are presynaptic and receptors postsynaptic, a chemical
synapse is normally one-way. Electrical synapses, joined by gap junctions,
are faster and may be bidirectional, but they are less flexible.

### Neuromuscular junction and applied synapse facts

At the skeletal neuromuscular junction, acetylcholine opens nicotinic
receptors in the motor end plate, generating an end-plate potential and then a
muscle action potential. **Acetylcholinesterase** rapidly hydrolyses ACh to
terminate contraction.

| Agent/condition | Synaptic lesion | Functional result |
|---|---|---|
| Botulinum toxin | blocks vesicle release of ACh | flaccid paralysis |
| Curare-like blockers | block nicotinic ACh receptors | muscle cannot be excited; flaccid weakness |
| Organophosphate insecticides | inhibit acetylcholinesterase | ACh persists; excessive glandular/muscle stimulation, then depolarisation block |
| Myasthenia gravis | autoimmune loss/blockade of ACh receptors | fatigable skeletal-muscle weakness |


::: caution Trap — depolarisation is Na⁺ in, repolarisation is K⁺ out
When the threshold (~ −55 mV) is reached, voltage-gated **Na⁺ channels open and Na⁺ rushes in** — that upstroke is **depolarisation** (towards +30 mV). Then **K⁺ channels open and K⁺ leaves**, restoring the **resting potential (~ −70 mV)** — that downstroke is **repolarisation**. MCQs that make K⁺ *entry* cause the impulse have the traffic backwards. Two permanent companions: the impulse is **all-or-none**, and **saltatory** conduction at the nodes of Ranvier is why myelinated fibres are faster.
:::

## 9.3 The eye and the ear

A **receptor** is a specialised cell or ending that transduces one form of
energy into receptor potentials. The eye contains **photoreceptors** for light;
the ear contains **mechanoreceptors** for sound pressure, head rotation,
gravity and linear acceleration.

### Structure of the eye

The eyeball has three coats:

1. **Sclera** — tough outer protective layer; its transparent anterior part is
   the **cornea**, which supplies most of the eye's refractive power.
2. **Choroid/uvea** — vascular, pigmented middle layer that absorbs stray
   light; anteriorly it forms the **ciliary body** and pigmented **iris**.
3. **Retina** — inner neural layer containing photoreceptors and processing
   neurons.

The **pupil** is not a structure but the opening in the iris. Circular
sphincter muscles under parasympathetic control constrict it in bright light;
radial dilator muscles under sympathetic control widen it in dim light or
arousal. Aqueous humour, secreted by ciliary processes into the anterior
segment, nourishes the avascular cornea and lens and drains mainly through the
canal of Schlemm. Blocked drainage can raise intraocular pressure in
**glaucoma**. The jelly-like **vitreous humour** fills the posterior cavity
and helps maintain shape.

### Focusing and accommodation

The cornea provides the greatest fixed bending of light because of the large
refractive-index change at the air–cornea surface. The lens provides fine
adjustment. For a distant object, ciliary muscles relax, suspensory zonules
are taut, and the lens is relatively thin. For a near object, the **ciliary
muscle contracts**, the zonular fibres slacken, and the elastic lens becomes
more convex, increasing its power. This is **accommodation**. The retinal image
is real, inverted and smaller; the brain interprets it as upright.

| Defect | Optical problem | Usual correction |
|---|---|---|
| **Myopia** (short sight) | distant rays focus in front of retina; eyeball too long or optics too strong | diverging/concave lens (negative power) |
| **Hypermetropia** (long sight) | near rays would focus behind retina; eyeball too short or optics too weak | converging/convex lens (positive power) |
| **Presbyopia** | ageing lens/capsule loses accommodative flexibility | reading or bifocal/progressive lens |
| **Astigmatism** | unequal corneal or lens curvature in different meridians | cylindrical lens |
| **Cataract** | lens becomes opaque | surgical lens replacement |

### Retina: rods, cones and visual pathway

Light passes through retinal neurons before reaching photoreceptors near the
pigmented epithelium. Photoreceptor signals pass to bipolar cells and then
ganglion cells; ganglion-cell axons form the optic nerve. At the **optic
disc**, where these axons and retinal vessels exit, no photoreceptors exist,
creating the **blind spot**.

| Feature | Rods | Cones |
|---|---|---|
| Approximate number | about 120 million | about 6 million |
| Pigment | rhodopsin | three classes of photopsin/iodopsin |
| Best in | dim light | bright light |
| Colour | no colour discrimination | red, green and blue channels for colour vision |
| Detail | poorer acuity; extensive convergence increases sensitivity | high acuity, especially in fovea |
| Distribution | abundant in peripheral retina | concentrated centrally; fovea almost exclusively cones |

Rhodopsin contains retinal derived from vitamin A; severe vitamin-A deficiency
can therefore impair dim-light vision (**night blindness**) and damage the
ocular surface. In the fovea, each cone has a relatively private pathway to the
brain, preserving fine spatial detail. Peripheral rods converge heavily,
sacrificing detail for sensitivity.

At the **optic chiasm**, fibres from the nasal half of each retina cross. Each
cerebral hemisphere therefore processes the opposite visual field. A pituitary
tumour pressing the central chiasm characteristically damages crossing nasal
fibres and causes loss of both temporal visual fields (bitemporal hemianopia).

### Structure and working of the ear

The ear has three divisions:

- **Outer ear:** pinna and external auditory canal collect and direct sound
  to the tympanic membrane.
- **Middle ear:** air-filled tympanic cavity containing **malleus, incus and
  stapes**. The Eustachian (auditory) tube connects it to the nasopharynx and
  equalises pressure across the eardrum.
- **Inner ear:** bony and membranous labyrinth containing the **cochlea** for
  hearing and vestibular apparatus for balance.

The ossicles solve an impedance-matching problem: sound in air would otherwise
reflect from the fluid-filled inner ear. Force collected over the relatively
large tympanic membrane is delivered by a lever system to the much smaller
oval window, increasing pressure roughly twentyfold. The stapes moves the oval
window; pressure waves travel through perilymph, displace the basilar
membrane, and are relieved at the round window.

Hair cells of the **organ of Corti**, resting on the basilar membrane, bend
against the tectorial membrane. This bending opens mechanically gated channels
and excites fibres of the cochlear division of cranial nerve VIII. The stiff
narrow **base** of the cochlea responds best to high frequencies; the flexible
wide **apex** responds best to low frequencies. Loudness is coded mainly by
amplitude of vibration, firing rate and recruitment of neurons.

### Balance

The three **semicircular canals** lie approximately in three perpendicular
planes. At the base of each, hair cells in the **crista ampullaris** project
into a gelatinous cupula. Head rotation makes endolymph lag behind, bends the
cupula and signals angular acceleration — **dynamic equilibrium**.

The **utricle and saccule** contain maculae whose hair cells are embedded in a
gelatinous sheet weighted by calcium-carbonate **otoliths**. Gravity or linear
acceleration shifts the sheet, signalling head position and straight-line
movement — **static/linear equilibrium**.

Middle-ear disease that dampens the drum or ossicles causes **conductive**
hearing loss. Damage to cochlear hair cells or the auditory nerve causes
**sensorineural** hearing loss. Excessive noise characteristically destroys
hair cells; unlike the ossicles, mature human cochlear hair cells regenerate
poorly.

## Formula and fact sheet

- CNS: brain and spinal cord. PNS: **12 cranial + 31 spinal nerve pairs**.
- Meninges outside → inside: **dura → arachnoid → pia** (“DAP”).
- Vital centres: **medulla**. Coordination: **cerebellum**. Homeostasis and
  pituitary link: **hypothalamus**. Sensory relay: **thalamus**.
- Spinal roots: dorsal = sensory/afferent; ventral = motor/efferent.
- Sympathetic = thoracolumbar; parasympathetic = craniosacral.
- Resting potential about −70 mV, threshold about −55 mV, peak about +35 mV.
- Depolarisation: Na⁺ inflow. Repolarisation: K⁺ outflow. Pump: 3 Na⁺ out,
  2 K⁺ in, using ATP.
- Conduction velocity: $v = \dfrac{\text{distance}}{\text{time difference}}$;
  subtract common delays in clinical two-point stimulation.
- Synaptic trigger: Ca²⁺ entry. Neuromuscular transmitter: acetylcholine.
  Terminating enzyme: acetylcholinesterase.
- Rods: rhodopsin, dim light, peripheral, low acuity. Cones: colour, bright
  light, fovea, high acuity.
- Near accommodation: ciliary contraction → zonules slacken → lens more convex.
- Myopia: concave/negative lens. Hypermetropia: convex/positive lens.
- Ear sequence: pinna → auditory canal → tympanum → malleus → incus → stapes
  → oval window → cochlea → organ of Corti → nerve VIII → temporal cortex.
- Semicircular canals: rotation. Utricle/saccule: gravity and linear motion.

## Chapter summary

- The CNS integrates information; the PNS connects it with receptors and
  effectors through cranial and spinal nerves.
- Cerebral cortex supports conscious perception and voluntary action, while
  the hypothalamus, cerebellum and medulla have sharply testable special roles.
- Spinal reflexes use dorsal sensory entry and ventral motor exit and can act
  before conscious perception.
- Sympathetic and parasympathetic divisions differ in origin, fibre length,
  transmitter and typical visceral effects.
- An action potential is an all-or-none, self-propagating reversal of membrane
  potential driven first by Na⁺ entry and then by K⁺ exit.
- Myelination permits rapid saltatory conduction between nodes of Ranvier.
- Chemical synapses use Ca²⁺-triggered transmitter release and are one-way,
  summable and pharmacologically vulnerable.
- In the eye, the cornea supplies most fixed refraction, the lens accommodates,
  rods favour sensitivity and foveal cones favour acuity and colour.
- In the ear, ossicles amplify pressure into cochlear fluids; hair cells in
  the organ of Corti transduce sound, while vestibular hair cells signal
  rotation, gravity and linear acceleration.

### Model answer D1: The nervous system (10 marks)

**Introduction.** The nervous system coordinates body activity by electrical impulses and chemical messengers. It has the **central nervous system (CNS)**, the **peripheral nervous system (PNS)** and the **autonomic nervous system (ANS)**.

**Central nervous system.** The **brain** (about 1.4 kg) is protected by the skull and three **meninges** (dura, arachnoid, pia) with cerebrospinal fluid.

1. **Cerebrum:** the largest part (about 80%), with two hemispheres joined by the corpus callosum. The outer **cortex** controls thought, memory, speech, sensation and voluntary movement.
2. **Diencephalon (thalamus and hypothalamus):** the thalamus relays sensations, the hypothalamus controls temperature, hunger, thirst, and the pituitary.
3. **Cerebellum:** balance and smooth muscle movement.
4. **Medulla oblongata:** centres for heartbeat, breathing and reflexes such as sneezing and vomiting.
5. **Spinal cord:** grey matter inside (H-shaped) and white matter outside; conducts impulses and controls **reflex action**.

**Peripheral nervous system.** **12 pairs of cranial nerves** and **31 pairs of spinal nerves** link the CNS to the body. It has a sensory (afferent) and a motor (efferent) division.

**Autonomic nervous system.** It controls involuntary organs and has two parts that act in opposite ways.

| Feature | Sympathetic | Parasympathetic |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | Thoraco-lumbar outflow | Cranio-sacral outflow |
| Neurotransmitter | Noradrenaline | Acetylcholine |
| Heart rate | Increases | Decreases |
| Pupil | Dilates | Constricts |
| Digestion | Slows | Speeds |
| Effect | "Fight or flight" | "Rest and digest" |

**Reflex arc.** Receptor → sensory neuron → spinal cord (interneuron) → motor neuron → effector. The knee jerk is a two-neuron reflex arc.

**Conclusion.** The CNS integrates, the PNS carries messages and the ANS runs the internal organs automatically.

### Model answer D2: Nerve impulse and synapse (10 marks)

**Introduction.** A **nerve impulse** is a wave of electrical change that travels along the axon of a neuron.

**Resting potential.** The resting axon is **polarised**: it is positive outside and negative inside, about **−70 mV**. The **sodium–potassium pump** removes 3 Na⁺ and brings in 2 K⁺ using ATP, and the membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺ at rest.

**Action potential.**

1. **Stimulus:** if the stimulus reaches the **threshold** (about −55 mV), voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open.
2. **Depolarisation:** Na⁺ rushes in and the inside becomes positive (about **+30 mV**).
3. **Repolarisation:** Na⁺ channels close, K⁺ channels open and K⁺ flows out, restoring −70 mV.
4. **Hyperpolarisation and recovery:** the potential dips slightly below −70 mV and the pump restores the ion balance.
5. **Propagation:** the local current excites the next patch of membrane. In **myelinated** fibres the impulse jumps from node to node (**saltatory conduction**), which is faster and saves energy.

The impulse obeys the **all-or-none law**, and during the **refractory period** a new impulse cannot start.

**Synapse.** A synapse is the junction between two neurons (axon terminal of the presynaptic neuron and dendrite of the post-synaptic neuron), separated by a **synaptic cleft** of about 20 nm.

1. The impulse reaches the terminal and opens **Ca²⁺ channels**.
2. Vesicles release a **neurotransmitter** (acetylcholine) by exocytosis.
3. It diffuses across the cleft and binds to receptors.
4. This opens ion channels and starts a new impulse.
5. The transmitter is broken down (acetylcholine by acetylcholinesterase) or taken back.

| Type | Medium | Speed | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chemical synapse | Neurotransmitter | Slower (0.5 ms delay) | One way |
| Electrical synapse | Ion flow via gap junctions | Very fast | Two way |

**Conclusion.** Ion movements create the impulse and neurotransmitters pass it between neurons, giving the nervous system its speed and one-way control.

### Model answer D3: The eye and the ear (10 marks)

**Introduction.** The eye is the organ of sight and the ear is the organ of hearing and balance.

**The eye.** It is a sphere about 2.5 cm across with three coats.

1. **Sclera** (white; at front the clear **cornea**, which bends light).
2. **Choroid** (dark, with blood vessels; forms the **iris** with the pupil, and the ciliary body).
3. **Retina** (inner coat): **rods** (dim light, about 120 million) and **cones** (colour, about 6 million); the **fovea** has the sharpest vision, and the **blind spot** has no receptors.

The **lens** is held by suspensory ligaments. The aqueous humour fills the front and the vitreous humour the back. **Image formation:** light passes through the cornea, aqueous humour, lens and vitreous, and forms an inverted real image on the retina; the brain turns it upright. **Accommodation:** for near objects the ciliary muscles contract and the lens becomes thicker.

**Defects:** myopia (short sight, concave lens), hypermetropia (long sight, convex lens), cataract (cloudy lens, treated by surgery, as in many Nepali eye camps).

**The ear.**

| Part | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|
| Outer ear | Pinna, auditory canal, ear drum | Collects and directs sound |
| Middle ear | Three ossicles (**malleus, incus, stapes**), Eustachian tube | Amplify vibrations; equalise pressure |
| Inner ear | **Cochlea** (organ of Corti), **semicircular canals**, utriculus and sacculus | Hearing and balance |

Sound vibrates the ear drum → ossicles → oval window → fluid in the cochlea → hair cells of the **organ of Corti** → auditory nerve → temporal lobe. The semicircular canals sense rotation and the utriculus and sacculus sense position.

**Conclusion.** The retina and the organ of Corti change light and sound into nerve impulses, and the brain turns them into sight and hearing.
