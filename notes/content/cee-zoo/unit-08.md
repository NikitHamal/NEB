The excretory system carries **2 of the 40 Zoology questions**. The paper builds
them from three blocks: the **organs and the nephron** (what each part is and
does), the **formation of urine** (filtration, reabsorption, secretion, the
counter-current multiplier and the three hormones that set urine volume), and
the **disorders** (nephritis, stones, failure, dialysis). Almost every question
turns on a direction, a number or a permeability: which limb lets water through,
which hormone makes urine small, which value is the renal threshold. The three
traps that decide marks are the **descending limb is permeable to water and the
ascending limb is not**, **ADH increases water reabsorption so its deficiency
gives a large dilute urine**, and **the posterior pituitary stores ADH but the
hypothalamus makes it**.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- **Organs**: a pair of kidneys, two ureters, the urinary bladder and the urethra, plus the accessory routes — **skin** (sweat), **lungs** (CO₂ and water vapour) and **liver** (urea from deamination, bile pigments).
- **Kidney in section**: capsule → cortex (glomeruli, convoluted tubules) → medulla (pyramids, loops, collecting ducts) → renal pelvis → ureter; about **1 million nephrons** per kidney.
- **Nephron**: Bowman's capsule with podocytes, PCT with a brush border, the loop of Henle, DCT with the macula densa, the collecting duct, and the peritubular capillaries/vasa recta.
- **Ultrafiltration**: pores and negative charge hold back cells and plasma proteins; net filtration pressure ≈ **10 mmHg**; GFR ≈ **125 mL min⁻¹** = **180 L day⁻¹**.
- **Reabsorption**: **100 % of glucose and amino acids** by active transport in the PCT; water by osmosis; the **counter-current multiplier** builds a gradient of **300 → 1200 mOsm L⁻¹**.
- **Secretion**: H⁺, K⁺, NH₃ and drugs; the tubule's route for acid–base control.
- **Hormones**: **ADH** (water, collecting duct), **aldosterone** (Na⁺ in, K⁺ out), **ANP** (Na⁺ and water out); each changes both the volume and the concentration of urine.
- **Disorders**: nephritis, calculi, acute and chronic failure, haemodialysis versus peritoneal dialysis, transplantation.
:::

## 8.1 Excretory organs and the structure of the nephron

### Excretion, egestion and secretion

**Excretion** is the removal of the waste products of metabolism from the body —
substances that were made inside cells and can no longer be used. It is not the
same as **egestion**, which is the removal of unabsorbed food from the gut, nor
as **secretion**, which is the release of a useful substance such as a hormone or
an enzyme.

The nitrogenous waste depends on the animal's habitat. **Ammonotelic** animals
(bony fish, aquatic invertebrates, tadpoles) excrete ammonia, which is very toxic
but dissolves easily in plentiful water. **Ureotelic** animals (man, mammals,
adult frogs, cartilaginous fish) spend energy converting ammonia to urea, which
is far less toxic and can be carried in a small volume of water.
**Uricotelic** animals (birds, reptiles, insects) convert it to uric acid, which
is almost insoluble and is passed as a paste — the best saving of water, at the
greatest energy cost.

### The organs

| Organ | Structure | What it removes |
|---|---|---|
| **Kidney** (a pair) | bean-shaped, retroperitoneal, T12–L3 | urea, uric acid, creatinine, excess salts, water, H⁺; also makes **renin** and **erythropoietin** |
| **Ureter** (a pair) | muscular tube, 25–30 cm, peristaltic | carries urine from the renal pelvis to the bladder |
| **Urinary bladder** | muscular sac with **transitional epithelium** and the **detrusor** muscle | stores urine (300–400 mL, up to 600–800 mL) |
| **Urethra** | female ≈ 4 cm, male ≈ 20 cm | carries urine out; in the male it also carries semen |

**Accessory excretory organs**

- **Skin** — sweat glands remove water, sodium chloride, urea and lactic acid; sebaceous glands remove sebum. Sweat is not a major route for nitrogen in man, but it matters in fever and heavy work.
- **Lungs** — remove carbon dioxide and water vapour at every breath.
- **Liver** — deaminates surplus amino acids and hands the ammonia to the urea cycle (ornithine cycle), so urea is made here; it also breaks down haemoglobin and excretes **bile pigments** (bilirubin, biliverdin) into bile, and detoxifies drugs and alcohol.
- **Large intestine** — carries bile pigments and cholesterol out in faeces.

::: caution Excretion is not only urine
A question that asks "which organ removes nitrogenous waste" may offer the
**liver** as well as the kidney. The liver *makes* urea; the kidney *excretes*
it. Both are correct answers to different stems, so read which verb is used.
The **spleen** is not an excretory organ — it destroys old red cells, but the
pigment it produces is passed to the liver.
:::

### Position, coverings and blood supply

Each kidney is about **10–12 cm long, 5–7 cm wide and 3 cm thick**, and weighs
**130–150 g**. It lies behind the peritoneum on the posterior abdominal wall.
The **right kidney is slightly lower** than the left because the liver pushes it
down. The medial border has a notch, the **hilum**, through which the renal
artery enters and the renal vein and ureter leave. The hilum opens into a
cavity, the **renal sinus**, filled with fat and the pelvis.

Three coverings protect it: the inner **fibrous capsule**, a middle **perirenal
fat** cushion and the outer **renal fascia**.

The blood supply is a trap in itself:

> **Renal artery → segmental → interlobar → arcuate → interlobular → afferent
> arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → peritubular capillaries or vasa
> recta → interlobular vein → arcuate → interlobar → renal vein.**

The kidney receives about **1.2 L of blood per minute**, roughly a quarter of the
cardiac output. The **efferent arteriole is narrower than the afferent**, so
blood damps up in the glomerulus and the pressure there stays high — that is what
drives filtration. The second capillary bed (peritubular and vasa recta) is at
low pressure, which is what allows reabsorption.

| **Renal pelvis** | funnel formed by major calyces | collects urine and passes it to the ureter |

### The nephron

A **nephron** is the structural and functional unit of the kidney; each kidney
holds about **one million**. There are two kinds. **Cortical nephrons** (about
85 %) sit high in the cortex with short loops that barely enter the medulla.
**Juxtamedullary nephrons** (about 15 %) lie near the medulla and have **long
loops** running deep into it; only they can build the steep gradient needed for
concentrated urine.

| Segment | Epithelium | Main job |
|---|---|---|
| **Bowman's capsule** | outer parietal layer of squamous cells; inner visceral layer of **podocytes** | receives the filtrate |
| **Proximal convoluted tubule (PCT)** | cuboidal with a **brush border** of microvilli and many mitochondria | **65–70 % of reabsorption**: all glucose and amino acids, most Na⁺, water and HCO₃⁻ |
| **Descending limb** | thin squamous | **water** leaves by osmosis; solutes stay |
| **Ascending limb** | thin then thick cuboidal | **impermeable to water**; Na⁺ and Cl⁻ pumped out (the "diluting segment") |
| **Distal convoluted tubule (DCT)** | cuboidal, no brush border | Na⁺ reabsorption under **aldosterone**, Ca²⁺ under PTH, K⁺ and H⁺ secretion |
| **Collecting duct** | principal cells and intercalated cells | water under **ADH**; final acid–base adjustment; urea recycling |
| **Peritubular capillaries / vasa recta** | endothelium | take back water and solutes; the vasa recta is a counter-current exchanger |

**The juxtaglomerular apparatus (JGA)** sits where the DCT touches its own
afferent arteriole. It has three parts: the **macula densa** (tall, crowded cells
of the DCT that sense Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in the filtrate), the **juxtaglomerular cells**
(smooth-muscle-like cells of the afferent arteriole that store **renin**) and the
extraglomerular mesangial cells between them. This is the sensor that starts the
renin–angiotensin system when blood pressure or sodium falls.

## 8.2 Formation of urine and its regulation

Urine is made in three processes, in this order: **ultrafiltration** in the renal
corpuscle, **selective reabsorption** along the tubule, and **tubular secretion**
from the blood into the tubule. A fourth process, **concentration**, happens in
the collecting duct under ADH.

### Step 1 — ultrafiltration

Blood pressure forces water and small solutes out of the glomerular capillaries
into Bowman's capsule. The barrier has three layers:

| Layer | Structure | What it holds back |
|---|---|---|
| **Fenestrated endothelium** | pores 70–100 nm | blood cells |
| **Basement membrane** | glycoproteins and collagen, **negatively charged** | large proteins; the charge repels albumin |
| **Podocyte slit diaphragms** | foot processes with slits about 4–7 nm | medium proteins |

So the filtrate is almost **protein-free**: water, glucose, amino acids, urea,
uric acid, creatinine, ions and small peptides pass; **cells and plasma proteins
do not**. Albumin (69 kDa) is held back by **both** its size and its negative
charge — a question that offers only "size" is incomplete when "size and charge"
is available.

The pressures at the glomerulus are:

$$ P_{\text{net}} = P_{\text{glomerular}} - (P_{\text{oncotic}} + P_{\text{capsular}}) = 55 - (30 + 15) = 10\ \text{mmHg} $$

| Pressure | Value |
|---|---|
| Glomerular capillary hydrostatic pressure | 55 mmHg (high because the efferent arteriole is narrow) |
| Plasma oncotic pressure (proteins pull water back) | 30 mmHg |
| Capsular hydrostatic pressure | 15 mmHg |
| **Net filtration pressure** | **10 mmHg** |

**GFR** (glomerular filtration rate) is about **125 mL min⁻¹**, i.e. **180 L
day⁻¹** — the whole plasma volume filtered about 60 times a day. The **filtration
fraction** is GFR ÷ renal plasma flow = 125 ÷ 625 = **20 %**.

::: example How much filtrate is reabsorbed
**Problem.** A student has a GFR of 125 mL min⁻¹ and passes 1.5 L of urine a day. What volume is reabsorbed, and what percentage is that?

**Solution.** Filtrate per day $= 125 \times 60 \times 24 = 180\,000$ mL $= 180$ L. Reabsorbed $= 180 - 1.5 = 178.5$ L. Percentage $= 178.5/180 \times 100 = 99.2\ \%$. So **about 99 % of the filtrate is taken back**; only 1 % leaves as urine.
:::

::: caution What is filtered is not what is excreted
The filtrate contains glucose, but normal urine does not, because the PCT
reabsorbs it. A stem that says "glucose is not filtered" is wrong; glucose
**is** filtered and then completely reabsorbed. The same is true of amino acids
and most bicarbonate.
:::

::: example Estimating GFR from creatinine clearance
**Problem.** A patient collects 1.5 L of urine in 24 hours. Urine creatinine is 100 mg/dL and plasma creatinine is 1.0 mg/dL. What is the creatinine clearance, and is it normal?

**Solution.** Amount excreted per day $= 100\ \text{mg/dL} \times 15\ \text{dL} = 1500$ mg. Clearance $=$ excretion ÷ plasma concentration $= 1500 \div 1.0 = 1500$ dL/day $= 150$ L/day. Divide by 1440 min: $150\,000/1440 = 104$ mL/min. A normal GFR is about 125 mL/min, so 104 mL/min is mildly reduced but near normal. Creatinine is used because it is filtered and hardly reabsorbed, so its clearance approximates the GFR.
:::

### Step 2 — selective reabsorption

Reabsorption is **selective**: the tubule takes back what the body needs and
leaves the waste. About **99 % of the water** and **99.5 % of the sodium** are
recovered, along with all the glucose and amino acids.

| Substance | Main site | Mechanism | Reabsorbed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Glucose** | PCT | **secondary active transport** with Na⁺ (SGLT), then facilitated diffusion out | 100 % below the threshold |
| **Amino acids** | PCT | active transport with Na⁺ | 100 % |
| **Na⁺** | PCT (65 %), ascending limb (25 %), DCT/CD (aldosterone) | Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase on the basolateral side drives it | 99.5 % |
| **Water** | PCT (65–70 %), descending limb, collecting duct (ADH) | **osmosis** | 99 % |
| **HCO₃⁻** | PCT | indirectly, as H⁺ is secreted | about 90 % |
| **Urea** | PCT and collecting duct | diffusion (some facilitated) | 40–50 % |
| **Creatinine** | — | not reabsorbed; some secreted | 0 % |

**Glucose and the renal threshold.** The carrier proteins can move only so much
glucose per minute; their limit is the **transport maximum (Tm)**, about
**375 mg min⁻¹**. As long as the filtered load stays below it, all glucose is
recovered. The plasma level at which the load reaches the Tm is the **renal
threshold**, about **180 mg/dL**. Above that, the carriers saturate and glucose
appears in the urine — **glycosuria**.

::: example Filtered load of glucose
**Problem.** Plasma glucose is 300 mg/dL and GFR is 125 mL min⁻¹. What mass of glucose is filtered each minute, and does any appear in the urine if the Tm is 375 mg min⁻¹?

**Solution.** 300 mg/dL $= 3$ mg/mL. Load $= 3 \times 125 = 375$ mg min⁻¹. This is exactly the Tm, so the carriers are just saturated and glucose begins to spill. At 400 mg/dL the load would be $4 \times 125 = 500$ mg min⁻¹, and about $500 - 375 = 125$ mg min⁻¹ would be lost.
:::

**Water and the counter-current multiplier.** Water is not pumped; it follows
solute by osmosis. The loop of Henle makes that possible by creating a
**gradient of osmolarity** in the medulla, from **300 mOsm L⁻¹** at the
corticomedullary junction to **1200 mOsm L⁻¹** (up to 1400) at the papilla. The
mechanism has four steps:

1. The filtrate entering the descending limb is **isotonic** (300).
2. The **descending limb is permeable to water but not to solute**. As it runs
   down into the salty medulla, water leaves by osmosis and the filtrate becomes
   steadily more concentrated, reaching about **1200** at the tip.
3. The **ascending limb is impermeable to water** but actively pumps **Na⁺ and
   Cl⁻** into the interstitium. Solute leaves, water cannot, so the filtrate
   becomes **dilute** — about **100** at the top.
4. The NaCl left behind in the medulla keeps the interstitium hypertonic, and
   the **vasa recta** carry away the water without washing the salt out. Because
   the two limbs carry fluid in **opposite directions** and have **opposite
   permeabilities**, the gradient is multiplied along the length of the loop.

The length of the loop sets the steepness of the gradient. Desert mammals such
as the kangaroo rat have very long loops and make urine of 5000 mOsm L⁻¹; birds
have short loops and concentrate weakly; fish have no loop at all. The same
logic explains why a long loop means a dry habitat.

**The DCT and collecting duct.** The DCT receives **hypotonic** fluid (about
100 mOsm L⁻¹). Here Na⁺ is reabsorbed under **aldosterone** and Ca²⁺ under
**parathyroid hormone**. The collecting duct is where the final decision is
made: if **ADH** is present, its cells insert **aquaporin-2** channels and water
leaves into the hypertonic medulla, so the urine becomes concentrated; if ADH is
absent, the duct stays almost impermeable and a large volume of dilute urine is
passed.

::: caution The two limbs are not interchangeable
The **descending** limb is permeable to **water** and not to solute; the
**ascending** limb is permeable to neither water nor (freely) solute, but
**pumps salt**. Any option that says "the ascending limb reabsorbs water" or
"the descending limb pumps sodium" is wrong. If both limbs behaved alike, the
gradient would vanish and no mammal could concentrate urine.
:::

### Step 3 — tubular secretion

Secretion moves substances **from the blood of the peritubular capillaries into
the tubule**, the opposite direction to reabsorption. It is the tubule's way of
getting rid of things that filtration alone cannot handle.

| Substance | Where secreted | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **H⁺** | PCT and intercalated cells of the collecting duct | regulates blood pH; can acidify urine to pH 4.5 |
| **K⁺** | principal cells of the DCT and collecting duct | the main route for potassium loss; increased by aldosterone |
| **NH₃** | PCT | combines with H⁺ to form NH₄⁺, trapping acid in the urine |
| **Creatinine** | PCT | a waste that is also filtered |
| **Drugs** | PCT | penicillin, aspirin, morphine, diuretics — many are protein-bound and never filtered |
| **Organic acids and bases** | PCT | e.g. para-aminohippuric acid (PAH), used to measure renal blood flow |

Secretion matters for two reasons. First, some wastes (K⁺, H⁺) are almost
entirely removed this way. Second, drugs bound to plasma proteins are too large
to filter, so secretion is their only exit.

### Hormonal regulation

Three hormones set the final volume and concentration of urine.

**ADH (vasopressin).** Made in the **supraoptic and paraventricular nuclei of
the hypothalamus**, carried down axons and **stored in the posterior pituitary**,
from which it is released. **Osmoreceptors** in the hypothalamus detect a rise
in plasma osmolarity (as little as 2 %), and ADH is released. It binds
V₂ receptors on the collecting duct and inserts **aquaporin-2** water channels,
so water is reabsorbed and the urine becomes **small and concentrated**. A fall
in osmolarity switches ADH off and a large dilute urine is produced.

**Diabetes insipidus.** A deficiency of ADH (or a failure of the kidney to
respond to it) gives **polyuria** — up to **15–20 L a day** of pale urine with a
specific gravity of **1.001–1.005** — and intense **polydipsia**. The urine is
dilute because the water is not reabsorbed, not because glucose is pulling water
into the tubule.

**Aldosterone.** A steroid from the **zona glomerulosa of the adrenal cortex**.
It increases **Na⁺ reabsorption** and **K⁺ secretion** in the DCT and collecting
duct; water follows the sodium. The result is a **small volume of urine** with
little sodium and more potassium, and a rise in blood pressure.

**The renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS).** When blood pressure, blood
volume or Na⁺ falls (or K⁺ rises), the **juxtaglomerular cells** release
**renin**. Renin converts **angiotensinogen** (from the liver) to
**angiotensin I**; **angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE)**, mostly in the
**lungs**, converts it to **angiotensin II**. Angiotensin II is a strong
vasoconstrictor and also stimulates **aldosterone** release, **ADH** release and
**thirst**. The net effect is to retain sodium and water and raise blood
pressure.

**ANP (atrial natriuretic peptide).** Stretch of the atria — as when blood volume
rises — releases this 28-amino-acid peptide from atrial muscle cells. ANP
**inhibits Na⁺ reabsorption** in the collecting duct, **inhibits renin and
aldosterone**, dilates the afferent arteriole and constricts the efferent one, so
**GFR rises**. The result is a **large volume of sodium-rich, dilute urine** and
a fall in blood pressure. It is the physiological opponent of the RAAS.

| Hormone | Source | Main action | Urine volume | Urine concentration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ADH** | hypothalamus → posterior pituitary | ↑ water permeability of DCT/CD (aquaporin-2) | ↓ | ↑ (up to 1200 mOsm L⁻¹) |
| **Aldosterone** | adrenal cortex, zona glomerulosa | ↑ Na⁺ reabsorption, ↑ K⁺ secretion | ↓ | ↑ (water follows Na⁺) |
| **ANP** | atrial muscle | ↓ Na⁺ reabsorption, ↓ renin, ↑ GFR | ↑ | ↓ (dilute) |
| **PTH** | parathyroid | ↑ Ca²⁺ reabsorption, ↓ phosphate reabsorption | little change | — |

::: memory The three hormones in one line
**ADH saves water, aldosterone saves salt (and wastes potassium), ANP throws
both away.** For each, ask two questions: *does urine volume go up or down?*
and *does the urine get more or less concentrated?*
:::

## 8.3 Renal disorders

### Nephritis

**Nephritis** is inflammation of the kidney. The commonest form is
**glomerulonephritis**, an **immune-complex (type III) reaction**: antibodies
made against a **β-haemolytic streptococcus** (after a throat or skin infection)
cross-react with the glomerular basement membrane, or antigen–antibody complexes
lodge there. It usually appears **1–3 weeks after** the infection.

Features: **haematuria** (smoky or cola-coloured urine), **proteinuria**,
**oedema** (especially around the eyes in the morning), **hypertension** and
**oliguria** (less urine). Most children recover; some progress to chronic renal
failure. **Nephrotic syndrome** is a related picture with heavy protein loss,
low blood albumin and generalised oedema.

### Kidney stones (renal calculi)

Stones form when salts in concentrated urine crystallise. About **75–80 % are
calcium oxalate**; others are uric acid, cystine and struvite (the last is
associated with infection). Risk factors are a **low fluid intake**, a hot
climate, hypercalcaemia, a diet high in oxalate (spinach, rhubarb, nuts, tea),
gout and repeated urinary infection.

A stone causes **renal colic** — severe pain from the loin to the groin as it
moves down the ureter — with **haematuria** and sometimes obstruction and
infection. Small stones pass with fluids and analgesia; larger ones are broken
by **extracorporeal shock-wave lithotripsy (ESWL)** or removed through a
ureteroscope.

### Renal failure

**Renal failure** is a fall in GFR severe enough to retain waste.

| | Acute renal failure | Chronic renal failure |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | hours to days | months to years |
| Common causes | shock, severe dehydration, blood loss, nephrotoxic drugs, obstruction | **diabetes mellitus** and **hypertension** (the two commonest), glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney |
| Reversible? | often, if the cause is corrected | no; progressive |
| Features | oliguria or anuria, rising urea and creatinine, high K⁺ | uraemia, anaemia (less erythropoietin), acidosis, oedema, bone disease |

When the kidneys fail, **urea and creatinine rise** (uraemia), **K⁺ rises**
(hyperkalaemia, dangerous for the heart), **H⁺ accumulates** (acidosis) and
**erythropoietin falls** (anaemia). Treatment is by dialysis or transplantation.

### Dialysis

Dialysis filters the blood through a semipermeable membrane against a
**dialysate** whose concentration is set so that wastes diffuse out while useful
substances stay.

| | Haemodialysis | Peritoneal dialysis |
|---|---|---|
| Membrane | cellulose or synthetic membrane in a dialyser | the **peritoneum** |
| Blood flow | pumped from an arteriovenous fistula | the patient's own capillaries |
| Schedule | 3–4 hours, 2–3 times a week, in hospital | 4–6 hour dwells, about 4 exchanges a day, at home |
| Efficiency | high | lower |
| Main risk | clotting (heparin used), hypotension, infection of the access | **peritonitis** |

In haemodialysis the blood and the dialysate flow in **opposite directions**
(counter-current), which keeps a concentration gradient along the whole membrane
so that diffusion does not stop halfway.

### Kidney transplantation

A healthy kidney from a **living related donor** or a **cadaver** is placed in
the iliac fossa and joined to the iliac vessels and the bladder. The donor and
recipient are matched for **ABO** and **HLA** antigens. The recipient takes
**immunosuppressant drugs** (ciclosporin, tacrolimus, steroids) for life to
prevent rejection. A working transplant gives a better quality of life and
longer survival than dialysis, but the supply of organs is far short of the
need.

::: tip The three numbers to hold
**125 mL min⁻¹** (GFR) → **180 L day⁻¹** (filtrate) → **1.5 L day⁻¹** (urine):
99 % reabsorbed. **180 mg/dL** is the renal threshold for glucose, **1200
mOsm L⁻¹** the maximum urine concentration. These three facts answer a large
share of the numerical questions.
:::

## Chapter summary

- The excretory organs are the **kidneys, ureters, bladder and urethra**; the **skin, lungs and liver** are accessory routes, and the liver *makes* urea while the kidney *excretes* it.
- Each kidney has about **one million nephrons**, in a **cortex** (corpuscles, convoluted tubules) and a **medulla** (pyramids, loops, collecting ducts) that drains through the **pelvis** into the ureter.
- **Ultrafiltration** at the glomerulus passes water and small solutes but holds back cells and protein by **pore size and negative charge**; net pressure ≈ **10 mmHg**, GFR ≈ **125 mL min⁻¹** (**180 L day⁻¹**).
- **Reabsorption** recovers about **99 %** of the filtrate: **glucose and amino acids by active transport in the PCT** (threshold **180 mg/dL**, Tm **375 mg min⁻¹**), water by **osmosis**.
- The **counter-current multiplier** works because the **descending limb is permeable to water** and the **ascending limb is not** but pumps salt; the medulla is graded **300 → 1200 mOsm L⁻¹**, and the vasa recta preserve the gradient.
- **Secretion** adds **H⁺, K⁺, NH₃, creatinine and drugs** to the tubule and is the main route for acid removal.
- **ADH** (made in the hypothalamus, stored in the posterior pituitary) concentrates urine; **aldosterone** retains Na⁺ and loses K⁺; **ANP** does the opposite. **Diabetes insipidus** = ADH problem = large dilute urine; **diabetes mellitus** = insulin problem = large glucose-containing urine.
- Abnormal constituents: **glucose** (diabetes), **protein** (nephritis), **ketones** (starvation, ketoacidosis), **blood** (nephritis, stones), **casts** (tubular damage).
- **Disorders**: nephritis, calcium oxalate stones, acute and chronic failure; **haemodialysis** uses a dialyser, **peritoneal dialysis** uses the peritoneum, and **transplantation** needs HLA matching and lifelong immunosuppression.

    (a) a concentration gradient is kept along the whole membrane (b) the blood is warmed (c) glucose can be added (d) clotting is prevented

::: note Answers and explanations — Set C
**1.** (b) — $125 \times 60 \times 24 = 180\,000$ mL = 180 L. Option (a) forgets the 60.
**2.** (d) — $(180 - 1.5)/180 = 0.992$, about 99 %.
**3.** (a) — 300 mg/dL = 3 mg/mL; $3 \times 125 = 375$ mg/min. This equals the Tm, so glucose just begins to spill.
**4.** (c) — load $= 4 \times 125 = 500$ mg/min; excreted $= 500 - 375 = 125$ mg/min. Option (b) is the load, not the loss.
**5.** (b) — clearance $= (100 \times 1500)/1.0 = 150\,000$ mL/day $= 104$ mL/min, close to the normal GFR.
**6.** (a) — a large volume of very dilute urine without glucose is diabetes insipidus; in diabetes mellitus the urine contains glucose and is concentrated.
**7.** (d) — nephrogenic diabetes insipidus: the hormone is present but the collecting duct does not respond, so the urine stays dilute.
**8.** (c) — low salt raises aldosterone, which reclaims sodium; water follows, so the volume is small.
**9.** (b) — the tubule secretes H⁺ to remove acid; potassium secretion (a) is increased in alkalosis, not acidosis.
**10.** (a) — counter-current flow keeps the concentration difference across the membrane all the way along, so diffusion continues.
:::
