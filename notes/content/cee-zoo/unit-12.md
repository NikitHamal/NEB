Microbial diseases and immunology are rated at **4 questions**—the largest
single block in Applied Zoology. The paper repeatedly tests a four-part chain:
**agent → transmission → cardinal feature → prevention**. It then asks for the
logic of immunity: innate versus adaptive, B cells versus T cells, antibody
classes, and why a booster or a secondary exposure produces a much stronger
response than the first encounter.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- Typhoid: *Salmonella* Typhi, faecal–oral, chronic gall-bladder carriage. TB: *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, airborne, acid-fast mycolic-acid wall, BCG is live attenuated *M. bovis*.
- Cholera: *Vibrio cholerae*, toxin-driven “rice-water” diarrhoea; immediate risk is dehydration and the key treatment principle is rehydration.
- HIV is an RNA retrovirus that destroys **CD4 helper T cells**; AIDS is the late immunodeficiency stage, classically CD4 below 200 cells mm⁻³ or an AIDS-defining illness.
- Influenza changes by **antigenic drift** (small mutations) and **shift** (major reassortment). Hepatitis A/E are mainly faecal–oral; B/C are mainly blood-borne and may become chronic.
- Candidiasis is an opportunistic fungal infection by *Candida albicans*, often part of normal microbiota until host control or microbial balance fails.
- IgM is first in a primary response; **IgG** dominates secondary responses and crosses the placenta; IgA protects secretions; IgE is linked to allergy.
- Vaccination produces **artificial active immunity**. Live attenuated vaccines replicate weakly; inactivated vaccines cannot replicate; toxoids are inactivated toxins.
:::

## 12.1 Typhoid, tuberculosis, HIV/AIDS, cholera, influenza, hepatitis and candidiasis

A **pathogen** is a microorganism or virus capable of causing disease in a
susceptible host. **Infection** means entry and multiplication; **disease**
means that infection or its toxins/immune consequences damage function enough
to cause signs and symptoms. Infection can therefore be asymptomatic, latent
or carried transmissibly without present illness.

```


| Agent group | Organisation | Chapter examples | Main attack principle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bacteria | prokaryotic cells, 70S ribosomes, cell wall | typhoid, TB, cholera | antibiotics can exploit bacterial structures/processes |
| Viruses | acellular nucleic acid + protein coat, sometimes envelope | HIV, influenza, viral hepatitis | vaccination and specific antivirals; antibiotics do not act on viruses |
| Fungi | eukaryotic cells, often chitinous wall | candidiasis | antifungal drugs target fungal membranes/walls/metabolism |

Transmission may be airborne/droplet, faecal–oral, blood/sexual, direct
contact, vector-borne, vertical (mother to child), or from the environment.
Prevention follows the route: interrupt that chain while treating the patient.

### Seven named diseases at examination depth

| Disease | Agent and transmission | Features/mechanism to recognise | Prevention or control |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Typhoid (enteric fever)** | *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhi; Gram-negative bacillus; contaminated food/water and human carriers | sustained/stepwise fever, headache, abdominal pain, constipation or diarrhoea, rose spots, possible intestinal bleeding/perforation; bacteria may persist in gall bladder | safe water, sanitation, food hygiene, typhoid conjugate vaccine (TCV); appropriate antibiotics because resistance occurs |
| **Tuberculosis** | *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*; airborne droplet nuclei from pulmonary/laryngeal TB | cough longer than 2 weeks, fever/night sweats, weight loss, haemoptysis; granuloma/caseous necrosis; latent infection may reactivate | BCG in infants, ventilation/masks, rapid diagnosis and complete combination therapy/DOTS strategy |
| **HIV/AIDS** | HIV-1/HIV-2, enveloped RNA retroviruses; sexual fluids, blood/sharing injecting equipment, mother to child | gp120 binds CD4/co-receptor; reverse transcriptase makes DNA and integrase inserts a provirus; progressive helper-T loss → opportunistic infections/cancers | safer sex, sterile equipment, screened blood, PrEP/PEP where indicated, maternal/infant prophylaxis, antiretroviral therapy (ART) |
| **Cholera** | *Vibrio cholerae* O1/O139; comma-shaped Gram-negative bacterium; faecal–oral/water | cholera toxin locks intestinal signalling on, causing massive Cl⁻ and water secretion; painless rice-water stools, vomiting, shock from dehydration | safe water/sanitation, oral cholera vaccine; immediate oral/IV rehydration saves life, antibiotics only adjuncts in selected severe cases |
| **Influenza** | influenza A/B viruses; segmented RNA genome, haemagglutinin (H) and neuraminidase (N); droplets/aerosols/contact | abrupt fever, myalgia, headache, cough/sore throat; drift causes seasonal change, shift may create pandemics | annual strain-matched vaccination, ventilation, respiratory hygiene; antivirals are most useful early/for high-risk patients |
| **Viral hepatitis** | HAV–HEV (details below) | liver inflammation: fatigue, nausea, right-upper-abdominal discomfort, dark urine and jaundice; outcome depends on virus | route-specific: WASH for A/E; HBV vaccination and blood/sexual precautions for B/C |
| **Candidiasis** | mainly *Candida albicans*, a dimorphic yeast; usually endogenous/opportunistic | oral white plaques (thrush), vulvovaginal itching/discharge, skin-fold infection; invasive blood infection in severely ill/immunocompromised hosts | control diabetes/immunosuppression, judicious antibiotics/steroids, catheter care; antifungal treatment |

### Three disease mechanisms students confuse

**Tuberculosis and acid-fastness.** The mycobacterial wall is rich in waxy
**mycolic acids**. In the Ziehl–Neelsen stain, hot carbol fuchsin enters this
wall and resists acid–alcohol decolourisation, so bacilli remain red on a blue
counterstain—hence **acid-fast bacilli (AFB)**. The wall also contributes to
slow growth and survival inside macrophages. BCG is live attenuated
*Mycobacterium bovis*, especially useful against severe childhood forms such
as miliary TB and TB meningitis; protection against adult pulmonary TB varies.

**Typhoid carriage.** *Salmonella* Typhi is adapted to humans. After recovery,
some people continue to harbour bacteria in the gall bladder and shed them in
faeces. Such a chronic carrier can contaminate food while feeling well—
explaining why sanitation and food-handler hygiene matter after symptoms end.

**Cholera is mainly a secretory, toxin-mediated loss of fluid.** The bacteria
usually do not need to invade the blood to kill. Their toxin raises cyclic AMP
in enterocytes and opens chloride channels; Na⁺ and water follow into the gut.
ORS works because intestinal Na⁺–glucose co-transport remains functional:
absorbed solute drags water back across the mucosa.

### HIV and the progression to AIDS

HIV carries RNA plus **reverse transcriptase**, integrase and protease. It
infects CD4-bearing helper T cells and some other immune cells. Because helper
T cells coordinate both antibody production and cytotoxic responses, their
loss progressively disables both arms of adaptive immunity. Acute infection
may resemble a flu-like illness. A long clinical-latency phase follows while
viral replication and immune damage continue. AIDS is the advanced stage, not
a separate virus.

HIV is **not** transmitted by casual touch, sharing food, coughing, toilets or
mosquito bites. ART does not normally eradicate integrated latent provirus,
but sustained suppression restores immune function, prevents AIDS, and a
person with a sustained undetectable viral load does not sexually transmit
HIV (“undetectable = untransmittable” for sexual transmission).

### Influenza: drift versus shift

- **Antigenic drift:** gradual point mutations in haemagglutinin/neuraminidase
  during replication; population immunity becomes less well matched; seasonal
  epidemics and annual vaccine updates follow.
- **Antigenic shift:** abrupt reassortment of genome segments when two
  influenza A viruses infect one cell; a substantially new H/N combination may
  meet little population immunity and cause a pandemic.

### Hepatitis: one word, several viruses

| Virus | Genome/route | Chronic infection? | Distinctive point |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HAV** | RNA; faecal–oral | no | acute, vaccine-preventable |
| **HBV** | partly double-stranded DNA; blood, sex, perinatal | yes | chronic hepatitis → cirrhosis/hepatocellular carcinoma; recombinant HBsAg vaccine |
| **HCV** | RNA; mainly blood | commonly | no licensed vaccine; modern direct-acting antivirals can cure most infections |
| **HDV** | defective RNA agent | only with HBV | requires hepatitis B surface antigen to assemble |
| **HEV** | RNA; mainly waterborne faecal–oral (some genotypes zoonotic/food-borne) | usually no in immunocompetent people | can be especially severe in pregnancy |

Jaundice reflects bilirubin accumulation and is a **sign**, not by itself a
complete diagnosis: the viral type and route determine prognosis and control.

### Candidiasis as an opportunistic infection

*Candida albicans* commonly lives harmlessly on mucosae. Disease emerges when
barriers break, competing bacteria are suppressed by broad-spectrum
antibiotics, glucose is poorly controlled, steroids/chemotherapy suppress
immunity, neutrophils fall, or HIV lowers CD4 numbers. Oral candidiasis in an
adult without a local explanation is therefore an important clue to immune
suppression. *Candida* is a fungus; antibacterial antibiotics do not treat it.

## 12.2 Innate and acquired immunity; antigens and antibodies

**Immunity** is the capacity to resist infection and limit damage. It has
innate mechanisms present before exposure and adaptive mechanisms that learn
from exposure.

### Innate immunity: immediate, broad and non-specific

1. **Physical/chemical barriers:** intact skin, mucus and cilia, tears and
   lysozyme, acidic stomach/vagina, antimicrobial peptides, normal microbiota.
2. **Phagocytes:** neutrophils and macrophages engulf microbes and debris.
3. **Inflammation:** histamine and other mediators increase blood flow and
   vascular permeability, producing redness, heat, swelling and pain while
   delivering cells and plasma proteins.
4. **Complement and interferons:** complement opsonises microbes, recruits
   cells and can lyse membranes; interferons warn neighbouring cells against
   viruses.
5. **Natural killer cells:** kill stressed/virus-infected cells without prior
   antigen-specific sensitisation.

Innate immunity responds within minutes to hours but has no conventional
immunological memory.

### Acquired/adaptive immunity: specific and remembered

Adaptive immunity is mediated by lymphocytes. **B cells** mature mainly in
bone marrow; **T cells** mature in the thymus. Antigen-presenting cells such
as dendritic cells and macrophages display peptide fragments to T cells.

- **Humoral immunity:** activated B cells proliferate into plasma cells that
  secrete antibodies and memory B cells. It is especially effective against
  extracellular microbes and toxins.
- **Cell-mediated immunity:** helper T cells (CD4) coordinate/cytokine-help;
  cytotoxic T cells (CD8) kill infected or abnormal body cells. It is vital
  against intracellular pathogens and in transplant rejection.

### Antigens and antibodies

An **antigen** is a molecule or molecular pattern specifically recognised by
an antibody, B-cell receptor or T-cell receptor. The precise region bound is
an **epitope/antigenic determinant**. A large molecule may carry many
epitopes. T cells usually recognise short peptide epitopes presented by MHC
molecules, whereas antibodies can bind shapes on intact molecules.

An **antibody (immunoglobulin)** is a Y-shaped glycoprotein made of two heavy
and two light chains. The variable tips of the two arms (**Fab** regions) bind
antigen; the stem (**Fc**) interacts with phagocytes/complement. Antibodies
protect by:

- **neutralising** toxins and blocking viral attachment;
- **opsonising** particles for phagocytosis;
- **agglutinating/precipitating** antigens into easier-to-clear complexes;
- activating **complement**; and
- directing antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity.

| Class | School-level identity |
|---|---|
| **IgG** | most abundant in blood/tissue fluids; main secondary-response antibody; only class that crosses human placenta in quantity |
| **IgM** | large pentamer; first prominent antibody in a primary response; strong agglutination/complement activation |
| **IgA** | dimeric in secretions—tears, saliva, mucus, colostrum/breast milk; mucosal protection |
| **IgE** | binds mast cells/basophils; allergy and defence against some parasites |
| **IgD** | mainly a receptor on naïve B cells; low serum concentration |

### Primary and secondary responses; active and passive immunity

On first exposure, rare matching lymphocytes must expand, so there is a lag of
several days; IgM appears first and total antibody is relatively modest. On
re-exposure, memory cells respond within 1–3 days, antibody rises much higher
and lasts longer, IgG predominates, and affinity is improved.

::: example Worked example — reading an antibody–response graph
A graph shows antibody concentration against time: after the first injection of antigen the curve climbs slowly to a modest peak over ~2 weeks, then falls; after a second injection months later it rises within days to a much higher, longer peak. Analyse it the CEE way:

- The slow, low curve is the **primary response** — naive B-cells are being selected and cloned; **IgM** appears first.
- The fast, high curve is the **secondary response** — **memory B-cells** formed earlier now respond, switching mainly to **IgG**; this is why booster doses work and why most childhood diseases strike only once.
- Any option saying the second peak is high "because more antigen was injected" misses the point: memory, not dose, is the difference.
:::

::: caution Trap — is it the antibody or the antigen being given?
**Vaccines give antigen** (live-attenuated, killed, or toxoid) and produce slow-onset, long-lasting **active** immunity; **antisera/immunoglobulin give ready-made antibody** (e.g. anti-tetanus serum, anti-snake venom) and produce immediate but short-lived **passive** immunity. The same confusion hides in "mother to baby": **IgG crosses the placenta**, while breast milk (colostrum) supplies **IgA** — both passive, neither a vaccine.
:::

## 12.3 Vaccines: live attenuated, inactivated and toxoid

A **vaccine** presents antigen in a controlled way so the adaptive system makes
memory without the danger of natural disease. The three syllabus types are
the foundation; modern subunit/conjugate/recombinant/vector/mRNA products use
the same immunological logic.

| Type | Construction and behaviour | Examples | Strengths/cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Live attenuated** | weakened live organism replicates to a limited extent | BCG, oral polio vaccine, measles–rubella | strong, broad and often long-lived humoral + cellular response; usually avoided in pregnancy/severe immunodeficiency |
| **Inactivated/killed** | whole organism destroyed by heat/chemicals/radiation; cannot replicate | inactivated polio (IPV), hepatitis A, rabies cell-culture vaccine | safer where replication is unacceptable; often needs adjuvant and booster doses |
| **Toxoid** | bacterial exotoxin chemically inactivated while retaining epitopes | tetanus toxoid, diphtheria toxoid | protects against toxin-mediated damage; boosters needed |
| **Subunit/recombinant/conjugate** | selected antigen, sometimes attached to a carrier protein | hepatitis B HBsAg, HPV, Hib, typhoid conjugate vaccine | focused safety; conjugation converts weak polysaccharide responses into T-dependent memory in infants |

Adjuvants enhance antigen presentation and inflammation so less antigen/more
durable memory can result. A **booster** is not a new primary lesson; it
recalls and expands memory clones, increasing antibody level and affinity.

### Herd immunity

When enough hosts are immune, an infectious person is likely to meet immune
rather than susceptible contacts, so chains of transmission die out. This
indirectly protects infants, people being treated for cancer and others who
cannot be vaccinated or respond poorly.

::: example Calculating an idealised herd-immunity threshold
For a homogeneously mixing population, the immune fraction at which each case
replaces itself on average is

$$p_c=1-\frac{1}{R_0}$$

If measles in a fully susceptible population has $R_0=15$,

$$p_c=1-\frac{1}{15}=0.933\approx93.3\%$$

If vaccine effectiveness were 95%, the perfectly uniform coverage required in
this simplified model would be $0.933/0.95\approx98.2\%$. Real populations mix
unevenly, so pockets of low coverage can sustain outbreaks below a reassuring
national average.
:::

::: caution Killed vaccines cannot “give the disease” by replication
An inactivated vaccine contains no replication-competent organism. Sore arm,
fever or malaise can still occur because innate immune activation itself
produces inflammatory symptoms. Conversely, live vaccines are usually safe in
healthy people but require caution when immune control of even a weakened
organism is unreliable.
:::

::: memory
**Active = taught (memory stays); passive = borrowed (fades in weeks).
Natural = life provides; artificial = medicine provides.** Four boxes, two
questions each: who taught, and who made the antibody.
:::

::: example Herd-immunity thresholds, exactly as the paper computes them
**Problem.** A disease with R₀ = 3 (each case infects three people in a
susceptible population) meets a vaccine. What vaccination coverage breaks
transmission? What about measles, with R₀ = 15?
**Solution.** Threshold = 1 − 1/R₀: for R₀ = 3, 1 − 1/3 = **66.7 %**; for
measles, 1 − 1/15 = **93.3 %**. The more infectious the germ, the higher the
wall of immunity it must meet — which is why measles returns first wherever
coverage slips.
:::

::: tip
Sort the vaccine shelf by what is in the vial: **a live but crippled germ
(attenuated), a dead germ (inactivated), a detoxified toxin (toxoid), or a
single piece (subunit)**. BCG and MMR are live; Salk polio and rabies are
killed; tetanus and diphtheria are toxoids; hepatitis B is a subunit.
:::

## Formula and fact sheet

- Typhoid—*Salmonella* Typhi; TB—*Mycobacterium tuberculosis*; cholera—*Vibrio cholerae*; candidiasis—*Candida albicans*.
- HIV—RNA retrovirus, gp120/CD4, reverse transcriptase, provirus, ART; AIDS is advanced immunodeficiency.
- Influenza drift = mutations; shift = reassortment of segmented genome.
- Hepatitis A/E = faecal–oral/acute; B/C = blood-borne/possibly chronic; D requires B. HBV is a DNA virus.
- Acid-fast stain: **red mycobacteria on blue background**; cause = mycolic acids.
- Innate = immediate/non-specific/no memory. Adaptive = specific, clonal, memory.
- B cells/plasma cells make antibodies; CD4 cells help; CD8 cells kill infected cells.
- IgM first/largest; IgG secondary/placental; IgA secretions; IgE allergy; IgD B-cell receptor.
- Active immunity makes memory (slow); passive gives ready antibodies (fast, short).
- Live attenuated: BCG/OPV/MR. Inactivated: IPV/HAV/rabies. Toxoid: tetanus/diphtheria.
- Herd threshold (idealised): $p_c=1-1/R_0$.

## Chapter summary

- The named diseases separate cleanly by agent class, route and mechanism:
  water-borne typhoid/cholera, airborne TB/influenza, blood/sexual HIV and
  HBV/HCV, and opportunistic candidiasis.
- TB's mycolic-acid wall explains acid-fast staining and macrophage survival;
  cholera toxin explains massive fluid loss without requiring bacteraemia.
- HIV disables adaptive immunity at its CD4 coordinator and converts normal
  microbiota or ordinarily controlled organisms into opportunistic threats.
- Influenza's segmented genome permits reassortment shifts as well as drift.
- Hepatitis viruses A–E share a target organ but differ in genome, route,
  chronicity and vaccine strategy.
- Innate defences act immediately and broadly; adaptive defences recognise
  epitopes specifically and remember them.
- Antibody classes have distinct timing, location and effector roles, with
  IgM first and IgG dominant later.
- Vaccines generate artificial active memory; their construction determines
  replication, strength, booster needs and cautions.

## Practice questions

**Set A — Recall (25 questions)**

1. Typhoid fever is caused by
   (a) *Vibrio cholerae* (b) *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (c) *Candida albicans* (d) *Salmonella* Typhi
2. The causative organism of tuberculosis is
   (a) *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (b) *Salmonella* Typhi (c) HIV (d) *Vibrio cholerae*
3. Cholera is caused by the comma-shaped bacterium
   (a) *Mycobacterium bovis* (b) *Salmonella* Typhi (c) *Candida albicans* (d) *Vibrio cholerae*
4. HIV primarily infects and destroys
   (a) red blood cells (b) platelets (c) epithelial cells only (d) CD4 helper T cells
5. Influenza is caused by a
   (a) bacterium (b) fungus (c) RNA virus (d) protozoan
6. Hepatitis B is transmitted most characteristically through
   (a) mosquito bites (b) contaminated drinking water (c) blood, sex and birth (d) respiratory droplets
7. Candidiasis is an infection caused mainly by a
   (a) yeast fungus (b) DNA virus (c) Gram-negative bacterium (d) tapeworm
8. Profuse painless “rice-water” stools are characteristic of
   (a) tuberculosis (b) influenza (c) cholera (d) hepatitis B
9. Mycobacteria are acid-fast chiefly because their walls contain abundant
   (a) cellulose (b) chitin (c) peptidoglycan only (d) mycolic acids
10. BCG vaccine contains live attenuated
   (a) *Mycobacterium bovis* (b) *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* (c) *Salmonella* Typhi (d) poliovirus
11. Immunity present from birth and non-specific is called
   (a) adaptive immunity (b) passive immunity (c) innate immunity (d) artificial immunity
12. A molecule specifically recognised by an antibody or lymphocyte receptor is an
   (a) enzyme (b) hormone (c) antigen (d) adjuvant
13. Antibodies are secreted in large quantity by
   (a) plasma cells (b) red blood cells (c) platelets (d) mast cells
14. The immunoglobulin class that crosses the human placenta is
   (a) IgM (b) IgA (c) IgE (d) IgG
15. The first prominent antibody class in a primary response is
   (a) IgG (b) IgM (c) IgA (d) IgD
16. The main antibody class in colostrum and mucosal secretions is
   (a) IgE (b) IgA (c) IgM (d) IgG
17. Allergy is associated especially with
   (a) IgD (b) IgM (c) IgA (d) IgE
18. Humoral immunity is based chiefly on
   (a) cytotoxic T cells (b) B cells and antibodies (c) neutrophils only (d) stomach acid
19. Infected body cells are killed directly mainly by
   (a) plasma cells (b) cytotoxic T cells (c) memory B cells (d) antibodies alone
20. A faster and stronger response on second exposure is due mainly to
   (a) memory lymphocytes (b) skin thickening (c) stomach acid (d) platelet increase
21. Which is a live attenuated vaccine?
   (a) tetanus toxoid (b) hepatitis B subunit (c) BCG (d) inactivated polio vaccine
22. Which contains a non-replicating whole pathogen?
   (a) BCG (b) inactivated polio vaccine (c) oral polio vaccine (d) measles–rubella vaccine
23. Tetanus vaccine used for active immunisation is a
   (a) live bacterium (b) ready-made antibody (c) toxoid (d) viral vector
24. Ready-made antiserum provides which type of immunity?
   (a) artificial passive (b) natural active (c) artificial active (d) innate cellular
25. Ordinary antibacterial antibiotics are ineffective against viruses mainly because viruses lack
   (a) proteins (b) bacterial walls and ribosomes (c) nucleic acids (d) host cells

::: note Answers and explanations — Set A
**1.** (d) — Typhoid is caused by *Salmonella enterica* serovar Typhi.
**2.** (a) — *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* is the principal cause of human TB.
**3.** (d) — *Vibrio cholerae* is a curved/comma-shaped Gram-negative rod.
**4.** (d) — HIV targets CD4 helper T cells and related immune cells.
**5.** (c) — Influenza viruses are segmented RNA viruses.
**6.** (c) — HBV spreads through blood, sexual fluids and from mother to child.
**7.** (a) — *Candida albicans* is a yeast-like fungus.
**8.** (c) — Cholera toxin causes profuse watery rice-water diarrhoea.
**9.** (d) — Waxy mycolic acids retain carbol fuchsin after acid–alcohol treatment.
**10.** (a) — BCG is an attenuated strain of *M. bovis*.
**11.** (c) — Innate defences are inherited, immediate and broad.
**12.** (c) — An antigen is the specifically recognised target; its bound region is an epitope.
**13.** (a) — Terminally differentiated B-lineage plasma cells secrete antibody.
**14.** (d) — IgG is transported across the placenta to the fetus.
**15.** (b) — IgM is the early dominant class in a primary response.
**16.** (b) — Secretory IgA protects mucosal surfaces and is abundant in colostrum.
**17.** (d) — IgE arms mast cells and basophils in allergic responses.
**18.** (b) — Humoral immunity means B-cell/antibody-mediated defence in body fluids.
**19.** (b) — CD8 cytotoxic T cells kill virus-infected and abnormal cells.
**20.** (a) — Memory B and T cells persist after first exposure.
**21.** (c) — BCG contains living but attenuated mycobacteria.
**22.** (b) — IPV is an inactivated/killed poliovirus vaccine.
**23.** (c) — Tetanus active vaccine is chemically inactivated toxin.
**24.** (a) — Transferred ready-made antibody is artificial passive immunity.
**25.** (b) — Viruses have no bacterial peptidoglycan wall or 70S ribosome for antibiotics to attack.
:::

**Set B — Understanding (15 questions)**

1. Selective toxicity of many antibiotics depends on bacteria possessing structures that are
   (a) identical to human nuclei (b) different from human-cell targets (c) made only of lipids (d) outside every cell
2. Seasonal influenza epidemics recur largely because viral surface antigens undergo
   (a) conjugation (b) binary fission (c) antigenic drift (d) endospore formation
3. A pandemic influenza strain is most likely after antigenic shift because reassortment creates
   (a) identical surface antigens (b) bacterial endotoxin (c) no viral envelope (d) a substantially new H/N combination
4. HIV impairs both humoral and cell-mediated immunity because it destroys the
   (a) red-cell precursor (b) plasma membrane of all cells (c) thymus at birth (d) helper T-cell coordinator
5. *Candida* often causes disease after broad-spectrum antibiotics or immunosuppression because it is normally
   (a) an obligate airborne virus (b) unable to live on mucosae (c) controlled microbiota that becomes opportunistic (d) carried only by mosquitoes
6. Which route pairing is correct?
   (a) HAV blood; HBV water (b) HEV sexual; HCV food (c) HBV droplets; HAV needles (d) HAV/HEV faecal–oral; HBV/HCV chiefly blood
7. A recovered typhoid patient may remain infectious because bacteria can persist and be shed from the
   (a) gall bladder in faeces (b) lungs in droplets (c) skin in sweat only (d) red cells by mosquitoes
8. Cholera can kill rapidly without high fever mainly because its toxin causes
   (a) massive intestinal fluid secretion (b) destruction of all villi (c) paralysis of respiratory muscles (d) complete liver failure
9. Latent tuberculosis differs from active tuberculosis because viable bacilli are
   (a) permanently transformed into viruses (b) present only outside the body (c) contained without current disease/transmission (d) unable ever to reactivate
10. Oral rehydration solution works in cholera because glucose promotes intestinal absorption of
   (a) antibodies through villi (b) bile into blood (c) sodium, with water following osmotically (d) bacteria into lymph
11. A secondary antibody response is larger and quicker chiefly because memory cells
   (a) replace all innate defences (b) eliminate the need for T cells (c) make only IgE (d) are already expanded and affinity-selected
12. Passive immunisation fades without memory because the recipient receives antibodies but does not
   (a) possess any blood (b) expand its own antigen-specific clones (c) recognise the injection site (d) make complement
13. Live attenuated vaccines often give broad cellular immunity but are used cautiously in severe immunodeficiency because the vaccine organism
   (a) is a purified toxoid (b) can still replicate (c) contains no antigen (d) destroys memory cells intentionally
14. Neutralising antibody protects most directly by
   (a) blocking pathogen/toxin binding to its target (b) raising stomach acidity (c) replacing cytotoxic T cells (d) digesting bacterial DNA itself
15. Herd immunity protects a non-immune person indirectly by
   (a) changing that person's genes (b) reducing chances of encountering the pathogen (c) giving ready-made IgM (d) sterilising the whole environment

::: note Answers and explanations — Set B
**1.** (b) — Antibiotics exploit prokaryote-specific walls, ribosomes and pathways.
**2.** (c) — Drift accumulates small H/N changes that partly evade prior immunity.
**3.** (d) — Shift can introduce novel haemagglutinin/neuraminidase combinations to humans.
**4.** (d) — CD4 help is needed for optimal B-cell and cytotoxic-T responses.
**5.** (c) — Disruption of bacterial competition or immune control permits *Candida* overgrowth/invasion.
**6.** (d) — A/E are enteric; B/C are predominantly blood-borne, with HBV also sexual/perinatal.
**7.** (a) — Chronic gall-bladder carriage leads to intermittent faecal shedding.
**8.** (a) — Toxin-driven chloride/water secretion causes hypovolaemic shock.
**9.** (c) — Latency is immunological containment, not necessarily sterilisation; reactivation can occur.
**10.** (c) — SGLT co-transports Na⁺ with glucose and water follows the absorbed solute.
**11.** (d) — Memory clones are numerous and their receptors have undergone affinity maturation.
**12.** (b) — Transferred antibody decays and does not create recipient memory cells.
**13.** (b) — Even attenuated organisms replicate, which may be hazardous without immune control.
**14.** (a) — Neutralisation prevents attachment/entry or toxin action.
**15.** (b) — High population immunity interrupts transmission chains.
:::

**Set C — Application (10 questions)**

1. A person has cough for three weeks, night sweats, weight loss and blood-streaked sputum. The first disease to exclude is
   (a) pulmonary tuberculosis (b) candidiasis (c) cholera (d) hepatitis A
2. In profuse rice-water diarrhoea with sunken eyes and weak pulse, the immediate life-saving principle is
   (a) withhold all fluids (b) give a live vaccine first (c) rapid fluid and electrolyte replacement (d) start antifungal therapy
3. A newborn is protected against several infections by maternal antibody acquired before birth. The class is mainly
   (a) IgM (b) IgA (c) IgG (d) IgE
4. For an idealised infection with $R_0=5$, the herd-immunity threshold is about
   (a) 80% (b) 50% (c) 20% (d) 95%
5. One bacterium doubles every 20 minutes under ideal conditions. After 3 hours, descendants number about
   (a) 512 (b) 64 (c) 128 (d) 9
6. An untreated HIV-positive patient has CD4 count 180 cells mm⁻³ and oral white plaques. The plaques most likely represent opportunistic
   (a) typhoid fever (b) influenza (c) hepatitis D (d) candidiasis
7. Serology early after a first infection shows high specific IgM but little IgG. This pattern indicates an early
   (a) passive response (b) allergic response (c) secondary response (d) primary response
8. A vaccine preparation consists of chemically inactivated tetanus toxin. It is classified as a
   (a) live attenuated vaccine (b) toxoid vaccine (c) passive antiserum (d) conjugate vaccine
9. Why is influenza vaccine reformulated frequently while measles vaccine is comparatively stable? Influenza undergoes rapid
   (a) conjugation (b) accommodation (c) antigenic drift (d) lysogeny only
10. A needle-stick injury carries greatest concern for transmission of
   (a) hepatitis A and cholera (b) blood-borne HBV, HCV and HIV (c) influenza and TB only (d) *Candida* by mosquito bite

::: note Answers and explanations — Set C
**1.** (a) — Chronic cough with systemic symptoms and haemoptysis is a classic pulmonary-TB presentation.
**2.** (c) — Rehydration corrects the hypovolaemia that causes death in cholera.
**3.** (c) — Maternal IgG crosses the placenta; milk mainly adds secretory IgA.
**4.** (a) — $p_c=1-1/5=0.80$, or 80% in the simplified homogeneous model.
**5.** (a) — Three hours is nine 20-minute generations; $2^9=512$.
**6.** (d) — Oral thrush due to *Candida* is a common opportunistic infection at low CD4 counts.
**7.** (d) — IgM dominance with little IgG is characteristic of an early primary response.
**8.** (b) — Inactivated toxin used as antigen is a toxoid.
**9.** (c) — H/N point mutations progressively change circulating influenza antigens.
**10.** (b) — Sharps transmit blood-borne viruses; HAV/cholera are faecal–oral.
:::
