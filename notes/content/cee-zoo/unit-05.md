The digestive system carries **2 of the 40 Zoology questions**. The paper asks it
in two ways: a **name-and-number** question (which cells secrete HCl, which enzyme
needs enterokinase, the pH of pancreatic juice, the dental formula) and a
**route** question (where is a nutrient absorbed, and into which vessel does it
go). Almost every mark sits in the enzyme table, the gland table and the
absorption routes. The traps are few but they are asked every year: **bile has no
enzymes**, **pepsin works only in acid and trypsin only in alkali**, and **most
absorption happens in the ileum, not the stomach**.

::: key What the paper asks from this chapter
- The **order of the canal** and the **epithelium** of each segment: stratified squamous (mouth, oesophagus, anal canal), simple columnar (stomach, intestine), and the special cells inside it (parietal, chief, goblet, Paneth, enteroendocrine).
- The **four secretions and their volumes**: saliva 1–1.5 L, gastric juice 2–3 L, bile 0.5–1 L, pancreatic juice 1–1.5 L per day, with their **pH** values.
- The **enzyme → substrate → product → site → optimum pH** table, including **enterokinase** (trypsinogen → trypsin) and the brush-border enzymes (maltase, sucrase, lactase, peptidases).
- **HCl** (five jobs), **mucus** (protection), **bile** (emulsification — never an enzyme) and **bicarbonate** (neutralising chyme).
- The **hormones**: **gastrin** (G cells, pyloric antrum, acid + protein), **secretin** (S cells, duodenum, acid → bicarbonate), **CCK** (I cells, fat → bile and pancreatic enzymes).
- **Absorption**: monosaccharides and amino acids into **blood capillaries** → **hepatic portal vein** → liver; fats as **chylomicrons** into **lacteals** → thoracic duct → subclavian vein.
- The applied trio: **peptic ulcer and *H. pylori***, **jaundice** (three types), **appendicitis**.
:::

## 5.1 The alimentary canal and the digestive glands

The **alimentary canal** is a single muscular tube about **8–9 m** long, open at
both ends, from mouth to anus. Its **digestive glands** — salivary, gastric,
intestinal, liver and pancreas — pour their secretions into it. The liver,
pancreas and salivary glands are *exocrine* here because their ducts open into
the canal.

### The plan of the canal

Every region of the canal is built on the same four coats, from the lumen
outwards:

| Coat | Tissue | Job |
|---|---|---|
| **Mucosa** (mucous membrane) | epithelium + lamina propria (loose connective tissue with capillaries and lymphatics) + muscularis mucosae | secretion, absorption, a first line of defence |
| **Submucosa** | loose connective tissue, blood and lymph vessels, **Meissner's plexus** | carries away absorbed food; supplies the mucosa |
| **Muscularis externa** | inner **circular** and outer **longitudinal** smooth muscle, with **Auerbach's (myenteric) plexus** between them | **peristalsis** and segmentation |
| **Serosa / adventitia** | mesothelium + connective tissue | reduces friction; suspends the gut |

The two nerve plexuses together form the **enteric nervous system**, the "little
brain" of the gut; it can run peristalsis on its own, but the **parasympathetic**
(vagus) speeds it up and the **sympathetic** slows it down.

### The mouth (buccal cavity)

- Lined by **stratified squamous non-keratinised epithelium**, which resists the
  abrasion of food.
- **Teeth**: an adult has **32** permanent teeth; the **dental formula** is
  **2·1·2·3 / 2·1·2·3 = 16 + 16** (incisors, canine, premolars, molars per half
  jaw). The deciduous (milk) set has **20** teeth, formula **2·1·0·2 / 2·1·0·2**.
  Teeth crush and grind food (**mastication**), giving the surface area that
  chemical digestion needs.
- **Tongue**: skeletal muscle covered with a mucous membrane; it mixes food,
  helps swallowing, and carries the **taste buds**.
- **Saliva** moistens the food, dissolves sapid substances so that they can reach
  the taste buds, and begins starch digestion. The ball of chewed food is the
  **bolus**.

**The salivary glands** are three pairs, and the paper asks which is which:

| Gland | Position | Duct | Secretion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Parotid** (largest) | in front of the ear, below the zygomatic arch | **Stensen's duct** opens beside the upper second molar | **serous** — watery, amylase-rich |
| **Submandibular** | under the lower jaw | **Wharton's duct** opens under the tongue | **mixed** (serous + mucous); most of the resting saliva |
| **Sublingual** (smallest) | floor of the mouth | ducts of **Rivinus** | mainly **mucous** |

Saliva is **1–1.5 L a day**, **pH 6.8** (range 6.2–7.4), about **99.5 % water**.
It contains **salivary amylase (ptyalin)**, **maltase** in small amount, the
glycoprotein **mucin** (which becomes mucus), **lysozyme** (kills bacteria),
**immunoglobulin A**, and the enzyme **lingual lipase** from the serous glands of
the tongue. Salivary amylase converts **starch → maltose** (plus dextrins) and
works best at pH 6.8; it is stopped by the acid of the stomach.

::: caution Saliva does not digest protein or fat to any useful extent
Lingual lipase is trivial in adults. Options such as "saliva digests protein" or
"saliva contains pepsin" are wrong. Saliva's protein enzyme is absent; its fat
enzyme is negligible.
:::

### The pharynx and the oesophagus

The **pharynx** is the shared passage for food and air; the **epiglottis** closes
the glottis during swallowing so that the bolus enters the oesophagus and not the
trachea.

The **oesophagus** is a muscular tube about **25 cm** long passing through the
diaphragm at the **cardiac sphincter**. It is lined by **stratified squamous
non-keratinised epithelium**, has **no digestive glands of its own** and secretes
no enzymes. Food moves down by **peristalsis** — a wave of contraction behind the
bolus — helped by gravity; the whole trip takes about 5–8 seconds. The cardiac
sphincter stops the acid contents of the stomach from running back; when it is
incompetent the result is **gastro-oesophageal reflux (heartburn)**.

### The stomach

The stomach is a J-shaped muscular bag holding **1.5–2 L**. Its regions are the
**cardia** (around the opening), the **fundus** (the dome above the opening), the
**body** and the **pyloric antrum**, which ends at the **pyloric sphincter**.

The mucosa is folded into **gastric pits** lined by **simple columnar epithelium**
made of several cell types:

| Cell | Secretion | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Surface mucous cells** | mucus + bicarbonate | a 1 mm gel layer that keeps HCl off the wall |
| **Parietal (oxyntic) cells** | **HCl** (pH 0.9–1.5 as secreted) and **intrinsic factor** | acid; intrinsic factor is needed to absorb **vitamin B₁₂** |
| **Chief (zymogenic) cells** | **pepsinogen**, **rennin** (infants), **gastric lipase** | protein and milk digestion |
| **G cells** (pyloric antrum) | **gastrin** | hormone — stimulates acid secretion |
| **Enterochromaffin-like cells** | histamine | amplifies acid secretion |
| **D cells** | somatostatin | inhibits acid secretion |

**Gastric juice** is **2–3 L a day** at **pH 1.5–2.5**. It contains HCl (about
0.5 %), pepsinogen, rennin in infants, gastric lipase, mucus and intrinsic
factor.

**HCl is not an enzyme.** It does five jobs:

1. **Converts pepsinogen → pepsin** (removes part of the molecule).
2. Gives pepsin its **acid optimum (pH 1.5–2)**.
3. **Kills most bacteria** swallowed with food — a chemical barrier.
4. **Denatures** dietary protein, unfolding it so that enzymes can reach the
   peptide bonds.
5. Stimulates the release of **secretin** and **CCK** when the acid chyme reaches
   the duodenum, and helps absorb a little calcium and iron.

**Pepsin** is the only proteolytic enzyme of the stomach. Secreted as inactive
**pepsinogen**, it is switched on by HCl and then activates more pepsinogen
(**autocatalysis**). It breaks proteins down to **peptones and polypeptides** —
never to amino acids. **Rennin (chymosin)** is present in the stomach of infants;
it curdles milk by converting **casein → calcium paracaseinate**, so that the milk
stays long enough to be digested. Adults have little rennin and curdle milk with
pepsin instead.

The stomach produces **chyme** — a soupy, acidic mixture — and passes it into the
duodenum in squirts through the pyloric sphincter. **Water, alcohol, some salts
and a few drugs (aspirin)** are absorbed here, but **almost no food**.

::: caution The stomach absorbs very little
A favourite wrong option is "most absorption occurs in the stomach". The stomach
absorbs water, alcohol and some drugs only. Over **90 %** of the absorption of
the products of digestion happens in the **small intestine**, mainly the
**ileum**.
:::

### The small intestine

The small intestine is about **6–7 m** long and is the organ of **digestion and
absorption**. It has three parts:

| Part | Length | Special features |
|---|---|---|
| **Duodenum** | about **25 cm**, C-shaped | receives the **bile duct** and the **pancreatic duct** at the hepatopancreatic ampulla; **Brunner's glands** in the submucosa secrete alkaline mucus |
| **Jejunum** | about **2.5 m** | longest villi, most digestion and absorption per cm |
| **Ileum** | about **3.5 m** | **Peyer's patches** (aggregated lymphoid follicles); the main site of **absorption** |

Its wall multiplies the absorptive surface three times over: **circular folds
(plicae circulares)**, then **villi** about 1 mm long, then **microvilli** (the
brush border) on every columnar cell. The total area becomes about **200 m²**,
roughly a tennis court.

The epithelium is **simple columnar** with **goblet cells** (mucus) and, at the
base of the glands, **Paneth cells** (antibacterial lysozyme). Between the villi
lie the **crypts of Lieberkühn**, simple tubular glands that supply new
epithelial cells every 3–5 days. Each villus holds a **blind-ended lymph vessel
(the lacteal)** in its centre, a **network of blood capillaries**, and smooth
muscle that makes it sway.

**Intestinal juice (succus entericus)** is about **2 L a day**, **pH 7.8–8.2**.
It is watery and alkaline, with mucus and the **brush-border enzymes**. The
enzymes are not really poured into the lumen — they sit **in the membrane of the
microvilli**, so that the last step of digestion happens as the nutrient touches
the cell that will absorb it.

### The large intestine

The large intestine is about **1.5 m** long and about 6 cm wide. It runs
**caecum → ascending colon → transverse colon → descending colon → sigmoid colon
→ rectum → anal canal**. Its epithelium is **simple columnar with very numerous
goblet cells**; there are **no villi** and no digestive enzymes.

- **Caecum**: a blind pouch below the ileocaecal junction, with the **ileocaecal
  valve** that stops backflow. In man it is small (about 6 cm) and its worm-like
  extension, the **vermiform appendix** (8–10 cm), is a **vestigial organ** — in
  herbivores the caecum is huge and digests cellulose.
- **Colon**: absorbs **water and mineral salts** (about 1.5 L of water a day) and
  compacts the residue into **faeces**. The resident **bacteria** ferment
  leftovers and **synthesise vitamin K and some B vitamins** (biotin, folate,
  B₁₂), which are absorbed here.
- **Rectum**: stores faeces until **defaecation**; the anal canal is lined by
  **stratified squamous epithelium** and guarded by two sphincters, the internal
  (involuntary, smooth muscle) and the external (voluntary, skeletal muscle).

### The liver and the gall bladder

The **liver** is the **largest gland** of the body — about **1.5 kg**, under the
diaphragm on the right. It is built of about a million **lobules**, each with a
central vein and plates of **hepatocytes** separated by **sinusoids** lined with
**Kupffer cells**. It has a **double blood supply**: the **hepatic artery**
(oxygen) and the **hepatic portal vein** (nutrients from the gut).

Its functions, in the order the paper asks them:

| Function | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Bile secretion** | 0.5–1 L a day; bile salts emulsify fat |
| **Carbohydrate metabolism** | stores **glycogen** (glycogenesis), breaks it down (glycogenolysis), makes glucose from amino acids and fat (**gluconeogenesis**) |
| **Protein metabolism** | deamination of amino acids, **urea formation** (ornithine cycle), synthesis of **albumin, globulin, prothrombin, fibrinogen** |
| **Fat metabolism** | oxidation of fat, formation of lipoproteins, cholesterol synthesis |
| **Storage** | **glycogen, vitamin A, D, E, K and B₁₂, iron** (as ferritin) |
| **Detoxification** | converts ammonia to urea, destroys drugs and alcohol, conjugates bilirubin |
| **Defence** | Kupffer cells phagocytose bacteria and old red cells |
| **Haemopoiesis** | in the fetus; stores iron for it later |

**Bile** is a golden-green, **alkaline (pH 7.6–8.6)** fluid, **0.5–1 L a day**,
with **no enzymes at all**. Its composition:

| Component | Amount / note | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **Bile salts** (sodium glycocholate, taurocholate) | the important part | **emulsify** fat; help absorb fatty acids and vitamins A, D, E, K |
| **Bile pigments** — bilirubin (yellow) and biliverdin (green) | from the breakdown of **haemoglobin** in the spleen | excretion only; give faeces their colour |
| **Cholesterol** | may crystallise into gallstones | excreted |
| **Lecithin, water, bicarbonate** | | solvent, neutraliser |

The **gall bladder** is a pear-shaped sac under the liver. It **stores bile** and
**concentrates it 5–10 times** by absorbing water. **Cholecystokinin (CCK)** from
the duodenum makes its smooth muscle contract and the **sphincter of Oddi**
relax, so bile squirts into the duodenum when fatty food arrives.

::: caution Bile is not an enzyme and does not digest fat
Bile **emulsifies** fat — it breaks a large drop into thousands of tiny ones, so
that the *surface area* for lipase rises enormously. The digestion itself is done
by **lipase**. "Bile contains lipase", "bile digests fat into fatty acids" and
"bile is acidic" are all wrong: bile is **alkaline** and has **no enzymes**.
:::

### The pancreas

The **pancreas** is a soft, lobulated gland lying behind the stomach, with its
head in the curve of the duodenum and its tail touching the spleen. It is
**heterocrine** — both exocrine and endocrine:

- **Exocrine part (about 99 %)**: acini of serous cells drain into ducts that join
  to form the **main pancreatic duct (duct of Wirsung)**, which opens with the
  bile duct at the hepatopancreatic ampulla.
- **Endocrine part**: the **islets of Langerhans** — β cells (insulin), α cells
  (glucagon), δ cells (somatostatin), PP cells.

**Pancreatic juice** is **1–1.5 L a day**, **pH 8.0–8.3**, and is the most
powerful digestive secretion. It contains:

| Enzyme | Action |
|---|---|
| **Pancreatic amylase** | starch → maltose |
| **Trypsin** (from trypsinogen) | proteins and peptones → peptides |
| **Chymotrypsin** (from chymotrypsinogen) | proteins → peptides |
| **Carboxypeptidase** (from procarboxypeptidase) | peptides → amino acids, from the C-terminal end |
| **Pancreatic lipase (steapsin)** | emulsified fats → fatty acids + glycerol |
| **Ribonuclease and deoxyribonuclease** | RNA and DNA → nucleotides |
| **Bicarbonate** | neutralises the acid chyme; brings the duodenal pH to about 8 |

The three proteases are secreted as inactive **zymogens**. **Trypsinogen** is
switched on by **enterokinase**; **trypsin** then switches on chymotrypsinogen and
procarboxypeptidase. This chain is a safety device: if the enzymes were made
active inside the pancreas they would digest it — the cause of **acute
pancreatitis**.

::: memory The three zymogen switches
**Enterokinase → trypsin → (chymotrypsin + carboxypeptidase).** Pepsinogen is
switched by **HCl**; trypsinogen by **enterokinase**. Never the other way round.
:::

### The glands and their secretions — one table

| Gland | Secretion | Volume / day | pH | Enzymes | Hormone that drives it |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Salivary** (parotid, submandibular, sublingual) | saliva | **1–1.5 L** | **6.8** | salivary amylase, maltase, lingual lipase | parasympathetic reflex (sight, smell, taste of food) |
| **Gastric** (parietal, chief, G cells) | gastric juice | **2–3 L** | **1.5–2.5** | pepsin, rennin, gastric lipase | **gastrin** |
| **Liver** | bile | **0.5–1 L** | **7.6–8.6** | **none** | **secretin** (volume), **CCK** (gall bladder contraction) |
| **Pancreas** | pancreatic juice | **1–1.5 L** | **8.0–8.3** | amylase, trypsin, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase, lipase, nucleases | **secretin** (bicarbonate), **CCK** (enzymes) |
| **Intestinal** (crypts, Brunner's) | succus entericus | **about 2 L** | **7.8–8.2** | maltase, sucrase, lactase, peptidases, enterokinase, intestinal lipase | local and vagal reflexes |

## 5.2 Physiology of digestion and absorption

**Digestion** is the breakdown of large, insoluble food molecules into small,
soluble ones that can cross the gut wall. It is **mechanical** (cutting,
grinding, churning, emulsifying) and **chemical** (hydrolysis by enzymes).
**Absorption** is the passage of the products through the mucosa into blood or
lymph. **Assimilation** is their use by the cells.

### Mechanical digestion

| Process | Where | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Mastication** (chewing) | mouth | breaks food up, mixes it with saliva, increases surface area |
| **Swallowing (deglutition)** | pharynx | the bolus is pushed into the oesophagus; the epiglottis guards the airway |
| **Peristalsis** | all of the canal | a wave of contraction behind the bolus moves it forward; it works even against gravity |
| **Segmentation** | small intestine | local rings of contraction chop and mix chyme with the enzymes; it does not move food along |
| **Churning** | stomach | the muscular wall kneads chyme and pushes it through the pylorus |
| **Emulsification** | duodenum | bile salts break fat into droplets of about 1 μm |

### Chemical digestion of carbohydrates

| Step | Enzyme | Site | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| starch → maltose + dextrin | **salivary amylase** | mouth | maltose, dextrin |
| starch → maltose | **pancreatic amylase** | duodenum | maltose |
| maltose → 2 glucose | **maltase** | brush border | glucose |
| sucrose → glucose + fructose | **sucrase** | brush border | glucose, fructose |
| lactose → glucose + galactose | **lactase** | brush border | glucose, galactose |

Cellulose is not digested at all: man has no **cellulase**, so it passes through
as **roughage (dietary fibre)** — which is not useless, because it gives the
colon something to work on and keeps the faeces soft.

### Chemical digestion of proteins

| Step | Enzyme | Site | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| protein → peptones, polypeptides | **pepsin** | stomach | peptones |
| peptones → peptides | **trypsin, chymotrypsin** | duodenum | peptides |
| peptides → amino acids (from the C-terminal end) | **carboxypeptidase** | duodenum | amino acids |
| peptides → amino acids (from the N-terminal end) | **aminopeptidase** | brush border | amino acids |
| dipeptides → amino acids | **dipeptidase** | brush border | amino acids |

Protein digestion therefore needs **three** pH environments in turn: acid in the
stomach, alkaline in the duodenum, and neutral at the brush border. This is why a
person whose pancreatic duct is blocked loses most protein digestion, and why the
faeces of such a patient contain undigested muscle fibres.

### Chemical digestion of fats

Fats are **not** digested in the mouth and hardly at all in the stomach (gastric
lipase acts only on already-emulsified butterfat, at pH 5–6). The sequence is:

1. **Emulsification** by **bile salts** in the duodenum — one large drop becomes
   many small ones, so the surface for lipase increases perhaps a thousandfold.
2. **Hydrolysis** by **pancreatic lipase**: triglyceride → **fatty acids +
   monoglycerides** (and some glycerol).
3. **Micelle formation**: bile salts gather round the fatty acids and
   monoglycerides and make water-soluble **micelles** about 5 nm across, which
   carry them to the brush border.
4. **Absorption** by simple diffusion into the epithelial cell.

### Chemical digestion of nucleic acids

Pancreatic **ribonuclease** and **deoxyribonuclease** split RNA and DNA into
nucleotides; intestinal **nucleosidases** and **phosphatases** complete the job to
**pentose sugar, phosphate and nitrogenous bases**.

### The parts played by HCl, mucus, bile and enterokinase

| Substance | Secreted by | Part played | What it is NOT |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HCl** | parietal cells | activates pepsinogen, gives pepsin its acid pH, kills bacteria, denatures protein, triggers secretin/CCK | **not an enzyme**; it digests nothing itself |
| **Mucus** | mucous cells of the whole canal | a viscous **glycoprotein** gel that lubricates and, with bicarbonate, keeps a **pH of about 7** at the cell surface; protects against acid, pepsin and mechanical injury | not a digestive juice |
| **Bile** | liver, stored in gall bladder | **emulsifies** fat; forms micelles; helps absorb vitamins A, D, E, K; neutralises acid chyme; excretes bilirubin and cholesterol | **no enzymes** |
| **Enterokinase** (enteropeptidase) | duodenal mucosa | converts **trypsinogen → trypsin**, the master switch of pancreatic proteolysis | not a digestive enzyme of food; it activates an enzyme |
| **Bicarbonate** | pancreas, Brunner's glands, bile | neutralises acid chyme, raises duodenal pH to about 8 | not an enzyme |

The stomach wall survives its own acid by a **mucosal barrier**: a thick mucus
layer, bicarbonate trapped in it, **tight junctions** between the surface cells
so that acid cannot leak between them, and a brisk blood flow that carries away
any H⁺ that gets through. **Prostaglandins** maintain this barrier — which is why
aspirin and other NSAIDs, which block prostaglandin synthesis, cause ulcers.

### The hormones of digestion

| Hormone | Secreted by | Stimulus | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Gastrin** | **G cells of the pyloric antrum** | food in the stomach — especially **protein** and distension; vagal stimulation | stimulates **parietal cells to secrete HCl** and chief cells to secrete pepsinogen; thickens the gastric mucosa |
| **Secretin** | **S cells of the duodenum** | **acid chyme** (pH below about 4.5) entering the duodenum | stimulates the pancreas to pour **bicarbonate-rich** juice; stimulates the liver to secrete bile; slows gastric emptying |
| **CCK (cholecystokinin)** | **I cells of the duodenum and jejunum** | **fatty acids and amino acids** in the duodenum | **gall bladder contraction** and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi; **pancreatic enzyme** secretion; slows gastric emptying; causes a feeling of fullness |
| Gastric inhibitory peptide (GIP) | duodenal K cells | glucose, fat | inhibits gastric secretion and motility; stimulates insulin |
| Motilin, somatostatin | gut | — | motility; inhibition |

The order to remember: **acid → secretin → bicarbonate**; **fat → CCK → bile and
enzymes**; **food in the stomach → gastrin → acid**.

::: caution Secretin and CCK are not "the other way round"
Secretin answers **acid** and makes the pancreas secrete **bicarbonate** (it has
almost no effect on enzymes). CCK answers **fat** and makes the gall bladder
**contract**. A stem that says "CCK stimulates bicarbonate secretion" is testing
exactly this swap.
:::

### Absorption

Absorption happens mostly in the **small intestine** — about **90 %** of it, and
most of that in the **ileum**, whose villi are numerous and whose transit is
slow. The **duodenum** absorbs iron and calcium; the **jejunum** absorbs most of
the monosaccharides and amino acids; the **ileum** absorbs vitamin B₁₂ (with
intrinsic factor), bile salts and the rest; the **colon** absorbs water, sodium
and chloride.

| Nutrient | Absorbed as | Site | Mechanism | Route |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbohydrates** | **monosaccharides** — glucose, galactose, fructose | jejunum, ileum | glucose and galactose by **secondary active transport** with Na⁺ (**SGLT-1**); fructose by **facilitated diffusion** (**GLUT-5**) | **blood capillary** → hepatic portal vein → liver |
| **Proteins** | **amino acids**, di- and tripeptides | jejunum, ileum | **Na⁺-dependent co-transport** (active) | **blood capillary** → hepatic portal vein → liver |
| **Fats** | **fatty acids and monoglycerides** (as micelles) | jejunum, ileum | **simple diffusion** into the epithelial cell, where they are re-esterified to triglyceride and packed with protein into **chylomicrons** | **lacteal** → lymph → thoracic duct → left subclavian vein |
| **Water** | water | small intestine (about 8 L a day), colon (1.5 L) | osmosis, following salts | blood capillary |
| **Salts** | Na⁺, Cl⁻, K⁺, HCO₃⁻ | mostly ileum, some colon | active transport and exchange | blood capillary |
| **Iron** | Fe²⁺ | **duodenum** | active transport, helped by vitamin C | blood → transferrin |
| **Calcium** | Ca²⁺ | duodenum | active transport, requires **vitamin D (calcitriol)** | blood |
| **Vitamins A, D, E, K** | with the fat | ileum | in **micelles** | **lacteal** |
| **Vitamin B₁₂** | with **intrinsic factor** | **ileum** | receptor-mediated endocytosis | blood |
| **Bile salts** | — | **ileum** | active transport (enterohepatic circulation) | portal vein → liver |

The mechanism of absorption is worth one more line, because it explains the
"against the gradient" questions: the epithelial cell keeps its internal Na⁺ low
by pumping Na⁺ out into the blood with the **Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase** of its basolateral
membrane. Na⁺ therefore keeps entering the cell from the lumen, and it drags
glucose or an amino acid in with it. The energy is spent on the pump, not on the
entry step — that is what "**secondary active transport**" means.

### The hepatic portal route

Every capillary in a villus drains into veins that join to form the **superior
mesenteric vein**, which unites with the **splenic vein** to form the **hepatic
portal vein**. This vein enters the liver and breaks up again into **sinusoids**,
so the liver sees the absorbed glucose and amino acids **before** any other organ
— the **first-pass** effect. The liver stores or converts what is needed and
releases the rest into the **hepatic veins → inferior vena cava → heart**.

Fats take a different road. A **chylomicron** is far too large to enter a blood
capillary, so it enters the **lacteal**, is carried through lymph nodes to the
**thoracic duct**, and joins the blood at the **left subclavian vein** — bypassing
the liver at first.

::: caution Fat does not go straight into the blood
The single most examined absorption fact: **fats → lacteals (lymph)**, everything
else water-soluble → **blood capillaries → hepatic portal vein**. "Fatty acids
are absorbed into the capillaries of the villus" is the standard wrong option.
:::

### Applied notes: ulcer, jaundice and appendicitis

**Peptic ulcer.** An ulcer is an open sore where acid and pepsin have eaten
through the mucosa. It occurs in the **stomach (gastric ulcer)** or, more often,
the **first part of the duodenum (duodenal ulcer)**. For decades the cause was
thought to be stress and excess acid; in **1982 Barry Marshall and Robin Warren**
showed that most ulcers are caused by the bacterium ***Helicobacter pylori***,
which lives in the mucus layer, splits urea with its **urease** and neutralises
acid around itself, and damages the barrier. The 2005 Nobel Prize followed. Other
causes are **NSAIDs** (aspirin, ibuprofen), smoking, alcohol and severe stress.
Symptoms: burning epigastric pain, worse when hungry in duodenal ulcer and after
food in gastric ulcer; complications are **bleeding, perforation and obstruction**.
Treatment: acid suppression (proton-pump inhibitors), antibiotics against
*H. pylori*, and stopping NSAIDs. **The ulcer is not caused by spicy food.**

**Jaundice.** Yellowing of the skin, sclera and mucous membranes when plasma
**bilirubin** rises above about **1 mg/dL** (normal 0.3–1.0 mg/dL). Bilirubin is
the yellow pigment made when haemoglobin is broken down in the spleen; the liver
conjugates it and passes it into bile, and the gut bacteria turn it into
**stercobilin**, which colours the faeces brown.

| Type | Cause | Plasma bilirubin | Urine | Faeces |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Pre-hepatic (haemolytic)** | too much haemoglobin broken down — malaria, incompatible transfusion, haemolytic anaemia of the newborn | **unconjugated** rises | no bilirubin, excess urobilinogen | normal or dark |
| **Hepatic (hepatocellular)** | liver cells damaged — viral hepatitis, alcohol, cirrhosis | both fractions rise | bilirubin present | pale |
| **Post-hepatic (obstructive)** | bile duct blocked — gallstone, cancer of the head of the pancreas | **conjugated** rises | dark, bilirubin present | **pale, clay-coloured** |

The **obstructive** type is the one to recognise: **dark urine, pale stools,
yellow skin and itch**. In the newborn, physiological jaundice appears in the
first week because the immature liver conjugates bilirubin slowly; it is treated
with blue light (**phototherapy**).

**Appendicitis.** Inflammation of the **vermiform appendix**, usually because its
lumen is blocked by a faecolith or lymphoid swelling. The classic story is pain
that begins around the **umbilicus** and settles in the **right iliac fossa** at
**McBurney's point**, with nausea, low fever and tenderness with guarding. The
danger is **perforation** and **peritonitis**, so the treatment is appendicectomy.
The appendix has no digestive function in man; it is a vestige of the large
caecum of herbivores, though it does contain lymphoid tissue.

### Worked examples

::: example How much HCl is in the stomach?
**Problem.** A fed person has 2.0 L of gastric juice at pH 2.0. How many moles and
how many grams of HCl does it contain?

**Solution.** pH = 2.0 means $[\mathrm{H^+}] = 10^{-2}$ mol L⁻¹. Since HCl is a
strong acid, it is fully dissociated, so the amount of HCl equals the amount of
H⁺:

$$n = cV = (10^{-2}\ \text{mol L}^{-1})(2.0\ \text{L}) = 0.020\ \text{mol}$$

$$m = nM = (0.020)(36.5) = 0.73\ \text{g}$$

About **0.73 g of pure HCl**, which is why the stomach needs a mucus barrier.
:::

::: example How much alkali neutralises that acid?
**Problem.** The same 0.020 mol of HCl reaches the duodenum. What mass of sodium
bicarbonate is needed to neutralise it?

**Solution.** NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂, so one mole of bicarbonate
neutralises one mole of acid. Required = 0.020 mol. M(NaHCO₃) = 23 + 1 + 12 +
3(16) = **84 g mol⁻¹**.

$$m = 0.020 \times 84 = 1.68\ \text{g}$$

The pancreas secretes far more than this in a day (about 1–1.5 L of juice at
pH 8.3), because acid keeps arriving with every meal.
:::

::: example The surface area of the small intestine
**Problem.** Treat the small intestine as a cylinder of radius 1.5 cm and length
5.0 m. Folds, villi and microvilli multiply its area about 450 times. Find the
plain area and the absorptive area.

**Solution.** Plain area $= 2\pi rL = 2\pi(0.015)(5.0) = 0.47\ \text{m}^2$.

Absorptive area $= 450 \times 0.47 = 2.1 \times 10^2\ \text{m}^2$, about
**200 m²** — the figure the textbooks quote, and roughly a tennis court. A
cylinder alone would give less than half a square metre, which is why the folds
matter.
:::

::: example Energy from a meal
**Problem.** A meal contains 250 g carbohydrate, 70 g protein and 60 g fat. Using
4 kcal g⁻¹ for carbohydrate and protein and 9 kcal g⁻¹ for fat, find its energy
in kcal and kJ.

**Solution.** Carbohydrate 250 × 4 = 1000; protein 70 × 4 = 280; fat 60 × 9 = 540.
Total = **1820 kcal**. In joules, 1820 × 4.184 = **7.6 × 10³ kJ** (7.6 MJ).
:::

::: tip Reading a digestion question
Three questions settle almost every stem. (1) **Which enzyme?** — and therefore
which pH. (2) **Where?** — mouth, stomach, duodenum or brush border. (3) **Into
what?** — blood capillary or lacteal. If the stem mentions bile, the answer is
emulsification, never digestion; if it mentions enterokinase, the answer is
trypsinogen → trypsin.
:::

## Chapter summary

- The canal is a **8–9 m** tube of four coats (mucosa, submucosa, muscularis, serosa); the **enteric plexus** runs peristalsis, modified by vagus (up) and sympathetic (down).
- The **mouth** has 32 permanent teeth, the **bolus** is made there, and **salivary amylase** (pH 6.8) starts starch digestion; the three gland pairs are parotid (Stensen's), submandibular (Wharton's) and sublingual (Rivinus).
- The **stomach** secretes 2–3 L of juice at pH 1.5–2.5: **parietal cells** give HCl and intrinsic factor, **chief cells** pepsinogen, rennin and lipase, **G cells** gastrin. HCl activates pepsinogen, kills bacteria and denatures protein; pepsin works only at pH 1.5–2 and stops at peptones.
- The **small intestine** (duodenum 25 cm, jejunum 2.5 m, ileum 3.5 m) receives bile and pancreatic juice, and its folds, villi and microvilli raise the absorptive area to about **200 m²**.
- The **liver** (1.5 kg, largest gland) makes **0.5–1 L of bile a day at pH 7.6–8.6 with no enzymes**; bile salts **emulsify** fat. The **gall bladder** stores and concentrates bile and contracts under **CCK**.
- **Pancreatic juice** (1–1.5 L, pH 8.0–8.3) supplies amylase, trypsin, chymotrypsin, carboxypeptidase, lipase, nucleases and bicarbonate. **Enterokinase** converts trypsinogen to trypsin, which activates the other two zymogens.
- **Hormones**: gastrin (food → acid), secretin (acid → bicarbonate), CCK (fat → bile + enzymes).
- **Absorption**: monosaccharides and amino acids into **blood capillaries → hepatic portal vein → liver**; fats as **chylomicrons into lacteals → thoracic duct**; B₁₂ in the ileum with intrinsic factor; water and salts in the colon.
- **Applied**: peptic ulcer is mostly caused by ***H. pylori***; **obstructive jaundice** gives dark urine and pale stools; **appendicitis** gives right iliac fossa pain at McBurney's point.

### Model answer D1: Alimentary canal and digestive glands (10 marks)

**Introduction.** The human digestive system has the **alimentary canal**, about 8–9 m long from mouth to anus, and the **digestive glands**. Digestion is the breaking of large insoluble food molecules into small soluble ones.

**Alimentary canal.**

1. **Mouth and buccal cavity:** the tongue mixes food with saliva. Humans have 32 permanent teeth; the dental formula is I 2/2, C 1/1, PM 2/2, M 3/3 (= 2123/2123 on each side).
2. **Pharynx and oesophagus:** the epiglottis closes the windpipe in swallowing. The oesophagus carries food by **peristalsis**.
3. **Stomach:** a J-shaped bag with cardiac, fundic and pyloric regions. Its inner wall has folds (**rugae**) and gastric glands: **chief cells** (pepsinogen), **parietal cells** (HCl and intrinsic factor) and mucous cells.
4. **Small intestine (about 6 m):** duodenum (C-shaped, receives bile and pancreatic juice), jejunum and ileum. The inner surface has **villi** and microvilli that give a huge absorbing area.
5. **Large intestine (about 1.5 m):** caecum with the **appendix**, colon (ascending, transverse, descending), rectum and anus. It absorbs water and salts and forms faeces.

**Digestive glands.**

| Gland | Secretion | Main function |
|---|---|---|
| Salivary (parotid, sub-mandibular, sub-lingual) | Saliva with amylase | Starch digestion, moistens food |
| Gastric glands | HCl, pepsinogen, mucus | Kill germs, digest protein |
| **Liver** (largest gland, about 1.5 kg) | **Bile** | Emulsifies fats; also stores glycogen, makes urea |
| Gall bladder | Stores bile | Releases bile into duodenum |
| **Pancreas** | Pancreatic juice (amylase, trypsin, lipase) | Digests all three food types |
| Intestinal glands | Intestinal juice | Final digestion |

**Role of the liver.** Bile salts have no enzyme, but they break fat into tiny droplets and make the medium alkaline for pancreatic enzymes. Bile pigments (bilirubin) give faeces their colour. Blockage of the bile duct causes **jaundice**.

**Conclusion.** Each part of the canal and each gland has a specific part in the continuous process of ingestion, digestion, absorption and egestion.

### Model answer D2: Digestion and absorption (10 marks)

**Introduction.** Digestion is **mechanical** (chewing, churning) and **chemical** (enzymes). The products are absorbed into blood or lymph, mainly in the small intestine.

**Digestion of carbohydrates.**

1. **Mouth:** salivary **amylase (ptyalin)** at pH about 6.8 changes starch to maltose.
2. **Duodenum:** pancreatic amylase completes the work.
3. **Ileum:** maltase, sucrase and lactase make **glucose, fructose and galactose**.

**Digestion of proteins.**

1. **Stomach:** HCl (pH 1.5–2.0) kills germs and activates **pepsinogen** to **pepsin**, which converts proteins to peptones. In infants rennin curdles milk.
2. **Duodenum:** **trypsin, chymotrypsin** and carboxypeptidase make smaller peptides.
3. **Ileum:** aminopeptidases and dipeptidases make **amino acids**.

**Digestion of fats.** **Bile salts emulsify** the fat. **Pancreatic lipase** changes it into fatty acids and glycerol (some monoglycerides). Intestinal lipase finishes the work.

| Food | Enzyme | Site | End product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Starch | Amylase | Mouth, duodenum | Maltose |
| Maltose | Maltase | Ileum | Glucose |
| Protein | Pepsin, trypsin | Stomach, duodenum | Peptides |
| Peptides | Peptidases | Ileum | Amino acids |
| Fat | Lipase (+ bile) | Duodenum | Fatty acids, glycerol |

**Absorption.** Most absorption occurs in the **villi** of the ileum. Each villus has a capillary network and a central **lacteal**. **Glucose and amino acids** enter the capillaries by diffusion and **active transport** and go through the hepatic portal vein to the liver. **Fatty acids and glycerol** form **micelles**, are rebuilt into **chylomicrons** in the epithelial cell and enter the lacteals, reaching the blood by the lymph. The large intestine absorbs water, mineral salts and some vitamins (K and B12 made by bacteria).

**Numerical.** A meal with 60 g carbohydrate, 20 g protein and 10 g fat gives (60 × 4) + (20 × 4) + (10 × 9) = 240 + 80 + 90 = **410 kcal**.

**Conclusion.** Digestion changes insoluble food into forms that pass through the villi, and the liver then controls what reaches the body.
