Groups B and C of the Grade 11 Physics paper are sixty-four of the seventy-five marks: eight questions of five marks and three of eight. This chapter gives you worked practice for both, arranged by the five content areas of the CDC syllabus — Mechanics, Heat and Thermodynamics, Waves and Optics, Electricity and Magnetism, and Modern Physics. Every answer is written the way you should write it in the hall: law first, substitution with units, answer boxed in words.

::: key How to use this chapter
- **Attempt first.** Cover the answer, write your own against the clock (12 minutes per 5-marker, 19 per 8-marker), then mark yourself against the model.
- **Split the marks.** A 5-mark numerical is usually law (1), substitution (2), arithmetic (1), unit and answer (1). An 8-mark question combines a derivation (3–4), a diagram (1–2) and a numerical (2–3).
- **Use the stated $g$.** Questions here say $g = 10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ or $9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$; use whatever your paper states.

## 3.1 Mechanics (units 1–8, 45 hours)

**Group B — Short answer (5 marks each)**

1. Using dimensional analysis, derive an expression for the time period $T$ of a simple pendulum, assuming it depends on its length $l$, the mass $m$ of the bob and the acceleration due to gravity $g$. State one limitation of the method. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Two forces of $10\ \text{N}$ and $6\ \text{N}$ act at a point with an angle of $60^{\circ}$ between them. Find the magnitude and direction of the resultant. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. A ball is projected with a speed of $20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ at $30^{\circ}$ above the horizontal ($g = 10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$). Find the time of flight, the maximum height and the horizontal range. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. A gun of mass $5\ \text{kg}$ fires a bullet of mass $20\ \text{g}$ at $400\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. State the principle you use and find the recoil velocity of the gun. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
5. State the laws of solid friction. A $20\ \text{kg}$ block rests on a horizontal floor with $\mu = 0.3$. Find the horizontal force needed to move it at constant speed ($g = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>
6. A $2\ \text{kg}$ body is released from rest at a height of $20\ \text{m}$ ($g = 10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$). Using energy, find its speed when it is $5\ \text{m}$ above the ground, and show that the total energy there equals the initial energy. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
7. Derive an expression for the acceleration due to gravity at a height $h$ above the Earth's surface. Find $g$ at a height $R/2$ ($g_0 = 9.8\ \text{m s}^{-2}$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>
8. A car of mass $1000\ \text{kg}$ rounds a level curve of radius $50\ \text{m}$ at $10\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Find the centripetal force, and the banking angle at which no friction is needed on this curve ($g = 10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>
9. A steel wire $2\ \text{m}$ long with cross-section $1\ \text{mm}^{2}$ is stretched by $1\ \text{mm}$ by a force of $100\ \text{N}$. Find the Young modulus and the elastic energy stored. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Answers — Group B, Mechanics
**1.** Let $T = k\,l^{a}m^{b}g^{c}$. Dimensions: $[T] = T$, $[l] = L$, $[m] = M$, $[g] = LT^{-2}$. So $T^{1}= L^{a}M^{b}L^{c}T^{-2c}$. Comparing: $M$: $b = 0$; $T$: $-2c = 1$ so $c = -\tfrac12$; $L$: $a + c = 0$ so $a = \tfrac12$. Hence $T = k\sqrt{l/g}$, independent of mass. *Limitation:* the dimensionless constant $k$ ($= 2\pi$) cannot be found by this method, and it fails if a quantity depends on a sum or a function such as $\sin\theta$.
**2.** $R = \sqrt{P^{2} + Q^{2} + 2PQ\cos\theta} = \sqrt{100 + 36 + 2(10)(6)(0.5)} = \sqrt{196} = 14\ \text{N}$. Direction from the $10\ \text{N}$ force: $\tan\alpha = \dfrac{Q\sin\theta}{P + Q\cos\theta} = \dfrac{6(0.866)}{10 + 3} = 0.400$, so $\alpha \approx 21.8^{\circ}$.
**3.** $T = \dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g} = \dfrac{2(20)(0.5)}{10} = 2.0\ \text{s}$. $H = \dfrac{u^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{2g} = \dfrac{400(0.25)}{20} = 5.0\ \text{m}$. $R = \dfrac{u^{2}\sin2\theta}{g} = \dfrac{400(0.866)}{10} = 34.6\ \text{m}$.
**4.** Principle: conservation of linear momentum (no external horizontal force). $0 = m_bv_b + Mv_g$, so $v_g = -\dfrac{m_bv_b}{M} = -\dfrac{0.020\times400}{5} = -1.6\ \text{m s}^{-1}$: the gun recoils at $1.6\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ opposite to the bullet.
**5.** Laws: (i) friction opposes relative motion; (ii) limiting friction is independent of the area of contact; (iii) $F \propto R$, $F = \mu R$; (iv) kinetic friction is less than limiting friction and independent of speed. $R = mg = 20\times9.8 = 196\ \text{N}$; $F = \mu R = 0.3\times196 = 58.8\ \text{N}$.
**6.** Fall of $15\ \text{m}$: $v^{2} = 2gh = 2(10)(15) = 300$, so $v = 17.3\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. At $5\ \text{m}$: $K = \tfrac12(2)(300) = 300\ \text{J}$, $U = mgh = 2(10)(5) = 100\ \text{J}$, total $400\ \text{J}$. Initially $U = 2(10)(20) = 400\ \text{J}$, $K = 0$. Equal, so mechanical energy is conserved.
**7.** At the surface $g_0 = GM/R^{2}$. At height $h$: $g = \dfrac{GM}{(R+h)^{2}}$, so $g = g_0\left(\dfrac{R}{R+h}\right)^{2} = g_0\left(1 + \dfrac{h}{R}\right)^{-2}$. For $h = R/2$: $g = 9.8\times\left(\dfrac{2}{3}\right)^{2} = 9.8\times\dfrac{4}{9} = 4.36\ \text{m s}^{-2}$.
**8.** $F = \dfrac{mv^{2}}{r} = \dfrac{1000\times100}{50} = 2000\ \text{N}$. With banking, $\tan\theta = \dfrac{v^{2}}{rg} = \dfrac{100}{50\times10} = 0.2$, so $\theta = 11.3^{\circ}$.
**9.** $Y = \dfrac{FL}{A\,\Delta l} = \dfrac{100\times2}{(1\times10^{-6})(1\times10^{-3})} = 2\times10^{11}\ \text{N m}^{-2}$. Energy $= \tfrac12F\Delta l = \tfrac12(100)(10^{-3}) = 0.05\ \text{J}$.
:::

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. (a) Derive the three equations of uniformly accelerated motion using the velocity–time graph. [5] (b) A car accelerates uniformly from $10\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ to $30\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ in $200\ \text{m}$. Find the acceleration and the time taken. [3] <span class="marks">[8]</span>
2. (a) Derive an expression for the orbital speed and period of a satellite at height $h$ above the Earth. [4] (b) What is a geostationary satellite? State its conditions. [2] (c) For $R = 6400\ \text{km}$, $g = 10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ and $h = 3600\ \text{km}$, find the orbital speed and period. [2] <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Answers — Group C, Mechanics
**1. (a)** Draw a straight line AB on a $v$–$t$ graph from $v = u$ at $t = 0$ to $v = v$ at time $t$. Slope $= a$, so $a = \dfrac{v-u}{t}$, giving **$v = u + at$**. The displacement is the area under the line: a rectangle $ut$ plus a triangle $\tfrac12(v-u)t$, so $s = ut + \tfrac12(v-u)t = ut + \tfrac12at^{2}$, giving **$s = ut + \tfrac12at^{2}$**. The area is also the trapezoid $s = \tfrac12(u+v)t$; substituting $t = (v-u)/a$ gives $s = \dfrac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{2a}$, so **$v^{2} = u^{2} + 2as$**.
**(b)** $a = \dfrac{v^{2}-u^{2}}{2s} = \dfrac{900 - 100}{400} = 2.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}$; $t = \dfrac{v-u}{a} = \dfrac{20}{2} = 10\ \text{s}$.
**2. (a)** Gravity supplies the centripetal force: $\dfrac{mv^{2}}{R+h} = \dfrac{GMm}{(R+h)^{2}}$, so $v = \sqrt{\dfrac{GM}{R+h}} = R\sqrt{\dfrac{g}{R+h}}$ (using $GM = gR^{2}$). Period $T = \dfrac{2\pi(R+h)}{v} = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{(R+h)^{3}}{GM}}$. Both are independent of the satellite's mass.
**(b)** A satellite that appears fixed over one point of the Earth. Conditions: orbit in the equatorial plane; period $24\ \text{h}$; same sense as Earth's rotation (west to east); height about $36\,000\ \text{km}$.
**(c)** $R + h = 10^{7}\ \text{m}$; $v = 6.4\times10^{6}\sqrt{\dfrac{10}{10^{7}}} = 6.4\times10^{6}\times10^{-3} = 6400\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. $T = \dfrac{2\pi\times10^{7}}{6400} = 9.8\times10^{3}\ \text{s} \approx 2.7\ \text{h}$.
:::

## 3.2 Heat and Thermodynamics (units 9–13, 26 hours)

**Group B — Short answer (5 marks each)**

1. Define linear, superficial and cubical expansivity. Show that $\gamma = 3\alpha$ for an isotropic solid. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. State Newton's law of cooling. A body cools from $80^{\circ}\text{C}$ to $60^{\circ}\text{C}$ in $10$ minutes in a room at $20^{\circ}\text{C}$. Estimate the time to cool from $60^{\circ}\text{C}$ to $40^{\circ}\text{C}$ using the average-temperature form. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. A $0.5\ \text{kg}$ metal block at $100^{\circ}\text{C}$ is dropped into $0.4\ \text{kg}$ of water at $20^{\circ}\text{C}$ and the mixture reaches $30^{\circ}\text{C}$. Find the specific heat capacity of the metal ($c_w = 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$; neglect the container). <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. Find the heat required to change $10\ \text{g}$ of ice at $-10^{\circ}\text{C}$ into steam at $100^{\circ}\text{C}$. ($c_{ice} = 2100$, $c_w = 4200\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, $L_f = 3.36\times10^{5}$, $L_v = 2.26\times10^{6}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}$) <span class="marks">[5]</span>
5. Define thermal conductivity. Find the rate of heat flow through a glass pane of area $2\ \text{m}^{2}$ and thickness $0.1\ \text{m}$... use $k = 0.8\ \text{W m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$ with faces $20\ \text{K}$ apart. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
6. State Stefan–Boltzmann's law. A black body of surface area $0.01\ \text{m}^{2}$ is at $727^{\circ}\text{C}$. Find the power radiated ($\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\ \text{W m}^{-2}\text{K}^{-4}$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>
7. State the zeroth law of thermodynamics and explain how it underlies the use of a thermometer. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
8. Calculate the volume of $2$ mol of an ideal gas at $27^{\circ}\text{C}$ and $1.013\times10^{5}\ \text{Pa}$ ($R = 8.31\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Answers — Group B, Heat
**1.** $\alpha = \dfrac{\Delta l}{l\,\Delta\theta}$ (per unit length per kelvin); $\beta = \dfrac{\Delta A}{A\,\Delta\theta}$; $\gamma = \dfrac{\Delta V}{V\,\Delta\theta}$. A cube of side $l_0$: $V_0 = l_0^{3}$; at $\theta$, $l = l_0(1+\alpha\theta)$, so $V = l_0^{3}(1+\alpha\theta)^{3} \approx V_0(1 + 3\alpha\theta)$ (neglecting $\alpha^{2}$, $\alpha^{3}$). Comparing with $V = V_0(1+\gamma\theta)$: $\gamma = 3\alpha$. Similarly $\beta = 2\alpha$.
**2.** The rate of loss of heat of a body is proportional to the excess of its temperature over the surroundings (for small differences). First interval: mean excess $= 70 - 20 = 50$; fall $= 20$ in $10$ min, so rate $= 2.0\ \text{K min}^{-1}$ at $50\ \text{K}$ excess, i.e. $0.04\ \text{min}^{-1}$ per kelvin. Second interval: mean $= 50^{\circ}\text{C}$, excess $30$; rate $= 0.04\times30 = 1.2\ \text{K min}^{-1}$; time $= 20/1.2 = 16.7$ min.
**3.** Heat lost by metal $=$ heat gained by water: $0.5\,c\,(100-30) = 0.4(4200)(30-20)$, so $35c = 16800$, $c = 480\ \text{J kg}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$.
**4.** Ice warms $-10 \to 0$: $0.010\times2100\times10 = 210\ \text{J}$. Melts: $0.010\times3.36\times10^{5} = 3360\ \text{J}$. Water $0 \to 100$: $0.010\times4200\times100 = 4200\ \text{J}$. Boils: $0.010\times2.26\times10^{6} = 22\,600\ \text{J}$. Total $= 30\,370\ \text{J} \approx 3.0\times10^{4}\ \text{J}$.
**5.** Thermal conductivity $k$: the heat flowing per second through unit area of a slab of unit thickness when the faces differ by $1\ \text{K}$ (steady state), unit $\text{W m}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$. $H = \dfrac{kA\Delta\theta}{d} = \dfrac{0.8\times2\times20}{0.1} = 320\ \text{W}$.
**6.** The energy radiated per second by a black body is proportional to the fourth power of its absolute temperature and its area: $P = \sigma AT^{4}$. $T = 727 + 273 = 1000\ \text{K}$. $P = 5.67\times10^{-8}\times0.01\times10^{12} = 567\ \text{W}$.
**7.** If two bodies are each in thermal equilibrium with a third, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. A thermometer is the third body: when it reads the same for two bodies they have the same temperature and no net heat flows between them. In a mercury thermometer, the mercury reaches equilibrium with the body and its column length marks the common temperature.
**8.** $T = 300\ \text{K}$. $V = \dfrac{nRT}{P} = \dfrac{2\times8.31\times300}{1.013\times10^{5}} = 4.92\times10^{-2}\ \text{m}^{3} \approx 49\ \text{litres}$.
:::

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. (a) Derive the expression $P = \tfrac13\rho\overline{c^{2}}$ for the pressure of an ideal gas using the kinetic theory. [5] (b) Deduce that the mean kinetic energy of a molecule is $\tfrac32kT$. [1] (c) Find the r.m.s. speed of nitrogen ($M = 28\times10^{-3}\ \text{kg mol}^{-1}$) at $27^{\circ}\text{C}$. [2] <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Answers — Group C, Heat
**1. (a)** Take $N$ molecules, each of mass $m$, in a cubical box of side $l$. A molecule with velocity components $u_x, u_y, u_z$ strikes a wall perpendicular to $x$ and rebounds elastically; momentum change $= 2mu_x$. It returns to the same wall after time $2l/u_x$, so the rate of change of momentum (force) is $\dfrac{2mu_x}{2l/u_x} = \dfrac{mu_x^{2}}{l}$. For all molecules, $F = \dfrac{m}{l}\sum u_x^{2}$ and the pressure on the wall of area $l^{2}$ is $P = \dfrac{m}{l^{3}}\sum u_x^{2} = \dfrac{m}{V}\,N\,\overline{u_x^{2}}$. By symmetry $\overline{u_x^{2}} = \overline{u_y^{2}} = \overline{u_z^{2}} = \tfrac13\overline{c^{2}}$. So $P = \dfrac{mN}{3V}\overline{c^{2}} = \tfrac13\rho\overline{c^{2}}$.
**(b)** For one mole $PV = RT$ and $P = \dfrac{1}{3}\dfrac{N_Am}{V}\overline{c^{2}}$, so $RT = \tfrac13N_Am\overline{c^{2}}$ and $\tfrac12m\overline{c^{2}} = \dfrac{3RT}{2N_A} = \tfrac32kT$ with $k = R/N_A$.
**(c)** $c_{rms} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3RT}{M}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{3\times8.31\times300}{0.028}} = \sqrt{2.67\times10^{5}} \approx 517\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.
:::

## 3.3 Waves and Optics (units 14–18, 15 hours)

**Group B — Short answer (5 marks each)**

1. An object is placed $10\ \text{cm}$ from a concave mirror of focal length $15\ \text{cm}$. Find the position and magnification of the image and state its nature. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. A coin lies at the bottom of a water tank $12\ \text{cm}$ deep. If $n_{water} = 4/3$, find the apparent depth. Explain why a pool looks shallower than it is. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Define the critical angle and total internal reflection. Find the critical angle for glass of refractive index $1.5$. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. A prism of angle $60^{\circ}$ and refractive index $\sqrt{2}$ is used. Find the angle of minimum deviation. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
5. An object is $30\ \text{cm}$ from a convex lens of focal length $20\ \text{cm}$. Find the image distance and magnification. Also find the power of a concave lens of focal length $25\ \text{cm}$. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
6. Define the dispersive power of a material. Find it for glass with $n_v = 1.532$, $n_r = 1.514$ and $n_y = 1.522$. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Answers — Group B, Waves and Optics
**1.** Real-is-positive convention: $\dfrac{1}{v} + \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{f}$ with $u = 10$, $f = 15$ gives $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{15} - \dfrac{1}{10} = -\dfrac{1}{30}$, so $v = -30\ \text{cm}$: a **virtual** image $30\ \text{cm}$ behind the mirror. $m = -\dfrac{v}{u} = +3$: **erect and three times larger** (as in a shaving mirror).
**2.** $n = \dfrac{\text{real depth}}{\text{apparent depth}}$, so apparent depth $= \dfrac{12}{4/3} = 9\ \text{cm}$. Rays leaving the coin bend away from the normal on entering air, so to the eye they appear to come from a point nearer the surface.
**3.** The critical angle is the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the refracted ray grazes the interface (angle of refraction $90^{\circ}$). Total internal reflection occurs when light travelling in the denser medium meets the boundary at an angle greater than $C$. $\sin C = \dfrac{1}{n} = \dfrac{1}{1.5} = 0.667$, so $C = 41.8^{\circ}$.
**4.** $n = \dfrac{\sin\frac{A+D}{2}}{\sin\frac{A}{2}}$. $\sin\dfrac{60+D}{2} = \sqrt{2}\sin30^{\circ} = 0.7071$, so $\dfrac{60+D}{2} = 45^{\circ}$ and $D = 30^{\circ}$.
**5.** $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f} - \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{20} - \dfrac{1}{30} = \dfrac{1}{60}$, so $v = 60\ \text{cm}$ (real). $m = -\dfrac{v}{u} = -2$ (inverted, twice the size). Power $P = \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{-0.25} = -4\ \text{D}$.
**6.** Dispersive power $\omega = \dfrac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1}$: the ratio of the angular dispersion to the mean deviation. $\omega = \dfrac{1.532 - 1.514}{1.522 - 1} = \dfrac{0.018}{0.522} = 0.0345$.
:::

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. (a) Draw the path of a ray through a prism and derive $n = \dfrac{\sin\frac{A+D}{2}}{\sin\frac{A}{2}}$. [5] (b) A ray falls on a prism of angle $60^{\circ}$ at $50^{\circ}$ and emerges at $50^{\circ}$. Find the deviation and the refractive index. [3] <span class="marks">[8]</span>
2. Derive the mirror formula $\dfrac{1}{u} + \dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f}$ for a concave mirror and show that $f = R/2$. [6] Find $R$ if an object $20\ \text{cm}$ away gives a real image $60\ \text{cm}$ away. [2] <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Answers — Group C, Waves and Optics
**1. (a)** (Diagram: ray $PQ$ meets face $AB$ at $Q$, bends towards the normal, meets face $AC$ at $R$, emerges as $RS$ bending away; angles $i_1, r_1$ at $Q$ and $r_2, i_2$ at $R$; the deviation $D$ is between the incident ray $PQ$ extended and $RS$.) Deviation at the first face $= i_1 - r_1$; at the second $= i_2 - r_2$, so $D = (i_1 + i_2) - (r_1 + r_2)$. In the quadrilateral $AQNR$ (normals meeting at $N$), $r_1 + r_2 = A$. So $D = i_1 + i_2 - A$. At minimum deviation the ray passes symmetrically: $i_1 = i_2 = i$ and $r_1 = r_2 = r = A/2$, so $D_m = 2i - A$, or $i = \dfrac{A + D_m}{2}$. Snell's law $n = \dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r}$ gives $n = \dfrac{\sin\frac{A+D_m}{2}}{\sin\frac{A}{2}}$.
**(b)** Emergent angle equals incident angle, so this is minimum deviation: $D = i_1 + i_2 - A = 50 + 50 - 60 = 40^{\circ}$. $n = \dfrac{\sin50^{\circ}}{\sin30^{\circ}} = \dfrac{0.766}{0.5} = 1.53$.
**2.** (Diagram: object $AB$ beyond $C$; ray $AD$ parallel to the axis reflects through $F$; ray $AP$ strikes the pole and reflects with equal angle; image $A'B'$ between $F$ and $C$.) Use the real-is-positive convention: $PB = u$, $PB' = v$, $PF = f$. The ray $AP$ hits the pole at angle $\theta$ and reflects at the same angle, so right triangles $ABP$ and $A'B'P$ are similar: $\dfrac{AB}{A'B'} = \dfrac{u}{v}$. The ray $AD$ (with $DP = AB$) passes through $F$, so right triangles $DPF$ and $A'B'F$ are similar: $\dfrac{AB}{A'B'} = \dfrac{DP}{A'B'} = \dfrac{PF}{FB'} = \dfrac{f}{v - f}$. Equating, $\dfrac{u}{v} = \dfrac{f}{v-f}$, so $uv - uf = vf$. Dividing by $uvf$ gives $\dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{u} + \dfrac{1}{v}$. For a distant object $u = \infty$ and the image forms at $F$: $v = f$. A ray parallel to the axis meets the surface at $D$, and the radius $CD$ is the normal, so the angle of incidence $\angle CDA$ equals the angle of reflection $\angle CDF$, which makes $\triangle CDF$ isosceles with $CF = FD \approx FP$ for a small aperture. Hence $f = R/2$.
Numerical: $\dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{20} + \dfrac{1}{60} = \dfrac{4}{60}$, so $f = 15\ \text{cm}$ and $R = 2f = 30\ \text{cm}$.
:::

## 3.4 Electricity and Magnetism (units 19–23, 27 hours)

**Group B — Short answer (5 marks each)**

1. State Coulomb's law. Find the force between two $2\ \mu\text{C}$ charges placed $3\ \text{cm}$ apart in air ($k = 9\times10^{9}\ \text{N m}^{2}\text{C}^{-2}$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Define electric field intensity. Find the field at $0.30\ \text{m}$ from a point charge of $5\ \text{nC}$, and the force on a $2\ \text{nC}$ charge there. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Define electric potential and the volt. Find the work done in taking a $2\ \mu\text{C}$ charge through a potential difference of $100\ \text{V}$. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. Capacitors of $2\ \mu\text{F}$, $3\ \mu\text{F}$ and $6\ \mu\text{F}$ are connected (a) in series and (b) in parallel. Find the equivalent capacitance in each case. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
5. Derive the energy stored in a capacitor. Find it for $10\ \mu\text{F}$ charged to $100\ \text{V}$. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
6. A cell of emf $12\ \text{V}$ and internal resistance $1\ \Omega$ is connected to a $5\ \Omega$ resistor. Find the current, the terminal potential difference and the power dissipated in the external resistor. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
7. State Ohm's law and explain why it fails for a filament lamp. Find the resistance of a copper wire $2\ \text{m}$ long, $0.5\ \text{mm}^{2}$ in area ($\rho = 1.7\times10^{-8}\ \Omega\,\text{m}$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Answers — Group B, Electricity and Magnetism
**1.** The force between two point charges is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them, along the line joining them: $F = k\dfrac{q_1q_2}{r^{2}}$. $F = \dfrac{9\times10^{9}\times(2\times10^{-6})^{2}}{(0.03)^{2}} = \dfrac{9\times10^{9}\times4\times10^{-12}}{9\times10^{-4}} = 40\ \text{N}$ (repulsive).
**2.** The force per unit positive test charge at that point, $E = F/q$ (unit $\text{N C}^{-1}$). $E = \dfrac{kq}{r^{2}} = \dfrac{9\times10^{9}\times5\times10^{-9}}{0.09} = 500\ \text{N C}^{-1}$. $F = qE = 2\times10^{-9}\times500 = 1.0\times10^{-6}\ \text{N}$.
**3.** Potential at a point is the work done per unit positive charge in bringing it from infinity to that point. One volt is one joule per coulomb. $W = qV = 2\times10^{-6}\times100 = 2\times10^{-4}\ \text{J}$.
**4.** (a) Series: $\dfrac{1}{C} = \dfrac{1}{2} + \dfrac{1}{3} + \dfrac{1}{6} = 1$, so $C = 1\ \mu\text{F}$. (b) Parallel: $C = 2 + 3 + 6 = 11\ \mu\text{F}$.
**5.** Raising the charge on the plates from $q$ to $q + dq$ against the potential $V = q/C$ needs work $dW = \dfrac{q}{C}\,dq$. Total: $W = \int_0^Q\dfrac{q}{C}\,dq = \dfrac{Q^{2}}{2C} = \tfrac12CV^{2} = \tfrac12QV$. $U = \tfrac12(10\times10^{-6})(100)^{2} = 0.050\ \text{J}$.
**6.** $I = \dfrac{E}{R + r} = \dfrac{12}{6} = 2.0\ \text{A}$. Terminal p.d. $V = E - Ir = 12 - 2 = 10\ \text{V}$. $P = I^{2}R = 4\times5 = 20\ \text{W}$.
**7.** Ohm's law: at constant temperature the current through a conductor is proportional to the potential difference across it. A filament heats up, so its resistance rises with current and the $V$–$I$ graph is not a straight line. $R = \dfrac{\rho l}{A} = \dfrac{1.7\times10^{-8}\times2}{0.5\times10^{-6}} = 0.068\ \Omega$.
:::

[2] (c) Plates of area $0.02\ \text{m}^{2}$ are $1\ \text{mm}$ apart in air; find $C$ and the energy at $100\ \text{V}$ ($\varepsilon_0 = 8.85\times10^{-12}\ \text{F m}^{-1}$). ::: note Answers — Group C, Electricity and Magnetism

**(c)** $C = \dfrac{8.85\times10^{-12}\times0.02}{1\times10^{-3}} = 1.77\times10^{-10}\ \text{F} = 177\ \text{pF}$. $U = \tfrac12CV^{2} = \tfrac12(1.77\times10^{-10})(10^{4}) = 8.85\times10^{-7}\ \text{J}$.
**2. (a)** Current law: the algebraic sum of currents at a junction is zero, $\sum I = 0$ — conservation of charge. Voltage law: the algebraic sum of emfs and potential drops round a closed loop is zero, $\sum E = \sum IR$ — conservation of energy.
**(b)** The parallel pair: $\dfrac{1}{R}_p = \dfrac{1}{6} + \dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, so $R_p = 2\ \Omega$. ## 3.5 Modern Physics (units 24–26, 15 hours)

**Group B — Short answer (5 marks each)**

1. Define half-life and decay constant, and derive the relation between them. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. A radioactive sample of mass $80\ \text{g}$ has half-life $4$ days. How much remains after $12$ days? <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Calculate the binding energy and the binding energy per nucleon of $^{4}_{2}\text{He}$ ($m_p = 1.007825\ \text{u}$ for hydrogen atom, $m_n = 1.008665\ \text{u}$, $m_{He} = 4.002603\ \text{u}$, $1\ \text{u} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. Distinguish between nuclear fission and nuclear fusion. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
5. Using band theory, distinguish between a conductor, a semiconductor and an insulator. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
6. Explain what happens to the resistance of a semiconductor and a metal as temperature rises, and why. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Answers — Modern Physics, Group B
**1.** Half-life $T_{1/2}$: the time in which half of the radioactive nuclei in a sample decay. Decay constant $\lambda$: the probability of decay of a nucleus per unit time (fractional decay rate). From $N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$, at $t = T_{1/2}$, $N = N_0/2$: $\tfrac12 = e^{-\lambda T_{1/2}}$, so $\ln2 = \lambda T_{1/2}$ and $T_{1/2} = \dfrac{0.693}{\lambda}$.
**2.** $12/4 = 3$ half-lives, so $N = 80\times\left(\tfrac12\right)^{3} = 10\ \text{g}$.
**3.** Mass of constituents: $2(1.007825) + 2(1.008665) = 2.015650 + 2.017330 = 4.032980\ \text{u}$. Mass defect $= 4.032980 - 4.002603 = 0.030377\ \text{u}$. $B = 0.030377\times931.5 = 28.3\ \text{MeV}$; per nucleon $= 28.3/4 = 7.07\ \text{MeV}$.
**4.**

| Point | Fission | Fusion |
|---|---|---|
| Process | a heavy nucleus (U-235) splits into two medium nuclei | two light nuclei (hydrogen isotopes) join into a heavier one |
| Trigger | a slow neutron | temperature of about $10^{7}\ \text{K}$, so nuclei overcome their repulsion |
| Energy | about $200\ \text{MeV}$ per fission | about $17.6\ \text{MeV}$ per D–T fusion, which is more per unit mass |
| Waste | radioactive fission products | little radioactive waste |
| Use | reactors, atom bomb | the Sun, hydrogen bomb; not yet controlled commercially |
**5.** In a *conductor* the conduction and valence bands overlap or the conduction band is partly filled, so electrons move freely. In an *insulator* the forbidden gap is large (above about $5\ \text{eV}$) and no electrons reach the conduction band. In a *semiconductor* the gap is small ($\approx1.1\ \text{eV}$ for Si, $0.7\ \text{eV}$ for Ge), so at room temperature a few electrons cross the gap, leaving holes.
**6.** Metal: resistance rises, because the ion lattice vibrates more and scatters electrons more often; the number of carriers hardly changes. Semiconductor: resistance falls, because heat frees many more carriers across the gap, which outweighs increased scattering.
:::

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. (a) State the law of radioactive decay and derive $N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$. [4] (b) Draw the decay curve and mark $T_{1/2}$. [1] (c) A sample of $64\ \text{g}$ is reduced to $8\ \text{g}$ in $18$ days. Find the half-life and the decay constant. [3] <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Answers — Modern Physics, Group C
**1. (a)** Law: the rate of decay of a radioactive sample is proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei present. $-\dfrac{dN}{dt} = \lambda N$. Separating variables, $\dfrac{dN}{N} = -\lambda\,dt$. Integrating from $N_0$ at $t = 0$ to $N$ at $t$: $\ln\dfrac{N}{N_0} = -\lambda t$, so $N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$.
**(b)** (Graph: an exponential curve falling from $N_0$ at $t = 0$; mark $N_0/2$ at $t = T_{1/2}$, $N_0/4$ at $2T_{1/2}$, $N_0/8$ at $3T_{1/2}$.)
**(c)** $\dfrac{64}{8} = 8 = 2^{3}$, so $18\ \text{days} = 3T_{1/2}$ and $T_{1/2} = 6\ \text{days}$. $\lambda = \dfrac{0.693}{6} = 0.1155\ \text{day}^{-1}$.
:::

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. Derive the work–energy theorem for a constant force and use it to find the stopping distance of a $1000\ \text{kg}$ car moving at $20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ when a constant braking force of $5000\ \text{N}$ is applied. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**1.** For a constant force $F$ producing acceleration $a$ over displacement $s$: $v^{2} = u^{2} + 2as$ with $a = F/m$ gives $Fs = \tfrac12mv^{2} - \tfrac12mu^{2}$: the work done by the net force equals the change in kinetic energy. **2.** The principal focus is the point on the axis at which rays parallel to the axis converge after refraction.
