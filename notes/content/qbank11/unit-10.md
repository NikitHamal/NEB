This chapter does three things. It sets out the **pattern of the Grade 11 Biology paper** and shows how the teaching hours of the CDC syllabus translate into marks. It gives **two complete model papers**, each of 75 marks and three hours, followed by a full solution written the way you should write in the hall. And it ends with a **ranked list of predicted questions**, each with the reason for its rank.

::: caution What "predicted" means here
Nobody outside the examination board knows what will be set, and this chapter does not pretend to. A prediction is a judgement from two public facts: how many teaching hours the syllabus gives a topic, and how standard the question form is (a differentiation, a labelled diagram, a family description). Use the list to decide what to **revise first**, never as a promise. Every question below is an original practice item; none is claimed to be from a past paper.
:::

::: key How to sit a three-hour paper
Seventy-five marks in 180 minutes is 2.4 minutes a mark. Plan it: **MCQs, 12 minutes** (11 marks); **short answers, 80 minutes** (8 questions of 4 marks, about 10 minutes each); **long answers, 75 minutes** (4 questions of 8 marks, about 18 minutes each, diagram included); 13 minutes to check. A four-mark answer needs four separate markable points; an eight-mark answer needs a structure of definition, body, diagram and significance.
:::

::: note Group B model answers
**6. Mitosis versus meiosis.**

| Point | Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|---|
| Occurs in | somatic (body) cells | reproductive cells forming gametes or spores |
| Number of divisions | one | two (meiosis I and II) |
| Daughter cells | two, identical to the parent | four, with half the chromosome number |
| Chromosome number | stays the same (2n to 2n) | halved (2n to n) |
| Crossing over | absent | present in prophase I, giving variation |

**7. Lichen.** A lichen is a **symbiotic association between an alga (or cyanobacterium) and a fungus**: the alga makes food by photosynthesis and the fungus gives water, minerals and shelter. Importance: (i) they are **indicators of air pollution**, because they are very sensitive to sulphur dioxide and disappear from polluted air; (ii) they are **pioneers of primary succession** on bare rock, weathering it into soil; (iii) they yield **dyes and indicators**, such as litmus and orcein, and some are used as food and fodder (reindeer moss).

**8. Economic importance of Fabaceae.** (i) **Pulses** such as gram, pea, lentil, soybean and groundnut are rich sources of protein. (ii) The roots bear **root nodules with Rhizobium**, which fix atmospheric nitrogen and enrich the soil, so these plants are used in crop rotation. (iii) **Timber and fuel** are obtained from plants such as sissoo (*Dalbergia sissoo*). (iv) Others give **fodder, dyes and medicine**; for example indigo gives a dye and liquorice has medicinal use.

**9. Eutrophication.** Eutrophication is the **enrichment of a water body with nutrients, especially nitrates and phosphates, leading to excessive growth of algae and aquatic plants**. *Causes:* run-off of chemical fertiliser from farms, discharge of sewage and detergents. *Effects:* (i) an algal bloom covers the surface and blocks sunlight; (ii) when the algae die, bacteria decompose them and use up dissolved oxygen; (iii) fish and other animals die for lack of oxygen, and the water becomes foul and unfit to use.

**18.** Homologous organs have the **same fundamental structure and origin but different functions**, as in the human forelimb, whale flipper and bat wing; they show **divergent evolution** and common ancestry. Analogous organs have **different structure and origin but the same function**, as in the wing of an insect and the wing of a bird; they show **convergent evolution**. A homologous pair is evidence of common descent; an analogous pair is evidence of adaptation to similar conditions.

**19. Lamarck's theory.** Lamarck (1809) proposed that (i) organisms respond to the **environment**; (ii) organs that are **used** become better developed while organs that are **not used** weaken (use and disuse); (iii) these **acquired characters are inherited** by the offspring. Example: the long neck of the giraffe, which Lamarck explained by ancestors stretching to reach high leaves, the stretch being passed on. *Criticism:* acquired characters, such as a cut tail, are not inherited (Weismann cut the tails of mice for many generations and the offspring still had tails).

**20. Excretion in the earthworm.** The excretory organs are coiled tubes called **nephridia**, which are of three kinds: (i) **septal nephridia** on the septa, opening into the intestine; (ii) **integumentary nephridia** in the body wall, opening on the outside through pores; (iii) **pharyngeal nephridia** around the pharynx, opening into the buccal cavity. Each nephridium begins with a **funnel (nephrostome)** with cilia, collects waste from the coelomic fluid, and passes it along the tube, where useful substances and water are reabsorbed. The earthworm is **ureotelic**: the main waste is urea.

**21.** *In-situ conservation* protects species **in their natural habitat**, for example in Chitwan National Park and the Koshi Tappu Wildlife Reserve. *Ex-situ conservation* protects species **outside their natural habitat**, for example in the Central Zoo at Jawalakhel and the gharial breeding centre at Kasara in Chitwan.

::: note Group C model answers
**10. Mitosis.** *Definition.* Mitosis is the division of a somatic cell in which the nucleus divides into **two nuclei with the same chromosome number as the parent** (karyokinesis), usually followed by division of the cytoplasm (cytokinesis).

*Stages.* Diagram: one labelled sketch for each of the four stages, showing the spindle and the chromosomes.
(i) **Prophase** — chromatin condenses into chromosomes, each of two chromatids joined at the centromere; the nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear; the spindle begins to form.
(ii) **Metaphase** — the chromosomes line up at the equator (metaphase plate) and the spindle fibres attach to the centromeres.
(iii) **Anaphase** — the centromeres split and the sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles.
(iv) **Telophase** — the chromosomes reach the poles and uncoil; the nuclear envelope and nucleolus reappear; cytokinesis divides the cell (by cell plate in plants and by furrow in animals).

*Significance.* Mitosis gives **growth** and the **replacement** of worn cells, **repair** of wounds, and **asexual reproduction**, and it keeps the **chromosome number constant** in all body cells.

**11. Solanaceae** (the potato family). *Vegetative characters:* herbs, shrubs or small trees; leaves **alternate**, simple, without stipules; stems often with bicollateral vascular bundles; some have underground tubers (potato). *Floral characters:* flowers **bisexual, actinomorphic, pentamerous and hypogynous**; calyx of five sepals fused (**gamosepalous**, often persistent); corolla of five petals fused (**gamopetalous**); five stamens **epipetalous** (attached to the petals); gynoecium of **two fused carpels (bicarpellary, syncarpous)** with a **superior ovary**, **axile placentation** and a swollen placenta. The fruit is a **berry or capsule**.

*Floral formula:* ⊕ ⚥ K(5) C(5) A5 G(2) (⊕ actinomorphic, ⚥ bisexual, brackets show fused parts; the ovary is superior).

*Economic importance.* **Food:** potato (*Solanum tuberosum*), brinjal (*Solanum melongena*), tomato, chilli (*Capsicum*). **Drugs:** belladonna (*Atropa*) gives atropine; datura is medicinal and poisonous. **Others:** tobacco (*Nicotiana*), and ornamentals such as petunia.

**22. Digestive system of the frog.** The frog is **carnivorous**; its digestive system has the **alimentary canal** and **digestive glands**.

*Diagram:* a labelled sketch showing the mouth, buccal cavity, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine (duodenum, ileum), large intestine (rectum), cloaca, liver with gall bladder, and pancreas.

*Alimentary canal.* (i) The **mouth** opens into the **buccal cavity**, which has maxillary and vomerine teeth to hold prey and a **bifid, sticky tongue**, folded back and flicked out to catch insects. (ii) The cavity leads into the **pharynx** and a short **oesophagus**. (iii) The **stomach** is a J-shaped muscular bag with gastric glands that secrete **HCl and pepsin**; food becomes a semi-fluid **chyme**. (iv) The **small intestine** has the duodenum, where bile and pancreatic juice enter, and the coiled ileum, where digestion is completed and food is absorbed. (v) The **large intestine** (rectum) stores undigested matter, which leaves through the **cloaca** and the cloacal aperture.

*Digestive glands.* The **liver** secretes bile, which is stored in the gall bladder and emulsifies fats. The **pancreas** secretes pancreatic juice with enzymes (amylase, trypsin, lipase) that act in the duodenum.

**23. Evidences of organic evolution.**

*Comparative anatomy.* (i) **Homologous organs** such as the forelimbs of a human, a whale and a bat have the same plan but different uses and show common ancestry. (ii) **Analogous organs** such as the wings of insects and of birds show adaptation to the same function. (iii) **Vestigial organs** such as the vermiform appendix and the ear muscles are reduced remains of organs that were functional in ancestors.

*Embryology.* The embryos of fish, frog, chicken and human look alike in early stages and diverge later, showing common ancestry. Haeckel's **biogenetic law** says that ontogeny repeats phylogeny, and the tadpole of the frog with gills and a tail recalls its fish-like ancestors. (Haeckel's law is now held to be only partly true.)

*Palaeontology.* **Fossils** are remains or impressions of ancient organisms in rocks. The order of fossils in the layers shows the **sequence of life** from simple to complex. **Connecting links** such as *Archaeopteryx*, which has feathers like a bird and teeth and a tail like a reptile, link reptiles to birds. The fossil series of the horse shows a gradual change in size, toes and teeth.

::: note Group B model answers
**6. Functions of the Golgi apparatus.** (i) It **modifies and packages** proteins and lipids received from the endoplasmic reticulum. (ii) It **secretes** substances out of the cell in vesicles (such as enzymes and mucus). (iii) It forms **lysosomes**. (iv) It adds sugar groups to make **glycoproteins** and **glycolipids**, and in plant cells it helps in forming the **cell wall** and cell plate; in sperm it forms the **acrosome**.

**7. Characters of Bryophyta.** (i) They are **non-vascular** plants with no xylem or phloem. (ii) The plant body is a **gametophyte** and is dominant; it is thalloid or leafy and is fixed by **rhizoids**. (iii) The sporophyte is **dependent** on the gametophyte for water and food. (iv) **Water is needed for fertilisation**, because the male gametes swim; for this reason bryophytes are called the "amphibians of the plant kingdom". (v) Reproduction is vegetative (by gemmae) and sexual.

**8. Ecological adaptation.** It is a **structural, functional or behavioural feature that helps an organism survive in a particular habitat**. *Hydrophytes* (water plants): (i) roots are **reduced or absent**; (ii) tissue has large air spaces, **aerenchyma**, for buoyancy and gas exchange; (iii) floating leaves bear stomata only on the **upper** surface. *Xerophytes* (desert plants): (i) a **thick cuticle** and **sunken stomata** reduce water loss; (ii) leaves are reduced to **spines**, and stems are fleshy and photosynthetic; (iii) **deep roots** reach water. (Any two of each are enough.)

**9. DNA versus RNA.**

| Point | DNA | RNA |
|---|---|---|
| Sugar | deoxyribose | ribose |
| Nitrogen bases | A, T, G, C | A, U, G, C |
| Strands | usually double-stranded helix | usually single-stranded |
| Types and role | one kind; stores genetic information | mRNA, tRNA, rRNA; protein synthesis |
| Location | mainly the nucleus (also mitochondria, chloroplasts) | nucleus, cytoplasm and ribosomes |

**18. Vestigial organs** are **reduced, non-functional (or little-used) remains of organs that were fully functional in the ancestors**. Examples in humans: (i) the **vermiform appendix**; (ii) the **ear muscles** (auricular muscles); (iii) the **coccyx** (tail bone); (iv) the **wisdom teeth**; (v) the **nictitating membrane** at the inner corner of the eye. They are evidence of evolution.

**19. Phylum Arthropoda.** (i) The body is **segmented** and divided into head, thorax and abdomen (or fused regions). (ii) There are **jointed appendages**, which gives the phylum its name. (iii) The body is covered by a **chitinous exoskeleton**, which is moulted (ecdysis). (iv) The **circulatory system is open**, with a body cavity called a haemocoel, and a dorsal heart. (v) There are paired compound eyes, and respiration is by gills, book lungs or tracheae. Examples: prawn, butterfly, spider, cockroach.

**20.** *Amoeba:* (i) the shape is **irregular and changing**; (ii) it moves and feeds by **pseudopodia**; (iii) it has **one nucleus**; (iv) it multiplies by **binary fission** only. *Paramecium:* (i) the shape is **fixed, slipper-like**, because of the pellicle; (ii) it moves by **cilia** and feeds through an **oral groove**; (iii) it has **two nuclei**, a macronucleus and a micronucleus; (iv) it multiplies by binary fission and also shows **conjugation**.

|---|---|---|

| Need for practice | none; performed correctly the first time | needs practice and repetition |
| Variation | the same in all members of a species | differs from individual to individual |
| Examples | reflex action, nest-building, spider's web | conditioned reflex, imprinting, habituation |

**10. Meiosis I.** Meiosis is the division of a reproductive cell in which the **chromosome number is halved**. Meiosis I is the **reductional division**.

*Diagram:* labelled sketches of each stage showing paired chromosomes, the spindle and the nuclear envelope.

(i) **Prophase I** is long and has five sub-stages: **leptotene** (chromosomes become thread-like), **zygotene** (homologous chromosomes pair up, called synapsis, to form bivalents), **pachytene** (the pairs shorten, and **crossing over** occurs between non-sister chromatids), **diplotene** (the homologues begin to separate and are held at the **chiasmata**) and **diakinesis** (the chromosomes are fully condensed, and the nucleolus and nuclear envelope disappear).
(ii) **Metaphase I** — the bivalents line up at the equator, with the spindle fibres attached to the centromeres of each homologue.
(iii) **Anaphase I** — the **homologous chromosomes** separate and move to opposite poles; the centromeres do **not** divide.
(iv) **Telophase I** — two haploid nuclei form, and the cell divides into two. Meiosis II then follows and divides the chromatids as in mitosis, giving four haploid cells.

*Significance.* (i) It **halves the chromosome number** in gametes, so fertilisation restores the diploid number. (ii) **Crossing over** and random separation produce **genetic variation**. (iii) Variation is the raw material of evolution.

**11. Brassicaceae** (the mustard family, formerly Cruciferae). *Vegetative characters:* herbs, rarely shrubs; leaves **alternate**, simple, without stipules, often with a pungent smell and taste. *Floral characters:* the flowers are **bisexual, actinomorphic and hypogynous**; the calyx has **four sepals in two whorls** (2+2), free; the corolla has **four petals arranged like a cross (cruciform)**; there are **six stamens in two whorls, of which the outer two are short and the inner four long (tetradynamous)**; the gynoecium has **two fused carpels (bicarpellary, syncarpous)** with a **superior ovary**, **parietal placentation**, and a false partition called the **replum**. The fruit is a **siliqua or silicula**.

*Floral formula:* ⊕ ⚥ K2+2 C4 A2+4 G(2) (superior ovary).

*Economic importance.* **Vegetables:** cabbage, cauliflower, radish, turnip and broccoli. **Oil:** mustard and rapeseed oil are obtained from the seeds of *Brassica*. **Spice:** mustard seeds. **Ornamentals:** candytuft and wallflower.

**22. Respiratory system of the frog.** The frog breathes in three ways: through the **skin (cutaneous)**, through the lining of the **mouth (buccopharyngeal)** and through the **lungs (pulmonary)**. The skin is thin, moist and rich in blood capillaries, and it is the main route during hibernation and when in water.

*Pulmonary respiratory organs.* The system has **external nostrils**, the **nasal passage**, the **buccal cavity**, the **glottis**, the **laryngo-tracheal chamber** and a pair of **lungs**. The lungs are two **thin-walled, elastic sacs** with many folds, lined with capillaries, and they lie in the upper part of the body cavity.

*Mechanism of breathing.* (i) **Inspiration:** the floor of the buccal cavity is **lowered**, the **nostrils are open** and the glottis is **closed**, so air enters the buccal cavity. Then the **nostrils close**, the glottis opens and the floor of the cavity is **raised**, forcing air into the lungs. (ii) **Gas exchange** takes place in the lungs between air and blood. (iii) **Expiration:** the body wall muscles contract and the lungs recoil, so air is pushed out through the glottis and the nostrils.

**23. Darwin's theory of natural selection.** Charles Darwin put forward the theory in *On the Origin of Species* (1859), after his voyage on the *Beagle*. Alfred Russel Wallace reached the same idea independently.

*Postulates.* (i) **Overproduction:** organisms produce many more offspring than can survive. (ii) **Struggle for existence:** because resources are limited, the offspring compete for food, space and mates, and face enemies and disease. (iii) **Variation:** the members of a species differ from each other, and some variations are inherited. (iv) **Natural selection or survival of the fittest:** those with favourable variations survive and reproduce, and the others die. (v) **Origin of new species:** over many generations the favourable variations accumulate, and a new species arises.

*Example: industrial melanism in the peppered moth* (Biston betularia). Before industrialisation in England most moths were light and matched the light, lichen-covered tree trunks, so birds ate the dark ones. As soot darkened the trunks, dark moths were camouflaged and light ones were eaten, so the **dark form became common**. Natural selection thus acted on a variation that already existed. A second example is **antibiotic resistance** in bacteria.

*Limitation.* Darwin could not explain how variations arise or are inherited, which was later explained by genetics and mutation.
:::

## Chapter summary

- The paper is 75 marks: Botany 37 and Zoology 38, each with MCQs, four four-mark questions and two eight-mark questions.
- Faunal Diversity (34 hours) and Floral Diversity (30 hours) carry about half the hours; the frog, the earthworm and the four angiosperm families are the high-yield long answers.
- A four-mark answer needs four separate points; an eight-mark answer needs a definition, a body, a labelled diagram and a statement of significance.
- Mitosis keeps the chromosome number (2n to 2n) and makes two identical cells; meiosis halves it (2n to n), makes four cells and includes crossing over in prophase I.
- Key facts to hold: frog heart has three chambers; earthworm excretion is by nephridia; Darwin's theory has five postulates; the energy pyramid is always upright.
- A prediction is a guide to what to revise first, never a promise of what will be set.
