Group A of the NEB Grade 11 Mathematics paper is eleven one-mark multiple-choice questions, taken from across the twenty-one chapters of the syllabus. Eleven marks, and none of them needs more than a minute if you have the right fact ready. This chapter drills every chapter in syllabus order: each section gives five to eight practice MCQs, then an answer key in which every answer carries a **one-line reason**, and names the misconception where a wrong option is a real trap.

::: key What Group A actually tests
An MCQ in Mathematics is almost never a long calculation. It tests a **formula you must recall**, a **single substitution**, a **definition or property**, or a **common slip** (a sign, a missing factor of 2, a wrong quadrant) offered as a distractor. If a question is taking you more than ninety seconds, step back and look for the shortcut.
:::

## 11.1 Logic and Set

1. The negation of the statement "All students are present" is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) No student is present (b) Some student is absent (c) Some student is present (d) All students are absent
2. The implication $p \Rightarrow q$ is false only when <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $p$ true, $q$ true (b) $p$ true, $q$ false (c) $p$ false, $q$ true (d) $p$ false, $q$ false
3. The contrapositive of $p \Rightarrow q$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $q \Rightarrow p$ (b) $\sim p \Rightarrow \sim q$ (c) $\sim q \Rightarrow \sim p$ (d) $\sim q \Rightarrow p$
4. If $n(A)=20$, $n(B)=15$ and $n(A\cap B)=5$, then $n(A\cup B)$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 40 (b) 35 (c) 30 (d) 25
5. By De Morgan's law, $(A\cup B)'$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $A'\cup B'$ (b) $A'\cap B'$ (c) $A\cap B$ (d) $A\cup B'$
6. The compound statement $p \wedge \sim p$ is always <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) a tautology (b) true (c) a contradiction (d) a contingency

::: note Answers to 11.1
**1.** (b) — the negation of "all" is "at least one is not"; option (a) is the common over-negation.
**2.** (b) — a true hypothesis with a false conclusion is the only way to break an implication.
**3.** (c) — contrapositive negates and reverses; (a) is the converse and (b) is the inverse.
**4.** (c) — $n(A\cup B)=n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B)=20+15-5=30$; (b) forgets to subtract the overlap.
**5.** (b) — the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements.
**6.** (c) — a statement and its negation cannot both be true, so the conjunction is always false.
:::

## 11.2 Real Numbers

1. Which of the following is an irrational number? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0.\overline{3}$ (b) $\sqrt{16}$ (c) $\sqrt{7}$ (d) $\frac{22}{7}$
2. The solution set of $|x-3|<2$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $(1,5)$ (b) $[1,5]$ (c) $(-5,-1)$ (d) $(-\infty,1)\cup(5,\infty)$
3. The solutions of $|2x-1|=3$ are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $x=2$ only (b) $x=-1$ only (c) $x=2$ and $x=-1$ (d) $x=1$ and $x=-2$
4. $[-2,3]\cap(0,5)$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $(0,3)$ (b) $[0,3]$ (c) $(0,3]$ (d) $[-2,5)$
5. The solution set of $|x|\ge 4$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $[-4,4]$ (b) $(-4,4)$ (c) $(-\infty,-4]\cup[4,\infty)$ (d) $[4,\infty)$

::: note Answers to 11.2
**1.** (c) — 7 is not a perfect square; $0.\overline{3}=1/3$ and $22/7$ are rational, $\sqrt{16}=4$.
**2.** (a) — $|x-3|<2$ means $-2<x-3<2$, so $1<x<5$; the strict inequality gives open ends.
**3.** (c) — $2x-1=3\Rightarrow x=2$ and $2x-1=-3\Rightarrow x=-1$; (a) forgets the negative case.
**4.** (c) — the common part starts after 0 (open in the second set) and ends at 3 (closed in the first).
**5.** (c) — "$\ge$" gives two outer rays; (a) is the solution of $|x|\le 4$.
:::

## 11.3 Functions

1. If $f(x)=2x+3$, then $f^{-1}(x)$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{x+3}{2}$ (b) $\frac{x-3}{2}$ (c) $2x-3$ (d) $\frac{2}{x-3}$
2. The domain of $f(x)=\sqrt{x-4}$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $(4,\infty)$ (b) $[4,\infty)$ (c) $(-\infty,4]$ (d) $\mathbb{R}$
3. If $f(x)=x^2$ and $g(x)=x+1$, then $(f\circ g)(2)$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 5 (b) 9 (c) 7 (d) 25
4. The range of $f(x)=x^2+1$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $[0,\infty)$ (b) $(1,\infty)$ (c) $[1,\infty)$ (d) $\mathbb{R}$
5. The domain of $f(x)=\frac{1}{x^2-9}$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\mathbb{R}-\{3\}$ (b) $\mathbb{R}-\{-3\}$ (c) $\mathbb{R}-\{3,-3\}$ (d) $\mathbb{R}$
6. If $f(x)=\log_2 x$, then $f(8)$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 2 (b) 3 (c) 4 (d) 16

::: note Answers to 11.3
**1.** (b) — put $y=2x+3$ and solve for $x$: $x=(y-3)/2$.
**2.** (b) — we need $x-4\ge 0$; $\sqrt{0}$ is defined, so 4 is included.
**3.** (b) — $g(2)=3$, then $f(3)=9$; (a) wrongly computes $(g\circ f)(2)=5$.
**4.** (c) — $x^2\ge 0$, so $x^2+1\ge 1$ and the value 1 is attained at $x=0$.
**5.** (c) — the denominator vanishes at both $x=3$ and $x=-3$.
**6.** (b) — $2^3=8$.
:::

## 11.4 Curve Sketching

1. Which of the following is an even function? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $x^3$ (b) $\sin x$ (c) $x^2+\cos x$ (d) $x+\frac{1}{x}$
2. The period of $\sin 2x$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $2\pi$ (b) $\pi$ (c) $\frac{\pi}{2}$ (d) $4\pi$
3. The graph of $y=x^3$ is symmetric about <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) the $x$-axis (b) the $y$-axis (c) the origin (d) the line $y=x$
4. The amplitude of $y=3\sin 2x$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 2 (b) 3 (c) 6 (d) $\pi$
5. The graph of $y=\frac{1}{x+2}$ has a vertical asymptote at <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $x=2$ (b) $x=-2$ (c) $y=-2$ (d) $x=0$
6. The graph of $y=\ln x$ passes through the point <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $(0,1)$ (b) $(1,0)$ (c) $(e,0)$ (d) $(0,0)$

::: note Answers to 11.4
**1.** (c) — both $x^2$ and $\cos x$ are even, so their sum is even; $x^3$, $\sin x$ and $x+1/x$ are odd.
**2.** (b) — the period of $\sin bx$ is $2\pi/b=\pi$; (a) forgets to divide by $b$.
**3.** (c) — $(-x)^3=-x^3$, so the function is odd and symmetric about the origin.
**4.** (b) — amplitude is $|a|$ in $a\sin bx$; the 2 changes the period, not the height.
**5.** (b) — the denominator is zero at $x=-2$.
**6.** (b) — $\ln 1=0$; the graph never touches $x=0$.
:::

## 11.5 Sequence and Series

1. The 10th term of the A.P. $3, 7, 11, \dots$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 39 (b) 43 (c) 40 (d) 36
2. The sum of the first 20 natural numbers is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 200 (b) 210 (c) 420 (d) 190
3. The 5th term of the G.P. $2, 6, 18, \dots$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 54 (b) 162 (c) 486 (d) 90
4. The sum to infinity of $1+\frac12+\frac14+\cdots$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 1 (b) $\frac32$ (c) 2 (d) $\infty$
5. The G.M. of 4 and 16 is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 10 (b) 8 (c) 6.4 (d) 12
6. If $a, b, c$ are in H.P., then $\frac1a,\frac1b,\frac1c$ are in <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) G.P. (b) H.P. (c) A.P. (d) none of these
7. The sum of the first 15 odd natural numbers is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 225 (b) 240 (c) 120 (d) 255

::: note Answers to 11.5
**1.** (a) — $t_{10}=a+9d=3+9\times4=39$; (b) uses $10d$ by mistake.
**2.** (b) — $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}=\frac{20\times21}{2}=210$.
**3.** (b) — $r=3$, so $t_5=2\times3^4=162$; (c) is $t_6$.
**4.** (c) — $S_\infty=\frac{a}{1-r}=\frac{1}{1-1/2}=2$.
**5.** (b) — $\sqrt{4\times16}=8$; (a) is the A.M. and (c) is the H.M.
**6.** (c) — that is the definition of a harmonic progression.
**7.** (a) — the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers is $n^2=225$.
:::

## 11.6 Matrices and Determinants

1. For two matrices $A$ and $B$ for which the product is defined, $(AB)^T$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $A^TB^T$ (b) $B^TA^T$ (c) $AB$ (d) $BA$
2. The determinant of the matrix with rows $(2,3)$ and $(1,4)$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 11 (b) 5 (c) $-5$ (d) 8
3. If $A$ is a $2\times2$ matrix with $|A|=5$, then $|2A|$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 10 (b) 20 (c) 25 (d) 40
4. For a square matrix $A$, $A\cdot\text{adj}A$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $A^2$ (b) $|A|\,I$ (c) $I$ (d) $O$
5. The inverse of a square matrix $A$ exists if and only if <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $|A|=0$ (b) $|A|\ne 0$ (c) $A$ is symmetric (d) $A=A^T$
6. If $A$ is a $3\times3$ matrix with $|A|=4$, then $|\text{adj}A|$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 4 (b) 8 (c) 16 (d) 64

::: note Answers to 11.6
**1.** (b) — the transpose of a product reverses the order; (a) is the commonest slip.
**2.** (b) — $2\times4-3\times1=5$.
**3.** (b) — for an $n\times n$ matrix $|kA|=k^n|A|$, so $2^2\times5=20$; (a) multiplies once only.
**4.** (b) — $A\,\text{adj}A=|A|I$, the identity behind the inverse formula.
**5.** (b) — a singular matrix ($|A|=0$) has no inverse.
**6.** (c) — $|\text{adj}A|=|A|^{n-1}=4^2=16$.
:::

## 11.7 Quadratic Equation

1. The roots of $x^2-5x+6=0$ are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 1, 6 (b) 2, 3 (c) $-2,-3$ (d) $-1,-6$
2. The nature of the roots of $2x^2-4x+2=0$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) real and distinct (b) real and equal (c) imaginary (d) irrational
3. The sum and product of the roots of $2x^2-7x+3=0$ are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac72,\frac32$ (b) $-\frac72,\frac32$ (c) $\frac72,-\frac32$ (d) $7,3$
4. The quadratic equation whose roots are $2$ and $-3$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $x^2-x-6=0$ (b) $x^2+x-6=0$ (c) $x^2+x+6=0$ (d) $x^2-5x-6=0$
5. If the roots of $x^2+kx+9=0$ are equal, then $k$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 6 only (b) $-6$ only (c) $\pm6$ (d) $\pm3$
6. If $\alpha,\beta$ are the roots of $x^2-4x+1=0$, then $\alpha^2+\beta^2$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 16 (b) 14 (c) 18 (d) 12

::: note Answers to 11.7
**1.** (b) — $(x-2)(x-3)=0$; (c) has the wrong signs.
**2.** (b) — $D=16-16=0$, so the roots are real and equal.
**3.** (a) — sum $=-b/a=7/2$, product $=c/a=3/2$; (b) forgets the minus sign in $-b/a$.
**4.** (b) — sum $=-1$, product $=-6$, so $x^2-(\text{sum})x+\text{product}=x^2+x-6$.
**5.** (c) — $D=k^2-36=0$ gives $k=\pm6$; both signs work.
**6.** (b) — $\alpha^2+\beta^2=(\alpha+\beta)^2-2\alpha\beta=16-2=14$.
:::

## 11.8 Complex Numbers

1. The value of $i^{46}$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $1$ (b) $-1$ (c) $i$ (d) $-i$
2. $(2+3i)(2-3i)$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $-5$ (b) $13$ (c) $4-9i$ (d) $7$
3. The modulus of $3+4i$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 7 (b) 25 (c) 5 (d) 1
4. The square roots of $-3+4i$ are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\pm(1+2i)$ (b) $\pm(2+i)$ (c) $1-2i$ only (d) $\pm(2-i)$
5. The conjugate of $\frac{1}{1+i}$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{1-i}{2}$ (b) $\frac{1+i}{2}$ (c) $1-i$ (d) $\frac{1}{1-i}$ only
6. The argument of the complex number $-1$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0$ (b) $\frac{\pi}{2}$ (c) $\pi$ (d) $\frac{3\pi}{2}$

::: note Answers to 11.8
**1.** (b) — $46=4\times11+2$, so $i^{46}=i^2=-1$.
**2.** (b) — $z\bar z=a^2+b^2=4+9=13$; (c) forgets $i^2=-1$.
**3.** (c) — $\sqrt{9+16}=5$; (b) is $|z|^2$.
**4.** (a) — $(1+2i)^2=1-4+4i=-3+4i$; both $\pm$ roots are needed, and (c) leaves out the other one.
**5.** (b) — $\frac{1}{1+i}=\frac{1-i}{2}$, whose conjugate is $\frac{1+i}{2}$; (a) stops one step short.
**6.** (c) — the point $-1$ lies on the negative real axis, angle $\pi$.
:::

## 11.9 Inverse Circular Functions

1. $\sin^{-1}\left(\frac12\right)$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{\pi}{3}$ (b) $\frac{\pi}{6}$ (c) $\frac{\pi}{4}$ (d) $\frac{5\pi}{6}$
2. The range (principal values) of $\cos^{-1}x$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $[-\frac{\pi}{2},\frac{\pi}{2}]$ (b) $[0,\pi]$ (c) $(0,\pi)$ (d) $[0,2\pi]$
3. $\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x$ equals, for $-1\le x\le 1$, <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0$ (b) $\pi$ (c) $\frac{\pi}{2}$ (d) $\frac{\pi}{4}$
4. $\tan^{-1}\frac12+\tan^{-1}\frac13$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{\pi}{6}$ (b) $\frac{\pi}{3}$ (c) $\frac{\pi}{4}$ (d) $\frac{\pi}{2}$
5. $\cos^{-1}\left(-\frac12\right)$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{\pi}{3}$ (b) $-\frac{\pi}{3}$ (c) $\frac{2\pi}{3}$ (d) $\frac{4\pi}{3}$
6. $\sin\left(\cos^{-1}\frac35\right)$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac35$ (b) $\frac45$ (c) $\frac34$ (d) $\frac53$

::: note Answers to 11.9
**1.** (b) — principal value in $[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$; $5\pi/6$ also has sine $\frac12$ but is outside the range.
**2.** (b) — $\cos^{-1}$ takes values in $[0,\pi]$; (a) is the range of $\sin^{-1}$.
**3.** (c) — the standard complementary identity.
**4.** (c) — $\tan^{-1}\frac{\frac12+\frac13}{1-\frac16}=\tan^{-1}1=\frac{\pi}{4}$.
**5.** (c) — $\cos^{-1}(-x)=\pi-\cos^{-1}x=\pi-\frac{\pi}{3}$; (b) lies outside $[0,\pi]$.
**6.** (b) — a 3-4-5 triangle: if the cosine is $3/5$, the sine is $4/5$.
:::

## 11.10 Trigonometric Equations and General Values

1. The general solution of $\sin x=\frac12$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $2n\pi\pm\frac{\pi}{6}$ (b) $n\pi+(-1)^n\frac{\pi}{6}$ (c) $n\pi\pm\frac{\pi}{6}$ (d) $n\pi+\frac{\pi}{6}$
2. The general solution of $\cos x=0$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $n\pi$ (b) $2n\pi$ (c) $(2n+1)\frac{\pi}{2}$ (d) $n\pi+\frac{\pi}{4}$
3. The general solution of $\tan x=\sqrt{3}$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $n\pi+\frac{\pi}{3}$ (b) $2n\pi+\frac{\pi}{3}$ (c) $n\pi\pm\frac{\pi}{3}$ (d) $n\pi+\frac{\pi}{6}$
4. The number of solutions of $\sin x=\frac12$ in $[0,2\pi]$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 1 (b) 2 (c) 3 (d) 4
5. If $\cos x=\cos\alpha$, then the general value of $x$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $n\pi+\alpha$ (b) $n\pi+(-1)^n\alpha$ (c) $2n\pi\pm\alpha$ (d) $2n\pi+\alpha$
6. The smallest positive solution of $\tan x=-1$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{\pi}{4}$ (b) $\frac{3\pi}{4}$ (c) $\frac{5\pi}{4}$ (d) $\frac{7\pi}{4}$

::: note Answers to 11.10
**1.** (b) — for $\sin x=\sin\alpha$ the general value is $n\pi+(-1)^n\alpha$ with $\alpha=\pi/6$.
**2.** (c) — cosine vanishes at odd multiples of $\pi/2$; (a) is the answer for $\sin x=0$.
**3.** (a) — tangent has period $\pi$, so $n\pi+\alpha$ with $\alpha=\pi/3$.
**4.** (b) — $x=\frac{\pi}{6}$ and $x=\frac{5\pi}{6}$.
**5.** (c) — cosine repeats every $2\pi$ and is even, hence $\pm\alpha$.
**6.** (b) — tangent is negative in the second quadrant; the reference angle is $\pi/4$, giving $\pi-\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{3\pi}{4}$.
:::

## 11.11 Straight Line

1. The perpendicular distance of the point $(2,1)$ from the line $3x+4y+5=0$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 3 (b) 5 (c) $\frac{15}{25}$ (d) 15
2. The distance of the origin from the line $5x+12y-26=0$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 26 (b) 13 (c) 2 (d) 1
3. The equations of the bisectors of the angles between the coordinate axes are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $y=0$ and $x=0$ (b) $y=\pm x$ (c) $y=\pm 2x$ (d) $x+y=1$
4. The distance of the point $(-1,3)$ from the line $x+y=0$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 2 (b) $\sqrt{2}$ (c) $2\sqrt{2}$ (d) 1
5. The two bisectors of the angles between two intersecting straight lines are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) parallel (b) perpendicular to each other (c) coincident (d) inclined at $45^\circ$ always

::: note Answers to 11.11
**1.** (a) — $\frac{|3(2)+4(1)+5|}{\sqrt{9+16}}=\frac{15}{5}=3$; (c) forgets the square root.
**2.** (c) — $\frac{|-26|}{\sqrt{25+144}}=\frac{26}{13}=2$.
**3.** (b) — the axes are perpendicular, so the bisectors make $45^\circ$ with each: $y=\pm x$.
**4.** (b) — $\frac{|-1+3|}{\sqrt{2}}=\sqrt{2}$; (a) forgets to divide by $\sqrt{2}$.
**5.** (b) — the internal and external bisectors always meet at a right angle.
:::

## 11.12 Pair of Straight Lines

1. The equation $x^2-y^2=0$ represents <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) a circle (b) the lines $y=x$ and $y=-x$ (c) one line (d) parallel lines
2. The angle between the lines $x^2-5xy+6y^2=0$ has $\tan\theta$ equal to <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac17$ (b) $\frac15$ (c) $1$ (d) $\frac{1}{6}$
3. The lines given by $ax^2+2hxy+by^2=0$ are perpendicular if <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $h=0$ (b) $a=b$ (c) $a+b=0$ (d) $ab=h^2$
4. The lines given by $ax^2+2hxy+by^2=0$ coincide if <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $h^2=ab$ (b) $h=0$ (c) $a+b=0$ (d) $a=b$
5. A homogeneous equation of the second degree in $x$ and $y$ represents a pair of lines <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) parallel to the axes (b) passing through the origin (c) not intersecting (d) of equal slope
6. The equation of the bisectors of $ax^2+2hxy+by^2=0$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{x^2-y^2}{a-b}=\frac{xy}{h}$ (b) $\frac{x^2+y^2}{a+b}=\frac{xy}{h}$ (c) $\frac{x^2-y^2}{a+b}=\frac{xy}{h}$ (d) $\frac{x^2-y^2}{a-b}=\frac{xy}{a}$

::: note Answers to 11.12
**1.** (b) — $(x-y)(x+y)=0$.
**2.** (a) — $a=1,\ h=-\frac52,\ b=6$; $\tan\theta=\frac{2\sqrt{h^2-ab}}{|a+b|}=\frac{2\times\frac12}{7}=\frac17$.
**3.** (c) — the angle formula has $a+b$ in the denominator; perpendicular means that denominator is zero.
**4.** (a) — $h^2-ab=0$ makes the numerator of $\tan\theta$ zero, so the angle is zero.
**5.** (b) — with no linear or constant term the origin satisfies the equation.
**6.** (a) — the standard result; (b) and (c) mix up the signs.
:::

## 11.13 Coordinates in Space

1. The distance between $(1,2,3)$ and $(4,6,15)$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 11 (b) 12 (c) 13 (d) 14
2. The direction cosines of the $x$-axis are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $(0,1,0)$ (b) $(1,0,0)$ (c) $(0,0,1)$ (d) $(1,1,1)$
3. The distance of the point $(3,-4,5)$ from the $xy$-plane is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 3 (b) 4 (c) 5 (d) $\sqrt{50}$
4. The direction cosines of the line joining $(1,2,3)$ and $(3,5,9)$ are <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac27,\frac37,\frac67$ (b) $\frac13,\frac13,\frac13$ (c) $2,3,6$ (d) $\frac{2}{49},\frac{3}{49},\frac{6}{49}$
5. Which of the following can be the direction cosines of a line? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac12,\frac12,\frac12$ (b) $\frac13,\frac23,\frac23$ (c) $\frac12,\frac13,\frac14$ (d) $1,1,1$
6. The distance of the point $(2,-3,6)$ from the origin is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 5 (b) 6 (c) 7 (d) 11

::: note Answers to 11.13
**1.** (c) — $\sqrt{3^2+4^2+12^2}=\sqrt{169}=13$.
**2.** (b) — the $x$-axis makes $0^\circ$ with itself and $90^\circ$ with the others.
**3.** (c) — the distance from the $xy$-plane is $|z|$.
**4.** (a) — direction ratios $2,3,6$ with $\sqrt{4+9+36}=7$; (c) leaves the ratios unnormalised.
**5.** (b) — only (b) satisfies $l^2+m^2+n^2=\frac19+\frac49+\frac49=1$.
**6.** (c) — $\sqrt{4+9+36}=7$.
:::

## 11.14 Vectors

1. Which pair of vectors is collinear? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $2\mathbf{i}-4\mathbf{j}$ and $-\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$ (b) $\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}$ and $\mathbf{i}-\mathbf{j}$ (c) $\mathbf{i}$ and $\mathbf{j}$ (d) $2\mathbf{i}+\mathbf{j}$ and $\mathbf{i}+2\mathbf{j}$
2. The value of $m$ for which $2\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}$ and $4\mathbf{i}+m\mathbf{j}$ are collinear is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 4 (b) 5 (c) 6 (d) 8
3. Two non-collinear vectors $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}$ are linearly independent; if $x\mathbf{a}+y\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}$ then <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $x=y=0$ (b) $x=y=1$ (c) $x=-y$ (d) $x=0$ only
4. The position vector of the midpoint of the points with position vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}$ (b) $\frac{\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}}{2}$ (c) $\frac{\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}}{2}$ (d) $\frac{2\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}}{3}$
5. The magnitude of $3\mathbf{i}+4\mathbf{j}$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 7 (b) 5 (c) 25 (d) 1
6. Three vectors are coplanar if <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) they are all unit vectors (b) one of them is a linear combination of the other two (c) they are mutually perpendicular (d) they have equal magnitudes

::: note Answers to 11.14
**1.** (a) — the second is $-\frac12$ times the first; the others have unequal component ratios.
**2.** (c) — $\frac24=\frac3m$ gives $m=6$.
**3.** (a) — that is the definition of linear independence.
**4.** (c) — the midpoint formula is the average of the position vectors.
**5.** (b) — $\sqrt{9+16}=5$; (a) adds the components.
**6.** (b) — that is the condition for coplanarity.
:::

## 11.15 Measures of Dispersion

1. The variance of the data $1,2,3,4,5$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 1 (b) 2 (c) 2.5 (d) 3
2. The coefficient of variation is defined as <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{\bar x}{\sigma}\times100$ (b) $\frac{\sigma}{\bar x}\times100$ (c) $\sigma^2\times100$ (d) $\frac{\sigma}{\sigma^2}$
3. If the mean is 50 and the standard deviation is 5, the coefficient of variation is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 10% (b) 5% (c) 25% (d) 50%
4. Karl Pearson's coefficient of skewness is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{\text{Mean}-\text{Mode}}{\text{S.D.}}$ (b) $\frac{\text{Mean}+\text{Mode}}{\text{S.D.}}$ (c) $\frac{\text{S.D.}}{\text{Mean}-\text{Mode}}$ (d) $\text{Mean}-\text{Median}$
5. If the mean is 40, the mode 30 and the S.D. 10, the coefficient of skewness is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $-1$ (b) 0 (c) 1 (d) 3
6. The skewness of a perfectly symmetrical distribution is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) positive (b) negative (c) zero (d) infinite

::: note Answers to 11.15
**1.** (b) — mean 3, squared deviations $4+1+0+1+4=10$, divide by $n=5$ to get 2; (c) wrongly divides by $n-1$.
**2.** (b) — C.V. $=\frac{\sigma}{\bar x}\times100\%$; (a) is inverted.
**3.** (a) — $\frac{5}{50}\times100=10\%$.
**4.** (a) — the mean minus the mode, divided by the standard deviation.
**5.** (c) — $\frac{40-30}{10}=1$, positive skew.
**6.** (c) — mean, median and mode coincide, so the numerator is zero.
:::

## 11.16 Probability

1. A fair die is thrown. The probability of getting a prime number is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac13$ (b) $\frac12$ (c) $\frac23$ (d) $\frac16$
2. For two events, $P(A\cup B)$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $P(A)+P(B)$ (b) $P(A)P(B)$ (c) $P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$ (d) $P(A)-P(B)$
3. If $A$ and $B$ are independent with $P(A)=0.3$ and $P(B)=0.4$, then $P(A\cap B)$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 0.7 (b) 0.1 (c) 0.12 (d) 0.58
4. Two coins are tossed. The probability of at least one head is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac14$ (b) $\frac12$ (c) $\frac34$ (d) 1
5. The probability of drawing a king or a queen from a pack of 52 cards is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{1}{13}$ (b) $\frac{2}{13}$ (c) $\frac{1}{26}$ (d) $\frac{4}{13}$
6. Two dice are thrown. The probability that the sum is 7 is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{1}{12}$ (b) $\frac{1}{9}$ (c) $\frac16$ (d) $\frac{7}{36}$

::: note Answers to 11.16
**1.** (b) — primes $2,3,5$: $\frac36=\frac12$; the trap is counting 1 as prime.
**2.** (c) — the addition law; (a) double-counts the overlap.
**3.** (c) — independence gives $0.3\times0.4=0.12$; (a) adds instead.
**4.** (c) — $1-P(\text{no head})=1-\frac14$.
**5.** (b) — $\frac{4+4}{52}=\frac{2}{13}$ (mutually exclusive events add).
**6.** (c) — six favourable outcomes out of 36.
:::

## 11.17 Limits and Continuity

1. $\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 0 (b) 2 (c) 4 (d) does not exist
2. $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin3x}{x}$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 1 (b) 3 (c) $\frac13$ (d) 0
3. $\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{3x+1}{x+5}$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 0 (b) 1 (c) 3 (d) $\infty$
4. $\lim_{x\to0}\frac{e^x-1}{x}$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 0 (b) 1 (c) $e$ (d) $\infty$
5. $\lim_{x\to\infty}\left(1+\frac1x\right)^x$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 1 (b) 0 (c) $e$ (d) $\infty$
6. The function $f(x)=\frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ for $x\ne 1$, $f(1)=k$, is continuous at $x=1$ when $k$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 0 (b) 1 (c) 2 (d) $-2$

::: note Answers to 11.17
**1.** (c) — factorise: $\frac{(x-2)(x+2)}{x-2}=x+2\to4$; it is the $\frac00$ form, not "does not exist".
**2.** (b) — $\frac{\sin3x}{x}=3\cdot\frac{\sin3x}{3x}\to3$; (a) forgets the factor 3.
**3.** (c) — divide by $x$: the ratio of leading coefficients $3/1$.
**4.** (b) — a standard limit.
**5.** (c) — the definition of $e$; (a) is the trap of putting $\frac1x\to0$ first.
**6.** (c) — the limit is $x+1\to2$, so continuity needs $f(1)=2$.
:::

## 11.18 Derivatives

1. $\frac{d}{dx}(x^5)$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $x^4$ (b) $5x^4$ (c) $5x^5$ (d) $\frac{x^6}{6}$
2. $\frac{d}{dx}(e^{2x})$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $e^{2x}$ (b) $2e^{x}$ (c) $2e^{2x}$ (d) $2xe^{2x}$
3. The slope of the tangent to $y=x^2$ at $x=3$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 3 (b) 6 (c) 9 (d) 2
4. $\frac{d}{dx}(x\sin x)$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\cos x$ (b) $x\cos x$ (c) $\sin x+x\cos x$ (d) $\sin x-x\cos x$
5. If $x^2+y^2=25$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{x}{y}$ (b) $-\frac{x}{y}$ (c) $-\frac{y}{x}$ (d) $\frac{y}{x}$
6. If $x=t^2$ and $y=2t$, then $\frac{dy}{dx}$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $t$ (b) $\frac1t$ (c) $2t$ (d) $\frac{1}{2t}$
7. The function $f(x)=x^2-4x+5$ has a minimum at <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $x=1$ (b) $x=2$ (c) $x=4$ (d) $x=-2$
8. For $f(x)=x^3$, the point $x=0$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) a maximum (b) a minimum (c) a point of inflection (d) a discontinuity

::: note Answers to 11.18
**1.** (b) — power rule; (d) is the integral.
**2.** (c) — the chain rule brings down the inner derivative 2.
**3.** (b) — $y'=2x=6$; (c) gives the value of $y$, not the slope.
**4.** (c) — product rule: $1\cdot\sin x+x\cos x$.
**5.** (b) — $2x+2y\,y'=0$.
**6.** (b) — $\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}=\frac{2}{2t}=\frac1t$.
**7.** (b) — $f'(x)=2x-4=0$ at $x=2$ and $f''=2>0$.
**8.** (c) — $f''(x)=6x$ changes sign at 0, so the curve changes concavity there; $f'(0)=0$ but $f$ keeps increasing, so it is not an extremum.
:::

## 11.19 Anti-derivatives

1. $\int x^3\,dx$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $3x^2+C$ (b) $\frac{x^4}{4}+C$ (c) $x^4+C$ (d) $\frac{x^3}{3}+C$
2. $\int_0^2 x\,dx$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 1 (b) 2 (c) 4 (d) 0
3. $\int \frac{2x}{x^2+1}\,dx$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\frac{1}{x^2+1}+C$ (b) $\ln(x^2+1)+C$ (c) $\tan^{-1}x+C$ (d) $2\ln x+C$
4. $\int xe^x\,dx$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $e^x(x-1)+C$ (b) $e^x(x+1)+C$ (c) $xe^x+C$ (d) $\frac{x^2}{2}e^x+C$
5. The area under $y=x$ from $x=0$ to $x=4$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 4 (b) 8 (c) 16 (d) 2
6. $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\,dx$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 0 (b) 1 (c) $-1$ (d) $\frac{\pi}{2}$
7. $\int \frac1x\,dx$ equals <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\ln|x|+C$ (b) $-\frac{1}{x^2}+C$ (c) $\frac{1}{x^2}+C$ (d) $x+C$

::: note Answers to 11.19
**1.** (b) — raise the power by one and divide by it; (a) is differentiation.
**2.** (b) — $\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^2=2$.
**3.** (b) — the numerator is the derivative of the denominator.
**4.** (a) — by parts: $xe^x-\int e^x\,dx=e^x(x-1)$; (b) has the wrong sign.
**5.** (b) — $\left[\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^4=8$, which is also the area of the triangle $\frac12\cdot4\cdot4$.

## 11.20 Computational Methods

1. The bisection method can be applied to $f(x)=0$ on $[a,b]$ if <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $f(a)f(b)>0$ (b) $f(a)f(b)<0$ (c) $f(a)=f(b)$ (d) $f'(a)=0$
2. The Newton–Raphson iteration formula is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $x_{n+1}=x_n+\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$ (b) $x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$ (c) $x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f'(x_n)}{f(x_n)}$ (d) $x_{n+1}=\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$
3. The first midpoint in the bisection method for a root in $[1,2]$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 1 (b) 1.25 (c) 1.5 (d) 2
4. Simpson's rule requires the number of sub-intervals to be <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) odd (b) even (c) a multiple of 3 (d) any number
5. For $f(x)=x^2-2$ with $x_0=2$, the first Newton–Raphson approximation $x_1$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 1.5 (b) 1.4 (c) 1.414 (d) 1
6. The trapezoidal rule with $n=2$ for $\int_0^2x^2\,dx$ gives <span class="marks">[1]</span>

::: note Answers to 11.20
**1.** (b) — opposite signs at the ends guarantee a root in between for a continuous $f$.
**2.** (b) — subtract the ratio; (a) has the wrong sign and (c) inverts the ratio.
**3.** (c) — $\frac{1+2}{2}=1.5$.
**4.** (b) — Simpson's $\frac13$ rule pairs the intervals.
**5.** (a) — $2-\frac{2}{4}=1.5$.
**6.** (b) — $h=1$: $\frac{1}{2}[0+2(1)+4]=3$; the exact value $\frac83$ is what Simpson's rule gives with $n=2$.
:::

## 11.21 Mechanics

1. The resultant of two forces $P$ and $Q$ acting at right angles is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $P+Q$ (b) $\sqrt{P^2+Q^2}$ (c) $P-Q$ (d) $PQ$
2. Forces of 3 N and 4 N act at right angles. Their resultant is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 7 N (b) 1 N (c) 5 N (d) 12 N
3. The horizontal component of a 10 N force acting at $30^\circ$ above the horizontal is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 5 N (b) $5\sqrt{3}$ N (c) 10 N (d) $10\sqrt{3}$ N
4. A particle starts with $u=5\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ and has $a=2\ \text{m s}^{-2}$. Its velocity after 3 s is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 6 m s⁻¹ (b) 9 m s⁻¹ (c) 11 m s⁻¹ (d) 15 m s⁻¹
5. A body falls from rest for 4 s ($g=10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$). The distance fallen is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 40 m (b) 80 m (c) 160 m (d) 20 m
6. The acceleration of a body down a smooth plane inclined at $30^\circ$ ($g=10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$) is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 10 m s⁻² (b) $5\sqrt{3}$ m s⁻² (c) 5 m s⁻² (d) 20 m s⁻²
7. A ball is thrown vertically up at $20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ ($g=10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$). The maximum height reached is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 10 m (b) 20 m (c) 40 m (d) 200 m

::: note Answers to 11.21
**1.** (b) — the parallelogram law with $\cos90^\circ=0$.
**2.** (c) — $\sqrt{9+16}=5$ N; (a) simply adds the magnitudes.
**3.** (b) — $10\cos30^\circ=5\sqrt{3}$; (a) is the vertical component.
**4.** (c) — $v=u+at=5+6=11$.
**5.** (b) — $s=\frac12gt^2=\frac12\times10\times16=80$ m; (c) forgets the $\frac12$.
**6.** (c) — $a=g\sin\theta=10\times\frac12=5$.
**7.** (b) — $h=\frac{u^2}{2g}=\frac{400}{20}=20$ m.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Group A is eleven one-mark MCQs; they are spread across algebra, trigonometry, geometry, vectors, statistics, calculus and the computational-or-mechanics unit.
- Logic: the contrapositive of $p\Rightarrow q$ is $\sim q\Rightarrow\sim p$; $n(A\cup B)=n(A)+n(B)-n(A\cap B)$.
- Algebra: $t_n=a+(n-1)d$, $S_\infty=\frac{a}{1-r}$, $\alpha+\beta=-\frac ba$, $\alpha\beta=\frac ca$, $|kA|=k^n|A|$, $|\text{adj}A|=|A|^{n-1}$, $|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$.
- Trigonometry: $\sin^{-1}x+\cos^{-1}x=\frac{\pi}{2}$; general values $n\pi+(-1)^n\alpha$, $2n\pi\pm\alpha$, $n\pi+\alpha$.
- Geometry: distance $=\frac{|ax_1+by_1+c|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2}}$; a pair of lines is perpendicular when $a+b=0$; $l^2+m^2+n^2=1$.
- Statistics and probability: C.V. $=\frac{\sigma}{\bar x}\times100$; $P(A\cup B)=P(A)+P(B)-P(A\cap B)$.
- Calculus: $\int\frac{f'(x)}{f(x)}dx=\ln|f(x)|$; standard limits $\frac{\sin x}{x}\to1$ and $\frac{e^x-1}{x}\to1$.
- Computational methods: $x_{n+1}=x_n-\frac{f(x_n)}{f'(x_n)}$; Simpson's rule needs an even number of intervals.
