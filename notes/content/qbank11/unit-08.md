Group A of the Biology paper is eleven marks — five from Botany, six from
Zoology — and it is the only part of the paper where a candidate who has read
the book carefully can score full marks in twelve minutes. This chapter is the
drill for it. Every syllabus unit of Grade 11 Biology gets its own bank of
multiple-choice questions, an answer key that gives a **reason** rather than a
letter, and a table of the facts that objective questions keep coming back to.

::: key How to use this bank
Do not read the questions. **Answer** them, on paper, covering the key. Retrieval
is what builds memory; recognition feels like learning and is not. Mark the ones
you got wrong, and come back to only those after three days, then after ten. The
reason line in the key matters more than the letter — if you cannot say *why* the
other three options are wrong, you have not finished the question.
:::

::: caution About the year tags in this book
A few questions carry a source tag. Everything untagged is an original or
predicted question written to the CDC syllabus. NEB has released one official
Grade 11 Biology paper (Botany + Zoology, Sub. Code 114) but **does not state
which year's examination it came from**, so questions drawn from it are tagged
`(NEB released paper)` and never with a year. No question in this book carries a
year we could not verify from a primary source.
:::

::: note Answer key — 8.1
**1.** (b) — cellulose is the wall material of every plant cell on Earth; starch is only a store.
**2.** (b) — the C–O–C link between two sugars is glycosidic; peptide belongs to protein, phosphodiester to nucleic acid.
**3.** (c) — in sucrose both anomeric carbons are locked in the linkage, so no free aldehyde group remains.
**4.** (b) — glycogen is animal starch, stored in liver and muscle; inulin is a plant store (*Dahlia*).
**5.** (b) — 20 standard amino acids; 64 is the number of codons, a classic swap.
**6.** (c) — the –COOH of one residue condenses with the –NH₂ of the next, releasing one water molecule.
**7.** (b) — hydrogen bonds between C=O and N–H hold the α-helix and β-pleated sheet; peptide bonds give the primary structure.
**8.** (b) — one glycerol plus three fatty acids gives a triglyceride.
**9.** (b) — the phosphate head is hydrophilic and the two fatty-acid tails hydrophobic, so it forms a bilayer in water.
**10.** (c) — uracil replaces thymine in RNA; the two differ only by a methyl group.
**11.** (b) — Chargaff: A = T = 30 %, so A + T = 60 % and G + C = 40 %, giving G = 20 %. Option (a) is chosen by students who think G = A.
**12.** (b) — deoxyribose lacks the –OH at carbon 2, which is what makes DNA chemically stable.
**13.** (a) — Watson and Crick, 1953, using Franklin's X-ray data.
**14.** (b) — the enzyme provides an alternative path of lower activation energy; it never shifts the equilibrium.
**15.** (b) — Fischer, 1894; Koshland's later induced-fit model is option (a)'s trap.
**16.** (b) — a competitive inhibitor resembles the substrate and occupies the active site; a non-competitive one binds elsewhere.
**17.** (b) — a loosely bound one is a coenzyme; apoenzyme is the protein part alone.
**18.** (b) — Mg²⁺ sits in the porphyrin ring; iron is in haemoglobin and cytochromes.
**19.** (b) — breaking hydrogen bonds absorbs much heat, so ponds and cells warm slowly.
**20.** (b) — 1838–39; Hooke only saw and named cells, Virchow added the third postulate.
**21.** (a) — Virchow, 1855.
**22.** (c) — prokaryotes do have a wall, ribosomes and a membrane; only the nuclear envelope is missing.
**23.** (b) — 70S = 50S + 30S; 80S is eukaryotic cytoplasmic.
**24.** (c) — peptidoglycan (murein); chitin is fungal, cellulose plant, pectin the middle lamella.
**25.** (b) — calcium and magnesium pectate cement adjacent walls; it dissolves in ripening fruit.
**26.** (c) — Singer and Nicolson; Danielli–Davson is the older sandwich model.
**27.** (a) — cristae carry the oxysomes (F₁ particles); grana and thylakoids belong to the chloroplast, and mixing the two words is the commonest error here.
**28.** (b) — their own circular DNA and 70S ribosomes let them make some of their own proteins, the basis of the endosymbiotic theory.
**29.** (a) — grana are the stacks; stroma is the surrounding matrix.
**30.** (c) — amyloplast stores starch, elaioplast oil, aleuroplast protein.
**31.** (b) — ribosomes stud the cytoplasmic face of RER, which therefore exports protein.
**32.** (b) — SER also detoxifies drugs and stores Ca²⁺ in muscle.
**33.** (a) — many small unconnected dictyosomes, unlike the single stacked Golgi of animal cells.
**34.** (b) — de Duve's term; on injury the membrane ruptures and the acid hydrolases autolyse the cell.
**35.** (b) — 60S + 40S = 80S; 50S + 30S = 70S. Note the subunit numbers do not add arithmetically, because S is a sedimentation coefficient, not a mass.
**36.** (c) — rRNA is transcribed and packed with protein in the nucleolus.
**37.** (a) — metacentric gives equal arms and a V shape at anaphase; telocentric has the centromere at the very end.
**38.** (b) — interphase is about 90–95 % of the cycle; it is growth, not rest.
**39.** (b) — S for synthesis; after S the DNA content has doubled but the chromosome number has not.
**40.** (c) — mitosis is equational: 2n stays 2n, so 24 chromosomes in each of the two daughter cells.
**41.** (b) — meiosis is reductional: 2n = 24 gives n = 12 in each of the four cells. Answering 24 here and 12 in question 40 is the single commonest cell-division error in the board exam.
**42.** (c) — pachytene, when the bivalent has four chromatids and recombination nodules appear.
**43.** (c) — the homologues begin to separate at diplotene and the crossover points show as X-shaped chiasmata.
**44.** (a) — homologous pairs separate in anaphase I, halving the number; meiosis II merely separates sister chromatids.
**45.** (c) — amitosis, seen in *Amoeba*, bacteria, and in the foetal membranes and diseased cells of higher organisms.
:::

::: caution The distractor that catches everyone: 2n versus n
Questions 40 and 41 are the same cell dividing two different ways. Fix the two
sentences in your head and never compute them again:
**mitosis — one cell, two daughters, 2n each, chromosomes identical to the parent.**
**meiosis — one cell, four daughters, n each, chromosomes recombined.**
The number is halved **once**, in anaphase I, and nowhere else. Meiosis II does
not halve anything; it separates sister chromatids exactly as mitosis does,
which is why it is called the equational division.
:::

::: example Reading a cell-division question
**Problem.** A pollen mother cell of a plant contains 36 chromosomes. How many
chromosomes will be present in (i) each pollen grain, (ii) each cell of the
tapetum dividing mitotically, (iii) the endosperm?

**Solution.** The pollen mother cell is diploid, so $2n = 36$ and $n = 18$.

(i) A pollen grain is formed by **meiosis**, so it is haploid: **18**.
(ii) The tapetum is ordinary somatic tissue dividing by **mitosis**, which keeps
the number: **36**.
(iii) The endosperm of an angiosperm is **triploid** (one male nucleus plus two
polar nuclei), so it has $3n = 3 \times 18 =$ **54**.

The whole question is answered by knowing which cells are the products of which
division. Never multiply or divide by 2 without first naming the division.
:::

::: example An energy-flow calculation the way the board asks it
**Problem.** In a grassland the producers fix
$9600\ \text{kJ m}^{-2}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$ of solar energy and use
$6400\ \text{kJ m}^{-2}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$ of it in their own respiration. Find the net
primary productivity, and the energy that reaches a snake feeding on frogs.

**Solution.** First the productivity:

$$ \text{NPP} = \text{GPP} - R = 9600 - 6400 = 3200\ \text{kJ m}^{-2}\ \text{yr}^{-1} $$

Only the NPP is available to the next trophic level. Now apply the ten per cent
law step by step, naming the level each time:

- grasshopper (2nd level): $0.10 \times 3200 = 320\ \text{kJ m}^{-2}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$
- frog (3rd level): $0.10 \times 320 = 32\ \text{kJ m}^{-2}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$
- snake (4th level): $0.10 \times 32 = 3.2\ \text{kJ m}^{-2}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$

So the snake receives $3.2\ \text{kJ m}^{-2}\ \text{yr}^{-1}$. Notice that the answer
is taken from NPP, not from GPP — starting from 9600 would give 9.6 and lose the
mark.
:::

Ask one question only: **do the two organs have the same basic plan and the same
embryonic origin?** If yes they are homologous, whatever they are used for
(a whale's flipper and your arm). If no they are analogous, however alike they
look (a butterfly's wing and a bird's wing). Homology means divergence from a
shared ancestor; analogy means convergence on a shared job.
:::

::: caution Which host is the primary host?
The **primary (definitive) host is the one in which the parasite reproduces
sexually.** For *Plasmodium* the sexual cycle is completed in the mosquito, so
the **mosquito is the primary host and the human is the secondary
(intermediate) host** — even though the human is the one who is ill. Deciding
by "which one matters more to us" is what makes this the single most-missed
one-mark question in Zoology.

::: example Eliminating three options before you choose one
**Problem.** *The primary host of Plasmodium is (a) man (b) female Anopheles
mosquito (c) female Culex mosquito (d) housefly.* Answer it without recalling
the definition.

**Solution.** Work by elimination, which is faster and safer than recall.

*Step 1 — use the biology you are sure of.* Malaria is spread by *Anopheles*,
not *Culex* (which spreads filariasis) and not the housefly (which spreads
typhoid and cholera mechanically). Options (c) and (d) go at once. Two options
remain and the question is already worth half a mark in expectation.

*Step 2 — find the discriminating rule.* The remaining pair, man and mosquito,
differ only in which cycle happens where. The rule is: **the definitive host is
where the sexual cycle occurs.** Gametocytes are taken up by the mosquito, and
fertilisation and zygote formation happen in its gut.

*Step 3 — apply and check.* Sexual cycle in the mosquito, therefore the
mosquito is primary, therefore **(b)**. Check the leftover: man harbours the
asexual schizogony, so man is the secondary host — which is consistent, so the
answer stands.

Notice that steps 1 and 3 used ordinary content knowledge and only step 2 used
the definition. In a bank of 300 questions you will not recall every
definition; you will almost always recall enough to delete two options, and a
one-in-two guess across eleven MCQs is worth about five and a half marks.
:::

## Chapter summary

This chapter is the objective half of your Biology revision: close to three
hundred one-mark questions arranged unit by unit against the CDC Grade 11
syllabus, each with an answer key that gives the reason rather than the letter.

- Group A of the NEB Biology paper carries **eleven marks, five from Botany and
  six from Zoology**, and is the only part of the paper where a wrong answer
  costs you the whole mark with no part credit. It is also the part most
  reliably won by practice.
- The marks follow the teaching hours. **Floral Diversity (30 h) and Faunal
  Diversity (34 h)** together account for half the syllabus, and the number of
  questions in sections 8.2 and 8.8 is in proportion.
- Most one-mark questions are decided by a **single discriminating fact**: 2n
  versus n, prokaryote versus eukaryote, homologous versus analogous, primary
  versus secondary host, endemic versus exotic, vulnerable versus endangered.
  The distractors in this bank are built from those exact confusions on
  purpose.
- Where the answer key says "this is the one everyone misses", stop and write
  the pair out in your own words. Those boxes mark the questions that separate
  an A from an A+.
- **Use the bank as retrieval practice, not as reading.** Cover the key, answer
  a whole section in one sitting against the clock (about thirty seconds a
  question), then mark it and read the reasons only for the ones you got wrong.
  Revisit those after two days and again after a week.
- A question in this book carries a year tag only where the paper it came from
  could be verified. Everything untagged is either an original question written
  to the CDC grid or a question from an undated official release.
