This chapter does three things. It reads the **shape of the Grade 11 Physics paper** (3 hours, full marks 75, Group A $11\times1$, Group B $8\times5$, Group C $3\times8$) and shows how the 128 teaching hours are spread over the 26 units. It then sets **two complete model papers** to that grid, each **fully solved**. Finally it lists **twenty-four predicted questions in ranked order**, each with the reason it is predicted and a short model answer.

::: caution What "model" and "predicted" mean here
The model papers are original practice papers built to the CDC pattern; they are not NEB papers and are not claimed to resemble any particular year. A **prediction** is a judgement, not a leak: nobody outside the board knows the paper. The ranking uses three public things only — the **teaching hours** of each unit, how **standard** the question is (a derivation or definition every textbook treats), and whether it is a **recurring numerical type**. Treat the list as a revision order. No question in this chapter carries a past-paper year, because Grade 11 papers are not published by NEB.
:::

## 4.1 Pattern and weight analysis of the Grade 11 Physics paper

### 4.1.1 The grid

| Part | Questions | Marks | What to expect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group A | 11 multiple-choice | $11\times1 = 11$ | one mark each; drawn from the whole syllabus |
| Group B | 8 short answer | $8\times5 = 40$ | a mix of definition, short derivation and numerical |
| Group C | 3 long answer | $3\times8 = 24$ | derivation plus numerical, often in parts such as $[3+3+2]$ |

All questions are compulsory. Time per mark is $180/75 = 2.4$ minutes: about 12 minutes for each Group B question and 19 for each Group C question.

### 4.1.2 Where the syllabus hours sit

| Area | Units | Hours | Share | Expected presence in the paper |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Mechanics | 1–8 | 45 | 35 % | the largest block: a Group C question and three or four Group B questions |
| Heat and Thermodynamics | 9–13 | 26 | 20 % | two Group B questions, sometimes a Group C part |
| Waves and Optics | 14–18 | 15 | 12 % | one or two Group B questions; mirror, lens or prism numerical |
| Electricity and Magnetism | 19–23 | 27 | 21 % | two Group B questions and often a Group C question |
| Modern Physics | 24–26 | 15 | 12 % | one Group B question; Group A items |

::: note Solutions — Group B (12–19)
**12.** Vertical fall: $h = \tfrac12gt^{2}$, so $t = \sqrt{\dfrac{2\times80}{10}} = 4\ \text{s}$. Horizontal distance $= 15\times4 = 60\ \text{m}$. At impact $v_y = gt = 40\ \text{m s}^{-1}$, $v_x = 15$; speed $= \sqrt{15^{2} + 40^{2}} = \sqrt{1825} = 42.7\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.
**13.** The rate of change of momentum of a body is proportional to the applied force and takes place in the direction of the force: $F = \dfrac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}$. Taking the rebound direction as positive, $\Delta p = 0.2\,[15 - (-20)] = 7.0\ \text{N s}$ (impulse). $F = \dfrac{7.0}{0.01} = 700\ \text{N}$.
**14.** Let $R_1$ and $R_2$ be the reactions at the left and right ends. Moments about the right end: $R_1(4) = 200(2) + 100(3) = 700$, so $R_1 = 175\ \text{N}$. Vertical balance: $R_1 + R_2 = 300$, so $R_2 = 125\ \text{N}$.
**15.** For a variable force the work is the area under the $F$–$x$ graph, $W = \int F\,dx$. $W = \int_0^4(3x + 2)\,dx = \left[\tfrac32x^{2} + 2x\right]_0^4 = 24 + 8 = 32\ \text{J}$.
**16.** The specific latent heat of vaporization is the heat needed to change $1\ \text{kg}$ of a liquid to vapour at its boiling point without change in temperature. Heat released $= mL_v + mc\Delta\theta = 0.2(2.26\times10^{6}) + 0.2(4200)(50) = 4.52\times10^{5} + 4.2\times10^{4} = 4.94\times10^{5}\ \text{J}$.
**17.** $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f} - \dfrac{1}{u} = -\dfrac{1}{10} - \dfrac{1}{15} = -\dfrac{5}{30}$, so $v = -6\ \text{cm}$ (virtual, on the same side as the object). $m = -\dfrac{v}{u} = \dfrac{6}{15} = 0.4$: erect, diminished, virtual.
**18.** $C = \dfrac{4\times8}{4 + 8} = 2.67\ \mu\text{F}$. In series each carries the same charge: $Q = CV = 2.67\times12 = 32\ \mu\text{C}$. $V_1 = \dfrac{32}{4} = 8\ \text{V}$; $V_2 = \dfrac{32}{8} = 4\ \text{V}$ (sum $12\ \text{V}$ ✓).
**19.** $E = mc^{2}$: energy and mass are equivalent. $E = 10^{-3}\times(3\times10^{8})^{2} = 9\times10^{13}\ \text{J}$. Mass defect is the difference between the sum of the masses of the separate nucleons and the mass of the nucleus; it appears as the binding energy $\Delta m\,c^{2}$.
:::

::: note Solutions — Group C (20–22)
**20. (a)** The total linear momentum of an isolated system remains constant. For two bodies in collision, by Newton's third law $F_{12} = -F_{21}$ for the same contact time $\Delta t$, so $F_{12}\Delta t = -F_{21}\Delta t$, i.e. $\Delta p_1 = -\Delta p_2$ and $\Delta(p_1 + p_2) = 0$.
**(b)** Momentum: $2(6) = 2v_1 + 4v_2$, so $v_1 + 2v_2 = 6$. Elastic: relative speed of approach equals relative speed of separation, $6 = v_2 - v_1$. Adding: $3v_2 = 12$, so $v_2 = 4\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ and $v_1 = -2\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (the $2\ \text{kg}$ body rebounds). Kinetic energy before: $\tfrac12(2)(36) = 36\ \text{J}$. After: $\tfrac12(2)(4) + \tfrac12(4)(16) = 4 + 32 = 36\ \text{J}$ ✓.
**21. (a)** (Diagram: bob of mass $m$ on a string of length $l$ at angle $\theta$ to the vertical; tension $T$ along the string; weight $mg$ downward; circle of radius $r = l\sin\theta$.) Vertically: $T\cos\theta = mg$. Horizontally the component $T\sin\theta$ provides the centripetal force: $T\sin\theta = m\omega^{2}r = m\omega^{2}l\sin\theta$, so $T = m\omega^{2}l$. Combining, $\omega^{2} = \dfrac{g}{l\cos\theta}$ and the period $T_p = \dfrac{2\pi}{\omega} = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{l\cos\theta}{g}}$.
**(b)** $T_p = 2\pi\sqrt{\dfrac{1.0\times0.5}{10}} = 2\pi\times0.2236 = 1.41\ \text{s}$. Tension: $T = \dfrac{mg}{\cos\theta} = \dfrac{0.5\times10}{0.5} = 10\ \text{N}$.
**22. (a)** E.m.f. is the work done by the source per unit charge in carrying it round the complete circuit (equal to the terminal p.d. on open circuit). Internal resistance $r$ is the resistance of the electrolyte and plates inside the cell. In a circuit with external resistance $R$, $E = IR + Ir$, so $I = \dfrac{E}{R + r}$.
**(b)** In series, $E_{tot} = 4.5\ \text{V}$ and $r_{tot} = 1.5\ \Omega$. $I = \dfrac{4.5}{3.0 + 1.5} = 1.0\ \text{A}$. Terminal p.d. $V = E - Ir = 4.5 - 1.5 = 3.0\ \text{V}$.
**(c)** $P = I^{2}R = 1^{2}\times3.0 = 3.0\ \text{W}$. Efficiency $= \dfrac{V}{E} = \dfrac{3.0}{4.5} = 66.7\ \%$.
:::

::: note Solutions — Group B (12–19)
**12.** Treat the two blocks as one system: $a = \dfrac{F}{m_1 + m_2} = \dfrac{10}{5} = 2.0\ \text{m s}^{-2}$. The string alone accelerates the $2\ \text{kg}$ block: $T = m_2a = 2\times2 = 4\ \text{N}$.
**13.** Down the incline: $mg\sin\theta - \mu mg\cos\theta = ma$, so $a = g(\sin30^{\circ} - 0.2\cos30^{\circ}) = 10(0.5 - 0.1732) = 3.27\ \text{m s}^{-2}$.
**14.** To escape, the kinetic energy given must equal the work against gravity to infinity: $\tfrac12mv_e^{2} = \dfrac{GMm}{R}$. With $GM = gR^{2}$: $v_e = \sqrt{2gR}$. $v_e = \sqrt{2\times9.8\times6.4\times10^{6}} = \sqrt{1.254\times10^{8}} = 1.12\times10^{4}\ \text{m s}^{-1} = 11.2\ \text{km s}^{-1}$.
**15.** $A = \pi r^{2} = \pi(0.5\times10^{-3})^{2} = 7.85\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^{2}$. $\Delta l = \dfrac{FL}{AY} = \dfrac{50\times2}{7.85\times10^{-7}\times2\times10^{11}} = 6.4\times10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 0.64\ \text{mm}$.
**16.** Linear expansivity is the fractional increase in length per unit original length per kelvin rise in temperature, $\alpha = \dfrac{\Delta l}{l\Delta\theta}$. $\Delta l = 12\times1.2\times10^{-5}\times30 = 4.3\times10^{-3}\ \text{m} = 4.3\ \text{mm}$. Gaps let the rails lengthen in summer; without them they would buckle.
**17.** With $f = -20$ and $u = 30$: $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f} - \dfrac{1}{u} = -\dfrac{1}{20} - \dfrac{1}{30} = -\dfrac{5}{60}$, so $v = -12\ \text{cm}$ (virtual, behind the mirror). $m = -\dfrac{v}{u} = \dfrac{12}{30} = 0.4$ (erect, diminished).
**18.** Each charge is $5\ \text{cm}$ from the midpoint. $E_1 = \dfrac{kq}{r^{2}} = \dfrac{9\times10^{9}\times4\times10^{-6}}{(0.05)^{2}} = 1.44\times10^{7}\ \text{N C}^{-1}$. Both fields point towards the negative charge, so they add: $E = 2.88\times10^{7}\ \text{N C}^{-1}$. Potential: $V = k\left(\dfrac{4\times10^{-6}}{0.05} + \dfrac{-4\times10^{-6}}{0.05}\right) = 0$.
**19.** Activity is the number of nuclei disintegrating per second; the SI unit is the becquerel ($1\ \text{Bq} = 1$ decay per second). $6\ \text{h} = 3$ half-lives, so $A = 800\times\tfrac18 = 100\ \text{Bq}$. Distinction: $\alpha$ particles are helium nuclei, strongly ionizing and stopped by paper; $\gamma$ rays are electromagnetic waves, weakly ionizing and very penetrating.
:::

::: note Solutions — Group C (20–22)
**20. (a)** For a constant net force $F$, $v^{2} = u^{2} + 2as$ and $F = ma$. Multiply by $m/2$: $\tfrac12mv^{2} - \tfrac12mu^{2} = mas = Fs = W$. The net work done equals the change in kinetic energy.
**(b)** Output power $= \dfrac{mgh}{t} = \dfrac{900\times10\times20}{60} = 3000\ \text{W}$. Input $= \dfrac{3000}{0.75} = 4000\ \text{W}$.
**(c)** Power is the rate of doing work, $P = W/t$; the unit is the watt ($\text{J s}^{-1}$).
**21. (a)** (Diagram: circle of radius $r$ with the body at the top and bottom, tension $T$ towards the centre, weight $mg$ downward.) At the top: $T_t + mg = \dfrac{mv_t^{2}}{r}$. The least speed is when $T_t = 0$: $v_t = \sqrt{gr}$. Between bottom and top the body rises $2r$; energy conservation gives $\tfrac12mv_b^{2} = \tfrac12mv_t^{2} + mg(2r)$, so $v_b^{2} = gr + 4gr = 5gr$ and $v_b = \sqrt{5gr}$. At the bottom, $T_b - mg = \dfrac{mv_b^{2}}{r} = 5mg$, so $T_b = 6mg$.
**(b)** $v_t = \sqrt{10\times0.9} = 3.0\ \text{m s}^{-1}$; $v_b = \sqrt{5\times10\times0.9} = \sqrt{45} = 6.7\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. $T_b = 6mg = 6\times0.2\times10 = 12\ \text{N}$.
**22. (a)** Laws: the incident ray, the refracted ray and the normal lie in one plane; $\dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r}$ is constant for a given pair of media (Snell). (Diagram: object $O$ at depth $d$ in the denser medium; a ray $OA$ at a small angle $r$ to the normal refracts at $A$ into air at angle $i$; the eye traces it back to the apparent position $I$ at depth $d'$.) With $x$ the horizontal distance of $A$ from the normal, for small angles $\sin r \approx \tan r = x/d$ and $\sin i \approx \tan i = x/d'$. Hence $n = \dfrac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \dfrac{x/d'}{x/d} = \dfrac{d}{d'}$, i.e. $n = \dfrac{\text{real depth}}{\text{apparent depth}}$.
**(b)** The apparent depth is the sum of the apparent thicknesses of each layer: $\dfrac{9}{1.5} + \dfrac{12}{4/3} = 6 + 9 = 15\ \text{cm}$ (the real depth is $21\ \text{cm}$).
:::

## 4.4 Predicted Questions — ranked

The ranking is by **teaching hours of the unit** and **how standard the question is**. Each prediction states its reason. *Tier A* are the questions a paper on this syllabus almost has to contain in some form; *Tier B* are likely; *Tier C* are possible and worth a pass if time remains. These are judgements, never guarantees.

### 4.4.1 Tier A — revise first

**P1. Equations of motion by the graphical method, and a numerical** (unit 3, $[5\text{ or }8]$; Mechanics). *Why:* kinematics is the foundation of Mechanics (45 h), and the graphical derivation is named in the syllabus. *Outline:* $v = u + at$ from the slope; $s$ as the area under the $v$–$t$ graph; $v^{2} = u^{2} + 2as$ by eliminating $t$. Example: $u = 5$, $a = 2$, $t = 6$ gives $v = 17\ \text{m s}^{-1}$, $s = 66\ \text{m}$.
**P2. Projectile motion: time of flight, maximum height, range** (unit 3, $[5\text{ or }8]$). *Why:* named in the syllabus and the standard application of vectors and kinematics. *Outline:* $T = \dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}$, $H = \dfrac{u^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{2g}$, $R = \dfrac{u^{2}\sin2\theta}{g}$; maximum range at $45^{\circ}$; $R$ equal for $\theta$ and $90^{\circ} - \theta$.
**P3. Conservation of linear momentum, with an elastic or inelastic collision** (unit 4/5, $[5\text{ or }8]$). *Why:* named in both units, and gives a clean numerical. *Outline:* proof from Newton's third law; elastic: $v_1 = \dfrac{m_1 - m_2}{m_1 + m_2}u$, $v_2 = \dfrac{2m_1}{m_1 + m_2}u$; inelastic: $v = \dfrac{m_1u}{m_1 + m_2}$, kinetic energy lost.
**P4. Laws of solid friction and angle of repose** (unit 4, $[5]$). *Why:* the syllabus names the laws and their verification. *Outline:* four laws; $\mu = \tan\theta$ at the angle of friction; incline with and without friction.
**P5. Work–energy theorem and conservation of energy** (unit 5, $[5\text{ or }8]$). *Why:* a six-hour unit with a standard proof. *Outline:* $W = \Delta K$; $K + U$ constant for a freely falling body; check at two heights.
**P6. Satellite: orbital speed, period, geostationary orbit** (unit 7, $[8]$). *Why:* gravitation has the largest unit weight (10 h) and the satellite topics fill half the unit. *Outline:* $v = \sqrt{GM/(R+h)}$; $T = 2\pi\sqrt{(R+h)^{3}/GM}$; energy $K = GMm/2r$, $U = -GMm/r$, $E = -GMm/2r$; geostationary conditions.
**P7. Variation of $g$ with altitude and depth; escape velocity** (unit 7, $[5]$). *Why:* named in the syllabus; clean derivations. *Outline:* $g_h = g\left(\dfrac{R}{R+h}\right)^{2}$; $g_d = g\left(1 - \dfrac{d}{R}\right)$; $v_e = \sqrt{2gR} = 11.2\ \text{km s}^{-1}$.
**P8. Young modulus, stress, strain and elastic energy** (unit 8, $[5]$). *Why:* a numerical type with exact formulae. *Outline:* $Y = \dfrac{FL}{A\Delta l}$; $U = \tfrac12F\Delta l$; energy per volume $\tfrac12\times\text{stress}\times\text{strain}$.
**P9. Combination of capacitors and energy stored** (unit 22, $[5]$). *Why:* a seven-hour unit with direct numericals. *Outline:* series $\dfrac{1}{C} = \sum\dfrac{1}{C_i}$; parallel $C = \sum C_i$; $U = \tfrac12CV^{2}$.
**P10. Kirchhoff's laws with a two-loop or bridge numerical; internal resistance** (unit 23, $[5\text{ or }8]$). *Why:* DC circuits carry 10 hours, the joint-largest unit weight. *Outline:* $\sum I = 0$ and $\sum E = \sum IR$; $I = \dfrac{E}{R + r}$; Wheatstone balance $\dfrac{P}{Q} = \dfrac{R}{S}$.
**P11. Calorimetry and latent heat numerical** (unit 11, $[5]$). *Why:* a recurring numerical type and a six-hour unit. *Outline:* heat lost $=$ heat gained; list each stage: warm, melt, warm, boil.
**P12. Kinetic theory: $P = \tfrac13\rho\overline{c^{2}}$ and rms speed** (unit 13, $[5\text{ or }8]$). *Why:* the standard derivation of an eight-hour unit. *Outline:* momentum change $2mu_x$; time $2l/u_x$; $\overline{u_x^{2}} = \tfrac13\overline{c^{2}}$; $c_{rms} = \sqrt{3RT/M}$.

### 4.4.2 Tier B — likely

**P13. Conical pendulum and motion in a vertical circle** (unit 6, $[5\text{ or }8]$). *Why:* named in the syllabus; clean derivations. *Outline:* $T = 2\pi\sqrt{l\cos\theta/g}$; $v_{top} = \sqrt{gr}$, $v_{bottom} = \sqrt{5gr}$, $T_{bottom} = 6mg$.
**P14. Banking of roads** (unit 6, $[5]$). *Why:* named in the syllabus. *Outline:* $\tan\theta = \dfrac{v^{2}}{rg}$; with friction $v^{2} = rg\,\dfrac{\mu + \tan\theta}{1 - \mu\tan\theta}$.
**P15. Moment, torque and equilibrium of a beam** (unit 4, $[5]$). *Why:* a standard numerical (two supports, a hanging load). *Outline:* take moments about one support; sum of forces zero.
**P16. Triangle, parallelogram law and resolution of vectors; scalar and vector products** (unit 2, $[5]$). *Why:* four hours, all formulae exact. *Outline:* $R = \sqrt{P^{2} + Q^{2} + 2PQ\cos\theta}$; $\tan\alpha = \dfrac{Q\sin\theta}{P + Q\cos\theta}$; $\vec A\cdot\vec B = AB\cos\theta$, $|\vec A\times\vec B| = AB\sin\theta$.
**P17. Thermal expansion: $\gamma = 3\alpha$ and the apparent expansion of a liquid** (unit 10, $[5]$). *Why:* named relations in the syllabus. *Outline:* $\beta = 2\alpha$, $\gamma = 3\alpha$; $\gamma_r = \gamma_a + \gamma_g$.
**P18. Newton's law of cooling and Stefan–Boltzmann law** (units 11/12, $[5]$). *Why:* named in the syllabus; formula-based. *Outline:* rate $\propto$ excess temperature; $P = \sigma AT^{4}$; net loss $\sigma A(T^{4} - T_0^{4})$.
**P19. Refraction: apparent depth, critical angle, total internal reflection** (unit 15, $[5]$). *Why:* four hours; standard numericals. *Outline:* $n = \dfrac{\text{real}}{\text{apparent}}$; $\sin C = 1/n$; optical fibre as application.
**P20. Prism: $n$ in terms of $A$ and $D_m$** (unit 16, $[5]$). *Why:* the prism formula is the syllabus's single derivation of its unit. *Outline:* $D = i_1 + i_2 - A$; $n = \dfrac{\sin\frac{A + D_m}{2}}{\sin\frac{A}{2}}$; thin prism $D = (n - 1)A$.

### 4.4.3 Tier C — possible

**P21. Radioactive decay law and half-life** (unit 24, $[5]$). *Why:* the one derivation of Modern Physics; a standard numerical. *Outline:* $N = N_0e^{-\lambda t}$; $T_{1/2} = 0.693/\lambda$.
**P22. Binding energy and mass defect** (unit 24, $[5]$). *Why:* a numerical type with a fixed constant ($1\ \text{u} = 931.5\ \text{MeV}$). *Outline:* $B = \Delta m\times931.5\ \text{MeV}$; binding energy per nucleon.
**P23. Band theory: conductor, semiconductor, insulator; $n$-type and $p$-type** (unit 25, $[5]$). *Why:* the whole content of a three-hour unit. *Outline:* band diagrams; doping with Group V (donor) or Group III (acceptor) atoms.
**P24. Dispersive power and the pure spectrum** (unit 18, $[5]$). *Why:* a standard definition with a one-line numerical. *Outline:* $\omega = \dfrac{n_v - n_r}{n_y - 1}$; the pure spectrum is formed with two prisms or a lens and a screen.

## Practice questions

**Group A — Multiple choice (1 mark each)**

1. The tension in a conical-pendulum string of mass $m$ at angle $\theta$ to the vertical is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $mg\sin\theta$ (b) $mg\cos\theta$ (c) $mg/\cos\theta$ (d) $mg/\sin\theta$
2. The maximum range of a projectile launched at $20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ ($g = 10$) is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $20\ \text{m}$ (b) $40\ \text{m}$ (c) $80\ \text{m}$ (d) $200\ \text{m}$
3. Two $6\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitors in parallel give <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $3\ \mu\text{F}$ (b) $6\ \mu\text{F}$ (c) $12\ \mu\text{F}$ (d) $36\ \mu\text{F}$
4. The minimum speed at the top of a vertical circle of radius $1.6\ \text{m}$ ($g = 10$) is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $2\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (b) $4\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (c) $8\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (d) $16\ \text{m s}^{-1}$

::: note Answers to Group A
**1.** (c) — $T\cos\theta = mg$.
**2.** (b) — $R_{max} = u^{2}/g = 400/10 = 40\ \text{m}$.
**3.** (c) — parallel capacitances add: $6 + 6 = 12$.
**4.** (b) — $\sqrt{gr} = \sqrt{16} = 4\ \text{m s}^{-1}$.
:::

**Group B — Short answer (5 marks each)**

1. A $60\ \text{kg}$ person stands on a lift accelerating upward at $2\ \text{m s}^{-2}$. Find the apparent weight ($g = 10$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>
2. Find the heat needed to raise $0.5\ \text{kg}$ of ice at $0^{\circ}\text{C}$ to water at $20^{\circ}\text{C}$ ($L_f = 3.36\times10^{5}\ \text{J kg}^{-1}$). <span class="marks">[5]</span>
3. Find the equivalent resistance between the ends when $2\ \Omega$ is in series with the parallel pair $3\ \Omega$ and $6\ \Omega$. <span class="marks">[5]</span>
4. A sample has $5\ \text{g}$ left after $12$ days from $40\ \text{g}$. Find its half-life. <span class="marks">[5]</span>

::: note Answers to Group B
**1.** $R - mg = ma$, so $R = m(g + a) = 60\times12 = 720\ \text{N}$ (apparent weight, compared with $600\ \text{N}$ at rest).
**2.** Melting: $0.5\times3.36\times10^{5} = 1.68\times10^{5}\ \text{J}$. Warming: $0.5\times4200\times20 = 4.2\times10^{4}\ \text{J}$. Total $2.10\times10^{5}\ \text{J}$.
**3.** Parallel: $\dfrac{3\times6}{9} = 2\ \Omega$; total $2 + 2 = 4\ \Omega$.
**4.** $40/5 = 8 = 2^{3}$, so $3T_{1/2} = 12$ days and $T_{1/2} = 4$ days.
:::

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. Derive the expression for the energy stored in a charged capacitor and find the energy released when a $20\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitor charged to $50\ \text{V}$ is discharged. <span class="marks">[8]</span>
2. Derive the relation between the focal length and radius of curvature of a concave mirror and find the image formed by an object $30\ \text{cm}$ from a concave mirror of $f = 10\ \text{cm}$. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Answers to Group C
**1.** Charging from $q$ to $q + dq$ against the p.d. $V = q/C$ needs $dW = \dfrac{q}{C}\,dq$. Total work $W = \int_0^Q\dfrac{q}{C}\,dq = \dfrac{Q^{2}}{2C} = \tfrac12CV^{2}$, stored as electric field energy. $U = \tfrac12(20\times10^{-6})(50)^{2} = 0.025\ \text{J}$, which is released as heat or light on discharge.
**2.** A ray parallel to the axis strikes the mirror at $D$; the radius $CD$ is the normal, so the angle of incidence and reflection are equal, $\theta$. The reflected ray cuts the axis at $F$. In $\triangle CDF$, $\angle FCD = \angle CDF = \theta$ (since $DA \parallel CP$), so $CF = FD$; for a small aperture $FD \approx FP$, hence $CP = CF + FP = 2FP$ and $f = R/2$. Numerical: $\dfrac{1}{v} = \dfrac{1}{f} - \dfrac{1}{u} = \dfrac{1}{10} - \dfrac{1}{30} = \dfrac{2}{30}$, so $v = 15\ \text{cm}$ (real, inverted); $m = -\dfrac{15}{30} = -0.5$ (diminished).
:::
