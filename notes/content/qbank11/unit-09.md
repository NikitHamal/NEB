Sixty-four of the seventy-five marks in the Biology paper are written answers.
Group B asks eight short questions of four marks each, four from Botany and
four from Zoology; Group C asks four long questions of eight marks each, two
from each section. That is where the paper is won, and it is also where most
candidates lose marks they already know the biology for — by writing a
paragraph where the examiner wanted five labelled points, by describing a
process without naming it, or by leaving out the diagram that carries two of
the eight marks on its own.

This chapter gives you, for every unit of the CDC syllabus, at least eight
four-mark questions and three eight-mark questions, each with a complete model
answer written the way you should write it in the hall — headed, numbered,
using the exact words an examiner ticks. Read the answer only after you have
attempted the question in writing and against the clock.

::: key How to use this chapter
- **Time yourself by the marks.** In a three-hour paper you have about 2.4
  minutes per mark: roughly **ten minutes for a four-mark answer and twenty for
  an eight-mark answer**, with ten minutes at the end to check.
- **Attempt first, read after.** Copying a model answer teaches you nothing.
  Write your version, then mark it against the model and underline what you
  left out.
- **Count your points.** A four-mark question usually wants four separate,
  markable statements; an eight-mark question wants a structure — definition,
  body, diagram, significance. If you cannot count four points in what you have
  written, you have not finished.
- **Draw.** Where the model answer has a diagram, the real paper has marks for
  it. A labelled diagram with six correct labels is faster to write than the
  paragraph that would replace it.
- **Use the examiner's note at the end of each unit** as a checklist before you
  move on.
:::

## 9.1 Biomolecules and Cell Biology

**Short-answer questions (4 marks each)**

1. Define enzymes. Write any four properties of enzymes. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** Enzymes are **biological catalysts** — globular proteins produced by
living cells which accelerate a biochemical reaction by lowering its activation
energy, without themselves being consumed or altered at the end of the
reaction.

Four properties:

i. **Protein nature.** All enzymes except ribozymes are proteins, so they are
   denatured by heat, extremes of pH and heavy metals.
ii. **Specificity.** One enzyme acts on one substrate or one type of bond; urease
    hydrolyses only urea. This follows from the shape of the active site.
iii. **Catalytic efficiency.** A very small quantity converts a very large
     quantity of substrate; one molecule of catalase decomposes about 5 million
     molecules of H₂O₂ per minute.
iv. **Sensitivity to temperature and pH.** Each enzyme has an optimum, usually
    around 37 °C and a pH characteristic of its site — pepsin at pH 2, trypsin
    at pH 8.

2. Differentiate between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells under any four heads. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.**

| Prokaryotic cell | Eukaryotic cell |
|---|---|
| Nucleus is not membrane-bound; the DNA lies naked in a nucleoid | A true nucleus with a nuclear envelope and nucleolus is present |
| Membrane-bound organelles (mitochondria, plastids, ER, golgi, lysosome) are absent | All these membrane-bound organelles are present |
| DNA is a single circular molecule without histones | DNA is linear, in several chromosomes, wound on histone proteins |
| Ribosomes are 70S | Ribosomes are 80S in the cytoplasm (70S inside mitochondria and plastids) |
| Cell division is amitosis or binary fission; no spindle is formed | Cell division is mitosis or meiosis with a spindle |
| Size usually 1–10 µm; e.g. bacteria, cyanobacteria | Size usually 10–100 µm; e.g. *Amoeba*, plant and animal cells |

3. Why is the mitochondrion called the powerhouse of the cell? <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** The mitochondrion is called the powerhouse because it is the site
where the chemical energy of food is converted into the usable energy currency
of the cell, **ATP**.

i. The **Krebs cycle** (citric acid cycle) takes place in the mitochondrial
   matrix, oxidising pyruvate completely to CO₂ and releasing NADH and FADH₂.
ii. The **electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation** take place on
    the inner membrane, where the energy of those electrons is used to pump
    protons and drive ATP synthase.
iii. Of the total ATP produced from one glucose molecule, about **thirty-four of
     the thirty-eight** are produced inside the mitochondrion; glycolysis in the
     cytoplasm gives only a net two.
iv. Its structure is adapted for this: the inner membrane is thrown into
    **cristae** to increase the surface area, and it carries the F₁ particles
    (oxysomes) on which ATP synthesis occurs.

Because every energy-requiring activity of the cell — active transport, muscle
contraction, synthesis — is ultimately paid for by this ATP, the organelle is
the power house of the cell.

4. What is the fluid mosaic model of the plasma membrane? Draw a labelled diagram. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** The fluid mosaic model, proposed by **Singer and Nicolson in 1972**,
describes the plasma membrane as a **fluid bilayer of phospholipids in which
protein molecules float like icebergs in a sea**, giving a mosaic appearance.

i. The phospholipid bilayer has **hydrophilic heads outward** and hydrophobic
   fatty-acid tails facing each other inward, so the membrane is a self-sealing
   barrier to water-soluble substances.
ii. The bilayer is **fluid** — the lipid molecules move laterally, and
    cholesterol between them regulates that fluidity.
iii. Proteins are of two kinds: **extrinsic (peripheral)** proteins on the
     surfaces and **intrinsic (integral)** proteins that span the bilayer and act
     as channels, carriers, pumps, enzymes and receptors.
iv. Short carbohydrate chains attached to lipids and proteins form
    **glycolipids and glycoproteins** on the outer face, which act as
    recognition and antigen sites.

The model explains selective permeability, membrane growth, cell fusion and the
movement of substances, which the earlier rigid sandwich model of Danielli and
Davson could not.

5. Distinguish between mitosis and meiosis under any four heads. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.**

| Mitosis | Meiosis |
|---|---|
| One division; one cycle of DNA replication followed by one nuclear division | Two successive divisions, meiosis I and II, after one replication |
| Two daughter cells are produced | Four daughter cells are produced |
| Chromosome number remains the same, 2n → 2n; it is an equational division | Chromosome number is halved, 2n → n; it is a reductional division |
| Daughter cells are genetically identical to the parent | Daughter cells are genetically different; crossing over and independent assortment occur |
| No synapsis, no chiasmata, no crossing over | Homologous chromosomes pair in zygotene and cross over in pachytene |
| Occurs in somatic cells — root and shoot apices, cambium | Occurs in reproductive cells — spore mother cells, gamete mother cells |
| Function: growth, repair, vegetative reproduction | Function: gamete formation, maintenance of chromosome number, variation |

6. Write the significance of meiosis. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.**

i. **It maintains a constant chromosome number in a species.** Fertilisation
   doubles the chromosome number; meiosis halves it in the formation of gametes
   or spores, so the number is restored generation after generation.
ii. **It produces variation.** Crossing over in pachytene recombines linked
    genes, and the independent assortment of maternal and paternal chromosomes
    in metaphase I produces 2ⁿ different gametes; both provide the raw material
    on which natural selection acts.
iii. **It is the basis of sexual reproduction**, since haploid gametes or spores
     can be formed in no other way.
iv. **It removes harmful combinations and produces new ones**, which is why
    meiosis is essential for evolution and for plant and animal breeding.

7. Describe the structure and functions of the nucleus. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** The nucleus, discovered by **Robert Brown in 1831**, is the largest
organelle and the control centre of the eukaryotic cell.

**Structure.** It has four parts.

i. **Nuclear envelope** — a double membrane with a perinuclear space between,
   perforated by **nuclear pores** which allow RNA and ribosomal subunits out and
   proteins in. The outer membrane is continuous with the rough ER.
ii. **Nucleoplasm** — the ground substance, containing nucleotides, enzymes and
    ions.
iii. **Chromatin** — a network of DNA wound on histones; it condenses into
     **chromosomes** during division.
iv. **Nucleolus** — a naked, spherical, dense body, the site of **rRNA synthesis
    and ribosome assembly**.

**Functions.** It stores the genetic information; it controls all metabolic
activities of the cell through the synthesis of mRNA; it directs cell division;
and through the nucleolus it manufactures ribosomes.

8. What are lysosomes? Why are they called suicidal bags? <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** Lysosomes are **single membrane-bound vesicles**, about 0.1–1.2 µm
across, discovered by **Christian de Duve (1955)**, containing about forty
**hydrolytic enzymes** — proteases, lipases, nucleases and acid phosphatase —
which work best at an acid pH of about 5.

Functions: intracellular digestion of food taken in by phagocytosis;
**autophagy**, the digestion of the cell's own worn-out organelles; and the
destruction of bacteria engulfed by white blood cells.

They are called **suicidal bags** because if the lysosomal membrane ruptures —
as it does in an injured, starved or dying cell — the enzymes are released into
the cytoplasm and digest the cell's own contents, killing the cell. This
self-destruction, called **autolysis**, is necessary in normal development: it
is how the tail of a tadpole is removed at metamorphosis.

9. Write short notes on: (a) endoplasmic reticulum (b) golgi apparatus. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.**

**(a) Endoplasmic reticulum.** A network of membrane-bound tubules, vesicles and
flattened cisternae running through the cytoplasm and continuous with the
nuclear envelope; described by Porter, Claude and Fullam. **Rough ER** bears
80S ribosomes on its outer face and synthesises and transports proteins for
export; **smooth ER** has no ribosomes and synthesises lipids and steroids,
detoxifies drugs in the liver, and stores calcium in muscle. The ER also gives
mechanical support to the cell — it is its endoskeleton.

**(b) Golgi apparatus.** Discovered by **Camillo Golgi (1898)**; a stack of four
to eight flattened cisternae with associated tubules and vesicles, called
**dictyosomes** in plant cells. Its *cis* face receives vesicles from the ER and
its *trans* face buds them off. It **processes, packages and secretes**: it
glycosylates proteins, forms lysosomes and secretory vesicles, synthesises
cell-wall polysaccharides in plants, and forms the acrosome of the sperm. It is
therefore the "export house" or traffic police of the cell.

10. Define carbohydrates. Classify them with one example of each. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** Carbohydrates are **polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or compounds
that yield them on hydrolysis**, with the general formula Cₓ(H₂O)ᵧ. They are the
chief source of energy and the commonest organic molecules on earth.

i. **Monosaccharides** — the simplest sugars, which cannot be hydrolysed
   further; sweet, soluble and reducing. Trioses (glyceraldehyde), pentoses
   (ribose, deoxyribose) and hexoses (**glucose**, fructose, galactose).
ii. **Oligosaccharides** — two to ten monosaccharide units joined by glycosidic
    bonds. Disaccharides: **sucrose** (glucose + fructose), maltose, lactose.
iii. **Polysaccharides** — many units, non-sweet and insoluble. Storage:
     **starch** in plants and **glycogen** in animals. Structural: **cellulose**
     in plant cell walls and **chitin** in fungal walls and arthropod cuticle.

**Long-answer questions (8 marks each)**

1. Describe the different stages of mitosis with suitable labelled diagrams. Add a note on its significance. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Answer.** **Mitosis** is the equational division of a somatic cell in which
one parent cell divides into two daughter cells, each with the same chromosome
number and the same genetic constitution as the parent. It was described by
**Walther Flemming (1882)**. It occupies the M phase of the cell cycle and has
two parts: **karyokinesis**, the division of the nucleus, and **cytokinesis**,
the division of the cytoplasm.

**Karyokinesis** passes through four stages.

**(i) Prophase.** The chromatin threads shorten and thicken into distinct
chromosomes, each already made of **two sister chromatids joined at the
centromere**, because DNA was replicated in the preceding S phase. The nucleolus
and the nuclear envelope begin to disappear. In animal cells the centrioles
move to opposite poles and the spindle begins to form; plant cells form an
anastral spindle without centrioles. Prophase is the longest stage of mitosis.

**(ii) Metaphase.** The nuclear envelope and nucleolus have completely
disappeared. The chromosomes, now at their most condensed and therefore most
easily counted and photographed, move to the **equator of the spindle** and
arrange themselves in a single plane, the **metaphase plate**. Each centromere
is attached by a kinetochore to spindle fibres from **both** poles. This is the
stage used for karyotyping.

**(iii) Anaphase.** The centromere of each chromosome **divides
longitudinally**, and the two sister chromatids — now daughter chromosomes — are
pulled to opposite poles by the shortening of the chromosomal fibres, appearing
V-, L-, J- or I-shaped according to the position of the centromere. Anaphase is
the shortest stage. At its end each pole has a complete diploid set.

**(iv) Telophase.** The chromosomes at each pole uncoil and lengthen back into
chromatin; the nuclear envelope and the nucleolus re-form; the spindle
disappears. Two daughter nuclei now lie in one cytoplasm.

**Cytokinesis** follows. In animal cells a **cleavage furrow** constricts the
cell from the outside inwards; in plant cells, because of the rigid wall, a
**cell plate** is laid down from the centre outwards by golgi vesicles and
becomes the middle lamella.

**Significance.**

i. It brings about the **growth** of a multicellular organism from the zygote,
   every cell receiving an identical set of genes.
ii. It **repairs and replaces** worn-out cells — skin, blood, the lining of the
    gut.
iii. It is the basis of **vegetative and asexual reproduction** and of the
     regeneration of lost parts.
iv. It maintains a constant **nucleo-cytoplasmic ratio** and keeps the
    chromosome number of the species constant in the body cells.
v. Its failure of control produces uncontrolled division — **cancer**.

2. Give an account of the ultrastructure and functions of the chloroplast. How is it adapted to its work? <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Answer.** The chloroplast is the green, double-membrane-bound plastid in which
**photosynthesis** occurs. It is the organelle through which almost all the
energy in the living world enters it. In higher plants it is discoid, 4–10 µm
long and 2–4 µm thick, and a palisade cell may contain twenty to forty of them.

**Ultrastructure.**

i. **Envelope.** Two smooth membranes separated by a narrow intermembrane
   space. The outer is freely permeable, the inner selectively permeable and
   without chlorophyll.
ii. **Stroma.** The colourless proteinaceous ground substance enclosed by the
    envelope, containing the enzymes of the **Calvin cycle** (notably RuBisCO),
    **circular naked DNA**, **70S ribosomes**, starch grains and oil droplets.
    Because it has its own DNA and ribosomes the chloroplast is a
    **semi-autonomous organelle**, which supports the endosymbiotic theory.
iii. **Thylakoids.** A system of flattened membranous sacs suspended in the
     stroma. Where they are stacked like a pile of coins they form a **granum**
     (40–60 per chloroplast, each of 5–30 thylakoids); the grana are joined by
     unstacked **stroma lamellae** or intergranal thylakoids.
iv. **Quantasomes.** The thylakoid membrane carries the photosynthetic units —
    chlorophyll a and b, carotenoids, the two photosystems PS I and PS II, the
    electron carriers and ATP synthase.

**Functions.**

i. The **light reaction** occurs on the thylakoid membranes: absorption of light
   by the photosystems, **photolysis of water** releasing O₂, non-cyclic and
   cyclic photophosphorylation producing **ATP** and **NADPH₂**.
ii. The **dark reaction (Calvin cycle)** occurs in the stroma, using that ATP and
    NADPH₂ to fix CO₂ into carbohydrate.
iii. It stores the product temporarily as **starch grains**, and it synthesises
     some of its own proteins and lipids.

**Adaptations to its work.**

i. The **stacking of thylakoids into grana** packs an enormous pigment-bearing
   surface into a small volume, so that the maximum light is absorbed.
ii. The **separation of the two reactions** — light reaction on a membrane, dark
    reaction in the fluid stroma — allows a proton gradient to be built across
    the thylakoid membrane and used for ATP synthesis.
iii. The **biconvex discoid shape** presents a large surface to the light and
     shortens the diffusion path for CO₂.
iv. **Chloroplasts can move within the cell** (cyclosis), turning their flat face
    to weak light and their edge to strong light.
v. Its **own DNA and ribosomes** allow it to make key subunits of RuBisCO on the
   spot.

3. What is the cell cycle? Describe its phases and explain how it is controlled. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Answer.** The **cell cycle** is the ordered sequence of events by which a cell
duplicates its contents and divides into two. In a typical human cell in culture
it takes about **24 hours**, of which mitosis occupies barely an hour. It has
two main phases: a long **interphase** and a short **M phase**.

**Interphase (about 95 % of the cycle).** Once wrongly called the resting stage,
it is the most active period of the cell's life. It has three sub-phases.

i. **G₁ phase (first gap).** The longest and most variable. The cell grows,
   synthesises RNA, proteins and organelles, and prepares the enzymes of DNA
   replication. Its chromosome content is 2n with one chromatid each.
ii. **S phase (synthesis).** **DNA replicates**, so each chromosome now consists
    of two identical sister chromatids joined at the centromere. Histones are
    synthesised. **The DNA amount doubles but the chromosome number does not** —
    this is the single point most often missed.
iii. **G₂ phase (second gap).** The cell continues to grow, synthesises the
     tubulin for the spindle and the proteins needed for division, and checks the
     replicated DNA for errors.

**M phase.** **Karyokinesis** (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) followed
by **cytokinesis**, giving two daughter cells that enter G₁ again.

**G₀ phase.** Cells that will not divide again — neurons, mature red blood
cells, cardiac muscle — leave the cycle at G₁ and enter a quiescent **G₀**
state. Liver cells stay in G₀ but can be recalled into the cycle after injury.

**Control of the cycle.** The cycle is driven by **cyclins** and
**cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs)**: a cyclin accumulates, binds its CDK,
activates it by phosphorylating target proteins, and is then destroyed, so the
cycle advances in one direction only. Progress is policed at three
**checkpoints**:

i. **G₁ (restriction) checkpoint** — is the cell big enough, are nutrients and
   growth signals present, is the DNA undamaged? This is the main decision
   point.
ii. **G₂ checkpoint** — has all the DNA been replicated correctly?
iii. **M (spindle) checkpoint** — is every chromosome attached to the spindle
     from both poles before anaphase begins?

If a checkpoint detects a fault the cycle is arrested while repair takes place,
and if the damage cannot be repaired the cell is destroyed by programmed cell
death (**apoptosis**). Tumour-suppressor genes such as **p53** operate here; p53
is called the guardian of the genome. When these controls fail the cell divides
without restraint, and that uncontrolled division is **cancer**.

## 9.2 Floral Diversity

**Short-answer questions (4 marks each)**

1. What is binomial nomenclature? Write any four rules of it. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** Binomial nomenclature is the system, introduced by **Carolus
Linnaeus** in *Species Plantarum* (1753), of giving every organism a scientific
name of **two words — the generic name followed by the specific epithet**, for
example *Mangifera indica*.

Four rules (from the ICN and ICZN):

i. The name is **Latin or latinised**, and is printed in *italics*; when
   handwritten each word is **separately underlined**.
ii. The **generic name begins with a capital letter and the specific epithet
    with a small letter**, even if it is derived from a person's name — *Rana
    tigrina*.
iii. The **name of the author** who first published the name is written after
     the specific epithet, in roman and not underlined — *Mangifera indica* Linn.
iv. The name must be based on a **type specimen** preserved in a recognised
    herbarium or museum, and the **earliest validly published name has priority**.

2. Write any four economic importances of fungi. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.**

i. **Food and food processing.** *Agaricus* (mushroom) and *Morchella* (guchhi
   chyau, a valuable export of Nepal) are eaten; *Saccharomyces* (yeast) leavens
   bread and ferments sugar to ethanol in brewing.
ii. **Medicine.** *Penicillium notatum* yields **penicillin**, the first
    antibiotic, discovered by Alexander Fleming (1928); *Claviceps* yields
    ergotamine and other fungi yield griseofulvin and statins.
iii. **Decomposition and soil fertility.** As saprophytes, fungi mineralise dead
     organic matter and return carbon and nitrogen to the soil; **mycorrhizal**
     fungi greatly increase the absorbing surface of tree roots.
iv. **Harmful effects.** They cause plant diseases of great economic loss —
    late blight of potato (*Phytophthora*), rusts and smuts of wheat — spoil
    stored grain, and cause human diseases such as ringworm and athlete's foot.

3. What is a lichen? Describe the nature of the association between its partners. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** A lichen is a **composite, self-supporting organism formed by the
symbiotic association of a fungus (the mycobiont, usually an ascomycete) and an
alga or cyanobacterium (the phycobiont)**. The fungus makes up most of the
thallus; the alga lies as a layer within it.

**Nature of the association.**

i. The **alga photosynthesises** and supplies carbohydrate to the fungus; if the
   partner is a cyanobacterium such as *Nostoc*, it also fixes atmospheric
   nitrogen.
ii. The **fungus absorbs and retains water and mineral salts**, anchors the
    thallus and shields the alga from strong light and desiccation.
iii. The relationship is often described as **mutualism**, but because the fungus
     sends haustoria into the algal cells and the alga can live independently
     while the fungus usually cannot, many workers call it **controlled
     parasitism or helotism**.
iv. Because they absorb minerals directly from rainwater they are extremely
    sensitive to SO₂ and are used as **bio-indicators of air pollution**; they are
    also the **pioneer colonisers of bare rock** in a xerosere.

4. Why are bryophytes called the amphibians of the plant kingdom? <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** Bryophytes are called the amphibians of the plant kingdom because,
although they have moved onto the land, they cannot complete their life cycle
without water.

i. They **grow on land**, usually in moist shady places, and have a plant body
   differentiated into a thallus or into stem-like and leaf-like structures with
   rhizoids.
ii. But the **antherozoids are flagellate and must swim** through a film of
    external water to reach the archegonium; without free water there is no
    fertilisation.
iii. They have **no true vascular tissue** — no xylem and phloem — so water moves
     slowly by diffusion and capillarity and the plant must stay small and close
     to a damp surface.
iv. They have **no true roots**, and the thin body lacks a thick cuticle, so they
    lose water readily and are poikilohydric.

They are therefore land plants that remain dependent on water for reproduction,
exactly as an amphibian is a land animal that must return to water to breed.

5. Differentiate between gymnosperms and angiosperms under any four heads. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.**

| Gymnosperms | Angiosperms |
|---|---|
| Ovules are naked, borne on open megasporophylls; there is no ovary | Ovules are enclosed in an ovary formed by folded carpels |
| No true flower; reproductive structures are borne in cones or strobili | A true flower with calyx, corolla, androecium and gynoecium |
| No fruit is formed; the seed is naked | The ovary ripens into a fruit enclosing the seed |
| No double fertilisation; the endosperm is haploid and formed before fertilisation | Double fertilisation occurs; the endosperm is triploid and formed after fertilisation |
| Xylem lacks vessels (except Gnetales); phloem lacks companion cells | Xylem has vessels; phloem has companion cells |
| Mostly wind-pollinated, perennial woody trees; about 1,000 species | Pollination by wind, water, insects and animals; herbs to trees; about 300,000 species |

6. Write the floral characters and any two economic importances of the family Brassicaceae. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** **Floral characters (Brassicaceae or Cruciferae, the mustard
family).**

- **Inflorescence** — racemose, typically a corymbose raceme.
- **Flower** — bracteate or ebracteate, pedicellate, complete, bisexual,
  **actinomorphic**, hypogynous, tetramerous.
- **Calyx** — 4 sepals, polysepalous, in two whorls of two (2 + 2).
- **Corolla** — 4 petals, polypetalous, arranged in the form of a cross,
  **cruciform**; valvate.
- **Androecium** — 6 stamens in two whorls, the outer of two short and the inner
  of four long — **tetradynamous**; polyandrous, epipetalous absent.
- **Gynoecium** — bicarpellary, syncarpous, superior ovary, at first unilocular
  but becoming bilocular by a false septum (**replum**); parietal placentation.
- **Fruit** — siliqua or silicula.
- **Floral formula** — ⊕ ⚥ K 2+2 C 4 A 2+4 G (2).

**Economic importance.** (i) Oil and food: *Brassica campestris* (mustard) and
*B. napus* yield edible oil and the seeds are a condiment; *B. oleracea* gives
cabbage, cauliflower, knol-khol and broccoli, and *Raphanus sativus* the radish.
(ii) Ornamentals and medicine: *Iberis* (candytuft) and *Matthiola* are grown in
gardens, and *Brassica* seed poultice is used as a counter-irritant.

7. Write the floral formula of Fabaceae and explain, whorl by whorl, how you would recognise a member of the family in the field. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** **Floral formula (Fabaceae, subfamily Papilionoideae):**
% ⚥ K (5) C 1+2+(2) A (9)+1 G 1.

Recognition in the field, whorl by whorl:

i. **Habit and leaf.** A herb, shrub or twiner with **pinnately compound leaves
   and a pulvinus** at the leaf base, and often root nodules containing
   *Rhizobium*.
ii. **Calyx** — 5 sepals united into a tube, slightly two-lipped.
iii. **Corolla** — the diagnostic whorl: a **papilionaceous (butterfly-shaped)
     corolla** of five unequal petals — one large posterior **standard
     (vexillum)**, two lateral **wings (alae)** and two anterior petals fused into
     a boat-shaped **keel (carina)**, with **descending imbricate** aestivation
     in which the standard overlaps the wings. This makes the flower
     **zygomorphic**, marked %.
iv. **Androecium** — 10 stamens, nine united by their filaments into a sheath and
    the tenth free — **diadelphous**, hidden in the keel.
v. **Gynoecium** — monocarpellary, superior, unilocular with **marginal
   placentation**, ripening into the unmistakable **legume or pod** that splits
   along both sutures.

8. Give an account of the taxonomic hierarchy with a suitable example. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.** The **taxonomic hierarchy** is the arrangement of taxonomic
categories in a descending series, each category containing one or more of the
category below it. It was given by **Linnaeus** and the obligatory categories
are seven.

Kingdom → Division (Phylum in animals) → Class → Order → Family → Genus →
Species.

As we go down the hierarchy the **number of organisms in a taxon decreases while
the number of characters they share increases**; the species is the basic unit
and consists of individuals that resemble one another and interbreed freely.

Example — mustard:

| Category | Taxon |
|---|---|
| Kingdom | Plantae |
| Division | Magnoliophyta (Angiospermae) |
| Class | Magnoliopsida (Dicotyledonae) |
| Order | Brassicales |
| Family | Brassicaceae |
| Genus | *Brassica* |
| Species | *campestris* |

9. Distinguish between artificial, natural and phylogenetic systems of classification. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.**

i. **Artificial system.** Based on **one or a few easily observed characters**
   chosen arbitrarily, such as habit or the number of stamens. Linnaeus's sexual
   system, which classified plants by the number and union of stamens and
   carpels, is the best example. It is convenient for identification but places
   unrelated plants together and separates related ones.
ii. **Natural system.** Based on **as many characters as possible — external
    morphology, anatomy, embryology and phytochemistry — taken together**, so
    that the groups formed express real overall resemblance. The system of
    **Bentham and Hooker** (*Genera Plantarum*, 1862–83) is the classic example
    and is still used in herbaria. It does not, however, show the evolutionary
    sequence.
iii. **Phylogenetic system.** Based on the **evolutionary relationship and common
     ancestry** of the groups, so that the classification is a picture of the
     course of evolution. **Engler and Prantl** and later **Hutchinson** and the
     modern APG system, which uses DNA sequence data, are examples.

10. Write any four differences between monocotyledonous and dicotyledonous plants. <span class="marks">[4]</span>

**Answer.**

| Dicotyledon | Monocotyledon |
|---|---|
| Seed has two cotyledons | Seed has one cotyledon |
| Tap root system | Fibrous (adventitious) root system |
| Leaves with reticulate venation | Leaves with parallel venation |
| Flower parts in fours or fives (tetramerous or pentamerous) | Flower parts in threes (trimerous) |
| Vascular bundles in the stem are arranged in a ring, conjoint, collateral and **open** with cambium; secondary growth occurs | Vascular bundles scattered, conjoint, collateral and **closed**; no normal secondary growth |
| Example: *Brassica*, *Pisum*, *Solanum* | Example: *Allium*, rice, wheat, *Lilium* |

**Long-answer questions (8 marks each)**

1. Describe the family Solanaceae under the heads of habit, vegetative characters, floral characters and economic importance. Give its floral formula. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Answer.** Solanaceae, the **potato or nightshade family**, contains about
2,500 species in 90 genera, distributed over the tropics and temperate regions;
it is well represented in Nepal.

**Habit.** Mostly annual or perennial **herbs** (*Solanum nigrum*, *Petunia*),
some shrubs (*Cestrum*, *Withania*) and a few small trees (*Solanum
grandiflorum*); a few are climbers. Hairs are common and the plants frequently
smell strongly because of alkaloids.

**Vegetative characters.**

- **Root** — branched tap root; in potato the underground stem forms tubers.
- **Stem** — herbaceous or woody, erect, cylindrical, branched, often hairy;
  underground in *Solanum tuberosum*; modified into thorns in some species.
- **Leaf** — alternate, **exstipulate**, simple (pinnately compound in tomato),
  entire or lobed, with **reticulate venation**.

**Floral characters.**

- **Inflorescence** — usually a **cymose** inflorescence, axillary or extra-axillary;
  solitary axillary in *Datura*.
- **Flower** — bracteate, pedicellate, complete, **bisexual**,
  **actinomorphic**, **hypogynous**, pentamerous.
- **Calyx** — 5 sepals, **gamosepalous**, persistent in tomato and *Withania*,
  valvate.
- **Corolla** — 5 petals, **gamopetalous**, rotate, campanulate or funnel-shaped,
  valvate.
- **Androecium** — 5 stamens, **epipetalous**, alternating with the petals,
  polyandrous; anthers usually basifixed and dithecous, dehiscing longitudinally
  or by apical pores (*Solanum*).
- **Gynoecium** — **bicarpellary, syncarpous**; ovary superior, **bilocular**,
  with the placentae swollen and bearing many ovules on **axile placentation**.
  The ovary is placed **obliquely** in the flower, a character found in no other
  family. A false septum makes the ovary four-chambered in *Datura*.
- **Fruit** — a **berry** (tomato, brinjal, *Solanum nigrum*) or a **capsule**
  (*Datura*, *Petunia*).
- **Seed** — endospermic, with a curved embryo.

**Floral formula.** ⊕ ⚥ K (5) C (5) A 5 G (2) — with the note that the ovary
is obliquely placed.

**Economic importance.**

i. **Food.** *Solanum tuberosum* (potato) is the world's fourth staple crop;
   *S. lycopersicum* (tomato), *S. melongena* (brinjal) and *Capsicum annuum*
   (chilli, a major cash crop in Nepal) are vegetables.
ii. **Medicine.** *Atropa belladonna* yields **atropine**, used to dilate the
    pupil; *Datura* yields hyoscyamine and stramonium, used in asthma; *Withania
    somnifera* (ashwagandha) is an important tonic in Ayurveda.
iii. **Narcotic.** *Nicotiana tabacum* yields **nicotine** for tobacco, and also
     an insecticide.
iv. **Ornamental.** *Petunia*, *Cestrum nocturnum* (raat ki rani) and *Solanum
    pseudocapsicum* are grown in gardens.
v. **Poisonous.** *Solanum nigrum* berries when unripe, and all parts of
   *Datura*, are poisonous because of their alkaloids.

2. What is alternation of generations? Describe the life cycle of a moss (*Funaria*) to illustrate it. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Answer.** **Alternation of generations** is the regular alternation, in the
life cycle of a plant, of two morphologically distinct phases: a **haploid
gamete-producing gametophyte** and a **diploid spore-producing sporophyte**.
The gametophyte produces gametes by mitosis; their fusion at fertilisation
doubles the chromosome number and gives the sporophyte; the sporophyte produces
spores by meiosis, which halves it again and restores the gametophyte. The
cycle is therefore driven by two events, **fertilisation and meiosis**, and the
plant is said to be **diplohaplontic**.

**The life cycle of *Funaria*.** In bryophytes the **gametophyte is the dominant,
independent, green phase** and the sporophyte is small and permanently attached
to it — the opposite of the situation in a fern or a flowering plant.

**(i) The gametophyte.** The leafy *Funaria* plant is haploid. It has a slender
erect axis bearing spirally arranged sessile leaves and, at the base,
multicellular branched **rhizoids** with oblique septa. It is autotrophic.

**(ii) Sex organs.** *Funaria* is **monoecious but autoicous** — the antheridia
and archegonia are borne on separate branches of the same plant. The main shoot
ends in a rosette of leaves (the perigonium) enclosing club-shaped
**antheridia**, each producing many biflagellate **antherozoids**, mixed with
sterile **paraphyses**. A lateral branch bears the flask-shaped
**archegonium**, with a swollen venter containing a single egg and a long neck,
mixed with **paraphyses**.

**(iii) Fertilisation.** In the presence of **water**, the antheridium bursts and
the antherozoids swim to the archegonium, attracted chemotactically by the
**sucrose and organic acids** released by the disintegrating neck canal cells.
One antherozoid fuses with the egg to form the **diploid zygote (oospore)**,
which is retained in the venter.

**(iv) The sporophyte.** The zygote divides mitotically to form the sporophyte or
**sporogonium**, which remains attached to and partly dependent on the
gametophyte. It has three parts: a **foot** embedded in the gametophyte for
anchorage and absorption; a long **seta**; and a **capsule** at the top, covered
at first by the **calyptra** derived from the old archegonial wall. The capsule
is complex, with an apophysis, a theca containing the **spore sac** and a
central sterile **columella**, and a **peristome of two rings of sixteen teeth**
beneath the operculum.

**(v) Spore formation and dispersal.** The **spore mother cells in the spore sac
divide by meiosis**, so the spores are **haploid** — this is the point at which
the diploid phase ends. The capsule dries, the operculum falls, and the
**hygroscopic peristome teeth** bend outward in dry weather to scatter the
spores.

**(vi) Germination.** A spore germinates into a green, branched, filamentous
**protonema**, on which buds arise, and each bud grows into a new leafy
gametophyte, completing the cycle.

**Significance.** The sporophyte enables the plant to produce and disperse large
numbers of resistant spores, an adaptation to land; the gametophyte, being
haploid, exposes every gene to selection; and the meiosis in the capsule
provides variation. The retention of the embryo on the gametophyte is the
feature that unites all land plants as **embryophytes**.

3. Describe the general characters of algae and give an account of their economic importance. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Answer.** Algae are **simple, autotrophic, chlorophyll-bearing, thalloid
plants** whose sex organs are unicellular (or, if multicellular, have no sterile
jacket) and which produce no embryo. Their study is **phycology**, and F. E.
Fritsch's classification of 1935 is the standard.

**General characters.**

i. **Habitat.** Predominantly **aquatic**, both freshwater and marine; also
   terrestrial on damp soil and bark, cryophytic on snow (*Chlamydomonas
   nivalis*), thermophilic in hot springs, epiphytic, endophytic, and symbiotic
   in lichens and in the coralloid roots of *Cycas*.
ii. **Plant body.** A **thallus** with no true root, stem or leaf and no vascular
    tissue. It ranges from unicellular motile (*Chlamydomonas*) and non-motile
    (*Chlorella*) forms through colonies (*Volvox*), filaments (*Spirogyra*,
    *Ulothrix*) and sheets (*Ulva*) to the giant parenchymatous kelps
    (*Macrocystis*, up to 60 m).
iii. **Cell structure.** Eukaryotic, with a cell wall usually of **cellulose**
     with pectin, and sometimes algin or agar; the chloroplast has a characteristic
     shape — spiral in *Spirogyra*, cup-shaped in *Chlamydomonas*, girdle-shaped
     in *Ulothrix* — and contains **pyrenoids** that store starch.
iv. **Pigments.** Always **chlorophyll a**, with other chlorophylls and
    accessory pigments that give the classes their names and their colours:
    Chlorophyceae green (chl a, b), Phaeophyceae brown (fucoxanthin),
    Rhodophyceae red (**r-phycoerythrin**).
v. **Reserve food.** Starch in green algae, **laminarin and mannitol** in brown
   algae, **floridean starch** in red algae.
vi. **Reproduction.** **Vegetative** by fragmentation, fission or hormogonia;
    **asexual** by zoospores, aplanospores, akinetes or hypnospores; **sexual** by
    isogamy, anisogamy or oogamy, and by conjugation in *Spirogyra*. Red algae
    have non-motile gametes and show complex post-fertilisation development.

**Economic importance.**

i. **Primary production and oxygen.** Marine phytoplankton fix a very large part
   of global CO₂ and release more than half the world's oxygen; they are the
   base of every aquatic food chain.
ii. **Food.** *Porphyra* (nori), *Laminaria* (kombu), *Ulva* (sea lettuce) and
    *Sargassum* are eaten in East Asia; **Chlorella** and **Spirulina** are
    protein-rich single-cell foods used by astronauts and in famine relief.
iii. **Industrial products.** **Agar-agar**, used to culture microorganisms and
     in food, comes from *Gelidium* and *Gracilaria*; **algin** from *Laminaria*
     and *Macrocystis* is used in ice cream, paint and textiles;
     **carrageenan** from *Chondrus* is an emulsifier; **diatomite** from diatom
     frustules is used in filters, polishes and insulation.
iv. **Fertiliser and fodder.** Seaweeds are used as manure for their potash, and
    **blue-green algae such as *Nostoc* and *Anabaena* fix nitrogen** in paddy
    fields; *Azolla* with *Anabaena* is a standard green manure.
v. **Medicine.** Antibiotic **chlorellin** from *Chlorella*; iodine from
   *Laminaria*; vermifuges from *Digenea*.
vi. **Harmful effects.** **Algal blooms** in eutrophic water deoxygenate it and
    kill fish; *Microcystis* and the dinoflagellates of the **red tide** release
    toxins that poison fish and shellfish; some algae block water pipes and
    spoil the taste of drinking water.

4. Describe the general characters of pteridophytes and explain why they are considered the first true vascular land plants. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

**Answer.** Pteridophytes — the ferns and their allies — are **seedless
vascular cryptogams**. About 12,000 species survive; in the Carboniferous they
formed the great tree forests whose remains are our coal.

**General characters.**

i. **Habitat.** Chiefly moist, shady terrestrial habitats; some xerophytic
   (*Selaginella rupestris*), some aquatic (*Marsilea*, *Salvinia*, *Azolla*),
   some epiphytic.
ii. **Plant body.** The dominant plant is the **sporophyte**, differentiated for
    the first time in the plant kingdom into **true root, stem and leaf**. The
    stem is usually an underground **rhizome**; the leaves are **microphyllous**
    (*Selaginella*, *Equisetum*) or **megaphyllous** and pinnately compound
    (*Dryopteris*), and the young leaf shows **circinate vernation**.
iii. **Vascular tissue.** **Xylem and phloem** are present, arranged in a stele;
     the xylem consists of **tracheids only, without vessels** (except
     *Selaginella* and *Pteridium*) and the phloem of **sieve cells without
     companion cells**. Protostele, siphonostele, dictyostele and polystele all
     occur.
iv. **Reproduction.** Spores are produced in **sporangia**, borne on
    **sporophylls** that may be aggregated into a **strobilus** or, in ferns,
    grouped into **sori** on the underside of the leaf and protected by an
    indusium. Most are **homosporous**; *Selaginella*, *Marsilea* and *Salvinia*
    are **heterosporous**, producing microspores and megaspores — a step towards
    the seed habit.
v. **Gametophyte.** The spore germinates into a small, green, independent,
   heart-shaped **prothallus** bearing **antheridia and archegonia**. The
   **antherozoids are multiflagellate and need external water** to reach the
   archegonium.
vi. **Alternation of generations** is heteromorphic, with a large independent
    sporophyte and a small independent gametophyte; after fertilisation the
    embryo develops on the prothallus and soon becomes independent.

**Why they are the first true vascular land plants.**

i. They are the **first group to possess true vascular tissue**. Xylem conducts
   water and mineral salts from the soil rapidly to the whole plant, and phloem
   translocates food. This is what frees a land plant from having to stay small
   and damp, and it is why tree ferns can be ten metres tall while no moss
   exceeds a few centimetres.
ii. The **lignified tracheids of the xylem give mechanical support**, so the
    plant can hold its leaves erect in air and compete for light.
iii. They are the **first plants with a true root** to anchor them and to absorb
     water from deep in the soil, instead of the rhizoids of a bryophyte.
iv. The **sporophyte is the dominant, independent generation**; the aerial parts
    are covered with a **cuticle** and bear **stomata**, so water loss is
    controlled.
v. Spores are produced in enormous numbers and are **wind-dispersed**, an
   adaptation to the terrestrial environment.

They remain, however, **incompletely adapted to land**, because the gametophyte
is thin and must live in a damp place and the antherozoids must swim to the
egg. They are therefore the first true vascular land plants but not the first
fully terrestrial ones — that position belongs to the seed plants, in which the
gametophyte is retained inside the sporophyte and the pollen tube replaces the
swimming sperm.
