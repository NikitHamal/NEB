Group A of the NEB Grade 11 Physics paper is eleven one-mark multiple-choice
questions — eleven marks, and the only eleven marks in the paper you can secure
in under fifteen minutes. This chapter drills all twenty-six syllabus units in
order. Each section opens with a **high-yield facts table** (the constants and
formulae the objective questions keep returning to), then gives eight MCQs, then
an answer key in which every answer carries a **one-line reason** and, where a
wrong option encodes a real misconception, names it.

::: key What Group A actually tests
Almost nothing in Group A is a long calculation. It tests four things: a
**definition**, a **unit or dimension**, a **direct substitution into one
formula**, or a **misconception** deliberately offered as a distractor. If an
MCQ is taking you more than ninety seconds, you have misread it.
:::

::: caution No question here carries a year tag — and that is deliberate
Coaching sites circulate lists labelled "NEB 2078", "NEB 2081" and so on, but
NEB does not publish its Grade 11 question papers, and we could not verify a
single item against an official released paper. Rather than print a year we
cannot stand behind, we print none. What we *can* stand behind is the
**syllabus weighting**, which is public, and every question below is built from
it. Treat this as a bank of exam-shaped questions, not a leaked paper.
:::

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Force | $[MLT^{-2}]$, newton |
| Work, energy, torque | $[ML^{2}T^{-2}]$ |
| Power | $[ML^{2}T^{-3}]$, watt |
| Pressure, stress, all elastic moduli, energy density | $[ML^{-1}T^{-2}]$, pascal |

1. The dimensional formula of the universal gravitational constant $G$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $[M^{-1}L^{3}T^{-2}]$ (b) $[ML^{3}T^{-2}]$ (c) $[M^{-1}L^{2}T^{-2}]$ (d) $[ML^{2}T^{-2}]$
2. Which pair does **not** have the same dimensions? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) work and torque (b) impulse and momentum (c) stress and pressure (d) force and power
3. The number of significant figures in $0.002340$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 7 (b) 6 (c) 4 (d) 3
4. Ten divisions of a vernier scale coincide with nine divisions of the main scale, and $1\ \text{MSD} = 1\ \text{mm}$. The least count is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0.1\ \text{mm}$ (b) $0.01\ \text{mm}$ (c) $1\ \text{mm}$ (d) $0.05\ \text{mm}$
5. Which quantity is dimensionless yet is given a unit? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) plane angle (b) strain (c) refractive index (d) relative density
6. Dimensional analysis **cannot** be used to find <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) whether an equation is dimensionally correct (b) the value of a dimensionless constant (c) the conversion factor between two systems of units (d) the dimensions of a derived quantity
7. If $Z = A^{2}B^{3}/C$ and the percentage errors in $A$, $B$ and $C$ are $1\%$, $2\%$ and $3\%$, the percentage error in $Z$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $6\%$ (b) $8\%$ (c) $10\%$ (d) $11\%$
8. The dimensional formula $[ML^{-1}T^{-2}]$ belongs to <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) surface tension (b) pressure (c) force constant (d) linear momentum

::: note Answers — 2.1
**1.** (a) — from $F = Gm_1m_2/r^2$, $G = Fr^2/m^2$ gives $[MLT^{-2}][L^2][M^{-2}] = [M^{-1}L^{3}T^{-2}]$.
**2.** (d) — force is $[MLT^{-2}]$, power is $[ML^{2}T^{-3}]$. Distractor (a) tempts students who think "torque is not energy so its dimensions must differ"; they are identical, only the physical meaning differs.
**3.** (c) — leading zeros are never significant, the trailing zero after the decimal point is; so 2, 3, 4, 0 = four.
**4.** (a) — $10\ \text{VSD} = 9\ \text{MSD}$, so $1\ \text{VSD} = 0.9\ \text{mm}$ and $\text{L.C.} = 1 - 0.9 = 0.1\ \text{mm}$. Option (b) is the screw-gauge least count, quoted from memory.
**5.** (a) — angle is length/length, so dimensionless, but SI still names the radian for it.
**6.** (b) — the method matches powers of $M$, $L$, $T$; a pure number carries none, so it survives undetermined.
**7.** (d) — $2(1) + 3(2) + 1(3) = 11\%$. Option (a) comes from adding the errors without their powers; (c) from forgetting the $C$ term.
**8.** (b) — pressure is force per area. Surface tension and force constant are both $[MT^{-2}]$, momentum is $[MLT^{-1}]$.
:::

## 2.2 Vectors

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Resultant (parallelogram law) | $R = \sqrt{P^{2}+Q^{2}+2PQ\cos\theta}$ |
| Direction of resultant | $\tan\alpha = \dfrac{Q\sin\theta}{P+Q\cos\theta}$ |
| Maximum, minimum resultant | $P+Q$ at $\theta = 0^{\circ}$; $|P-Q|$ at $\theta = 180^{\circ}$ |
| Scalar product | $\vec A\cdot\vec B = AB\cos\theta$ — a scalar |
| Vector product | $|\vec A\times\vec B| = AB\sin\theta$ — a vector, $\perp$ to both |
| Unit-vector rules | $\hat i\cdot\hat i = 1$, $\hat i\times\hat i = 0$, $\hat i\times\hat j = \hat k$ |
| Resolution | $A_x = A\cos\theta$, $A_y = A\sin\theta$ |

1. Two equal vectors of magnitude $P$ act at $120^{\circ}$ to each other. Their resultant has magnitude <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $2P$ (b) $P\sqrt{3}$ (c) $P$ (d) zero
2. Forces of $3\ \text{N}$ and $4\ \text{N}$ act at right angles. The resultant is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $1\ \text{N}$ (b) $5\ \text{N}$ (c) $7\ \text{N}$ (d) $12\ \text{N}$
3. The minimum number of coplanar vectors of **unequal** magnitude whose resultant can be zero is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) 2 (b) 3 (c) 4 (d) 5
4. If $\vec A\cdot\vec B = 0$ and neither vector is zero, the angle between them is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0^{\circ}$ (b) $45^{\circ}$ (c) $90^{\circ}$ (d) $180^{\circ}$
5. $\hat i\times\hat j$ is equal to <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $0$ (b) $1$ (c) $\hat k$ (d) $-\hat k$
6. The vector product of two parallel vectors is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) maximum (b) zero (c) unity (d) infinite
7. A vector of magnitude $10$ units makes $60^{\circ}$ with the $x$-axis. Its $x$-component is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $5$ units (b) $5\sqrt{3}$ units (c) $10$ units (d) $8.66$ units
8. Which of the following is a scalar quantity? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) torque (b) impulse (c) work (d) angular velocity

::: note Answers — 2.2
**1.** (c) — $R = \sqrt{P^2+P^2+2P^2\cos120^{\circ}} = \sqrt{2P^2-P^2} = P$. Option (a) is the answer for $0^{\circ}$; students who forget that $\cos120^{\circ}$ is *negative* pick it.
**2.** (b) — $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$. Option (c) adds the magnitudes, the commonest vector error of all.
**3.** (b) — two unequal vectors can never cancel; three can, if they close a triangle.
**4.** (c) — $AB\cos\theta = 0$ with $A,B \ne 0$ forces $\cos\theta = 0$.
**5.** (c) — right-hand rule, in cyclic order $\hat i\to\hat j\to\hat k$. Option (d) is $\hat j\times\hat i$, the order reversed.
**6.** (b) — $\sin 0^{\circ} = 0$. Option (a) is true of the *scalar* product; that swap is the classic confusion.
**7.** (a) — $A_x = 10\cos60^{\circ} = 5$. Option (d) is $10\sin60^{\circ}$, sine and cosine swapped.
**8.** (c) — work is $\vec F\cdot\vec s$, a scalar product. The other three are vectors.
:::

## 2.3 Kinematics

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Equations of motion | $v = u+at$, $s = ut+\tfrac12at^{2}$, $v^{2} = u^{2}+2as$ |
| Distance in the $n^{\text{th}}$ second | $s_n = u + \dfrac{a}{2}(2n-1)$ — a distance, unit metre |
| Slope and area | $x$–$t$ slope $= v$; $v$–$t$ slope $= a$, $v$–$t$ area $= s$ |
| Projectile | $T = \dfrac{2u\sin\theta}{g}$, $H = \dfrac{u^{2}\sin^{2}\theta}{2g}$, $R = \dfrac{u^{2}\sin2\theta}{g}$ |
| Maximum range | $R_{\max} = u^{2}/g$ at $\theta = 45^{\circ}$; $\theta$ and $90^{\circ}-\theta$ give equal range |
| Speed at the top | $u\cos\theta$, **not** zero |
| Horizontal projection | $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$, independent of $u$ |
| Relative velocity | $\vec v_{AB} = \vec v_A - \vec v_B$ |

1. From the velocity–time graph above, the total displacement of the body in $10\ \text{s}$ is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $100\ \text{m}$ (b) $120\ \text{m}$ (c) $140\ \text{m}$ (d) $200\ \text{m}$
2. The area under an acceleration–time graph gives <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) displacement (b) change in velocity (c) average velocity (d) jerk
3. At the highest point of its trajectory, the velocity of a projectile is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) zero (b) vertical and equal to $u\sin\theta$ (c) horizontal and equal to $u\cos\theta$ (d) equal to $u$
4. A stone is dropped from a height and another is thrown horizontally from the same height at the same instant. They <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) reach the ground together (b) the dropped one lands first (c) the thrown one lands first (d) it depends on the mass
5. For a freely falling body starting from rest, the distances covered in the first, second and third seconds are in the ratio <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $1:2:3$ (b) $1:3:5$ (c) $1:4:9$ (d) $1:1:1$
6. Two cars move towards each other at $10\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ and $20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. The speed of one relative to the other is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $10\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (b) $15\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (c) $30\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ (d) $200\ \text{m s}^{-1}$
7. A body is projected with speed $u$ at $30^{\circ}$. Another has the same range with the same speed at <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $45^{\circ}$ (b) $60^{\circ}$ (c) $75^{\circ}$ (d) $120^{\circ}$
8. The quantity $s_n = u + \frac{a}{2}(2n-1)$ has the SI unit <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\text{m s}^{-1}$ (b) $\text{m s}^{-2}$ (c) $\text{m}$ (d) $\text{m s}$

::: note Answers — 2.3
**1.** (c) — triangle A $= \tfrac12(4)(20) = 40$, rectangle B $= 4\times20 = 80$, triangle C $= \tfrac12(2)(20) = 20$; total $140\ \text{m}$. Option (d) is $20\times10$, the area of the whole rectangle — the error of treating a varying velocity as constant.
**2.** (b) — area $= \int a\,dt = \Delta v$. Option (a) is the area under a *velocity*–time graph; mixing the two graphs up is the standard slip.
**3.** (c) — only the vertical component vanishes.
**4.** (a) — vertical motion is identical and independent of the horizontal motion; $t = \sqrt{2h/g}$ contains no $u$.
**5.** (b) — $s_n \propto (2n-1)$, giving $1:3:5$. Option (c) is the ratio of *total* distances after 1, 2, 3 s.
**6.** (c) — opposite directions, so $v_{AB} = 10-(-20) = 30\ \text{m s}^{-1}$. Option (a) subtracts the speeds, which is right only for the *same* direction.
**7.** (b) — complementary angles give equal range, and $90^{\circ}-30^{\circ} = 60^{\circ}$.
**8.** (c) — it is the distance travelled *during* one particular second, so a metre. The "per second" in the wording tempts (a).
:::

::: example How to kill a projectile MCQ in twenty seconds
**Problem.** A ball thrown at $20\ \text{m s}^{-1}$ at $30^{\circ}$ ($g = 10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$) has range
(a) $20\ \text{m}$ (b) $34.6\ \text{m}$ (c) $40\ \text{m}$ (d) $10\ \text{m}$.

**Solution.** Do not compute components. Go straight to $R = u^{2}\sin2\theta/g$ with $2\theta = 60^{\circ}$:

$$ R = \frac{(20)^{2}\sin60^{\circ}}{10} = \frac{400 \times 0.866}{10} = 34.6\ \text{m} $$

Answer **(b)**. Option (c) is $u^{2}/g$, the *maximum* range — offered to anyone who reaches for $45^{\circ}$ out of habit.
:::

## 2.4 Dynamics

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Newton's second law | $\vec F = \dfrac{d\vec p}{dt} = m\vec a$ |
| Impulse | $\vec J = \vec F_{av}\Delta t = m\vec v - m\vec u$, unit N s $=$ kg m s⁻¹ |
| Conservation of momentum | $m_1u_1+m_2u_2 = m_1v_1+m_2v_2$ (no external force) |
| Torque | $\tau = F\,d_\perp = rF\sin\theta$, unit N m |
| Friction | $\mu_s = f_{lim}/N$, $\mu_k = f_k/N$, and $\mu_k < \mu_s$ |
| Angle of repose | $\mu_s = \tan\theta$ — equals the angle of friction $\lambda$ |
| Body on a smooth incline | $a = g\sin\theta$, $N = mg\cos\theta$ |
| Lift | apparent weight $= m(g+a)$ going up, $m(g-a)$ going down |

1. Newton's second law of motion in its most general form is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $F = mv$ (b) $F = dp/dt$ (c) $F = ma$ only (d) $F = mg$
2. The SI unit of impulse is the same as that of <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) force (b) energy (c) linear momentum (d) power
3. A rocket moves forward because <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) the exhaust gases push against the air (b) of conservation of linear momentum (c) of the buoyancy of the gases (d) the Earth attracts it less
4. A body of mass $2\ \text{kg}$ at rest is acted on by a force of $10\ \text{N}$ for $3\ \text{s}$. The impulse is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $6\ \text{N s}$ (b) $15\ \text{N s}$ (c) $30\ \text{N s}$ (d) $60\ \text{N s}$
5. In the free-body diagram above, the block is on the point of sliding. The coefficient of static friction is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\sin\theta$ (b) $\cos\theta$ (c) $\tan\theta$ (d) $1/\tan\theta$
6. Which statement about action and reaction is correct? <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) they act on the same body and cancel (b) they act on different bodies and never cancel (c) the action is always larger (d) they act at different instants
7. A man of mass $60\ \text{kg}$ stands in a lift accelerating upwards at $2\ \text{m s}^{-2}$ ($g = 10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$). The reading of the weighing machine is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $480\ \text{N}$ (b) $600\ \text{N}$ (c) $720\ \text{N}$ (d) $120\ \text{N}$
8. The coefficient of kinetic friction is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) greater than the coefficient of static friction (b) equal to it (c) less than it (d) always equal to 1

::: note Answers — 2.4
**1.** (b) — $F = ma$ follows from $F = dp/dt$ only when the mass is constant, which fails for a rocket.
**2.** (c) — impulse **is** the change in momentum, so N s $=$ kg m s⁻¹. Option (a) tempts students who read "impulse" as "a big force".
**3.** (b) — the gas carries momentum backwards, so the rocket carries an equal momentum forwards. Option (a) is the classic error: a rocket works in vacuum, where there is nothing to push against.
**4.** (c) — $J = F\Delta t = 10\times3 = 30\ \text{N s}$.
**5.** (c) — on the point of sliding, $f = \mu N$, with $f = mg\sin\theta$ and $N = mg\cos\theta$, so $\mu = \tan\theta$. This is the angle of repose.
**6.** (b) — they are equal and opposite but act on *different* bodies, so they can never be added together to cancel.
**7.** (c) — $N = m(g+a) = 60(12) = 720\ \text{N}$. Option (b) is the true weight, chosen by anyone who forgets the lift is accelerating; the machine reads the *normal reaction*, not the weight.
**8.** (c) — it always takes a larger force to start sliding than to keep it going.
:::

## 2.5 Work, energy and power

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Work | $W = Fs\cos\theta = \vec F\cdot\vec s$; variable force $W = \int F\,dx$ |
| Power | $P = W/t = \vec F\cdot\vec v$; $1\ \text{hp} = 746\ \text{W}$ |
| Work–energy theorem | $W_{net} = \Delta K$ |
| Kinetic energy | $K = \tfrac12mv^{2} = \dfrac{p^{2}}{2m}$, so $p = \sqrt{2mK}$ |
| Spring | $W = \tfrac12kx^{2}$ |
| $1\ \text{kW h}$ | $3.6\times10^{6}\ \text{J}$ — a unit of **energy** |
| Elastic collision | momentum **and** kinetic energy conserved; equal masses exchange velocities |
| Perfectly inelastic | $v = \dfrac{m_1u_1+m_2u_2}{m_1+m_2}$; KE is lost, momentum is not |

1. The work done by the centripetal force on a body in uniform circular motion is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) maximum (b) zero (c) negative (d) equal to $mv^{2}/r$
2. One kilowatt-hour is equal to <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $1000\ \text{J}$ (b) $3600\ \text{J}$ (c) $3.6\times10^{6}\ \text{J}$ (d) $3.6\times10^{3}\ \text{W}$
3. If the momentum of a body is doubled, its kinetic energy becomes <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) half (b) double (c) four times (d) unchanged
4. In a perfectly elastic head-on collision between two bodies of equal mass, one at rest, <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) both move together (b) they exchange velocities (c) both stop (d) the moving one rebounds
5. The work done by a conservative force around any closed path is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) always positive (b) always negative (c) zero (d) equal to the kinetic energy
6. A man of mass $60\ \text{kg}$ climbs a $5\ \text{m}$ staircase in $10\ \text{s}$ ($g = 10\ \text{m s}^{-2}$). His power output is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $30\ \text{W}$ (b) $300\ \text{W}$ (c) $3000\ \text{W}$ (d) $600\ \text{W}$
7. In an inelastic collision, the quantity that is **not** conserved is <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) linear momentum (b) total energy (c) kinetic energy (d) mass
8. A body of mass $m$ and kinetic energy $K$ has linear momentum <span class="marks">[1]</span>
   (a) $\sqrt{2mK}$ (b) $2mK$ (c) $\sqrt{mK}$ (d) $mK^{2}$

::: note Answers — 2.5
**1.** (b) — the force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so $\cos90^{\circ} = 0$. Option (d) is the *magnitude of the force*, offered to anyone who reads "work" and reaches for the nearest formula.
**2.** (c) — $1000\ \text{W}\times3600\ \text{s}$. Option (d) is the trap: kW h is energy, not power.
**3.** (c) — $K = p^{2}/2m$, so $K \propto p^{2}$. Option (b) comes from assuming $K \propto p$.

**5.** (c) — that is the definition of a conservative force.
**6.** (b) — $W = mgh = 60\times10\times5 = 3000\ \text{J}$, so $P = 3000/10 = 300\ \text{W}$. Option (c) is the work, not the power.

## 2.22 Capacitor

| High-yield fact | Value / form |
|---|---|
| Capacitance | $C = Q/V$; unit farad $= \text{C V}^{-1}$ |
| Parallel-plate | $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0 A}{d}$, with dielectric $C = \dfrac{\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r A}{d}$ |
| In series | $\dfrac{1}{C} = \dfrac{1}{C_1}+\dfrac{1}{C_2}+\dots$ — total is **less** than the smallest |

::: note Answers — 2.22
**1.** (c) — one farad is one coulomb per volt, an enormous unit, which is why real capacitors are in μF and pF.
**2.** (b) — bigger plates and a smaller gap both raise $C$.

::: note Answers — 2.25

::: example Reading a distractor set before you read the question
Look at question 3 of section 2.22 — three $3\ \mu\text{F}$ capacitors in series — and notice the shape of the options: $9$, $3$, $1$, $0.33$. Those four numbers are not random. $9\ \mu\text{F}$ is the answer you get with the *parallel* rule, $3\ \mu\text{F}$ is what you get if you do nothing at all, $1\ \mu\text{F}$ is the series answer, and $0.33\ \mu\text{F}$ is $1/3$ of the series answer, i.e. one extra reciprocal step. The examiner has written one option for each of the three commonest errors and one for the truth.

This is worth knowing because it works in reverse. If you can see that the four options are "parallel answer / do-nothing answer / series answer / over-reciprocated answer", you have identified the question as a **combination** question before you have read a word of the stem, and you know which single fact decides it: for capacitors, series means reciprocals. Spend your twenty seconds confirming that one fact rather than re-deriving the whole thing.
:::

::: example Estimating your way to an optics answer
Question 6 of section 2.17 — a $10\ \text{cm}$ convex lens with an object at $15\ \text{cm}$ — has options $6$, $30$, $-30$ and $25\ \text{cm}$. You can eliminate three of them without touching the lens formula.

The object is between $f$ and $2f$, so the standard result applies at once: the image is real, inverted, magnified and lies beyond $2f$, that is beyond $20\ \text{cm}$. That kills $6\ \text{cm}$ (too near), kills $-30\ \text{cm}$ (real means positive $v$ for a lens) and leaves $30\ \text{cm}$ against $25\ \text{cm}$. Now one line of arithmetic settles it: $1/v = 1/10 - 1/15 = 1/30$.

Option $6\ \text{cm}$ is not an accident either — it is the answer to $1/v = 1/10 + 1/15$, i.e. the *mirror* formula applied to a lens. The examiner put it there for exactly the student who has the two formulas the wrong way round.
:::

::: caution Do not learn the answer letters
Reorder the options and every letter in this chapter changes. What you are meant to carry out of a section is the *fact* in the high-yield table and the *reason* in the answer key, not "2.14 question 3 is (b)". If you find yourself recognising a question by its position on the page rather than by its physics, shuffle your revision: work the sections in reverse order, or cover the options and answer from memory before you look.
:::

## Chapter summary

- Group A of the NEB Physics paper is eleven one-mark objective items drawn from across all twenty-six units, so no unit is safe to skip; the whole of this chapter is built one section per syllabus unit for exactly that reason.
- Every section opens with a high-yield facts table. Those tables are the smallest thing you can revise that still covers the objective paper: constants, defining formulae, standard values and the one or two named results that MCQs keep returning to.
- The answer keys give a reason, not a letter. Where an option encodes a standard misconception — mass for weight, the mirror formula for the lens formula, the resistor rule for the capacitor rule, zero field for zero potential, mass defect for binding energy — the key names it, because recognising your own error is worth more than recognising the right answer.
- Recurring traps to carry into the hall: unit prefixes (μ, m, k) left unconverted; $\text{kW h}$ treated as a power; $c_{rms} \propto \sqrt{T}$ read as linear; the $\tfrac12$ in capacitor energy; the sign convention in mirror and lens problems; $\tfrac32kT$ per molecule versus $\tfrac32RT$ per mole; weightlessness explained as "no gravity".
- Objective marks are the cheapest marks in the paper and the easiest to throw away. One minute per item is the budget; if an item resists for longer, mark it, move on, and come back after Group C.
