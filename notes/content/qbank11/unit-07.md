This chapter does three things. It reads the **shape of the Grade 11 Chemistry paper** (3 hours, full marks 75, Group A $11\times1$, Group B $8\times5$, Group C $3\times8$) and shows how the 128 teaching hours are spread over the 17 units. It then sets **two complete model papers** to that grid, each **fully solved**. Finally it lists **twenty-four predicted questions in ranked order**, each with the reason it is predicted and a short model answer.

::: caution What "model" and "predicted" mean here
The model papers are original practice papers built to the CDC pattern; they are not NEB papers and do not claim to resemble any particular year. A **prediction** is a judgement, not a leak: nobody outside the board knows the paper. The ranking uses three public things only — the **teaching hours** of each unit, how **standard** the question is (a definition, reaction or numerical type that every textbook treats), and whether it is a **recurring numerical type**. Treat the list as a revision order. No question carries a past-paper year, because Grade 11 papers are not published by NEB.
:::

## 7.1 Pattern and weight analysis of the Grade 11 Chemistry paper

### 7.1.1 The grid

| Part | Questions | Marks | What to expect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group A | 11 multiple-choice | $11\times1 = 11$ | one mark each; drawn from the whole syllabus |
| Group B | 8 short answer | $8\times5 = 40$ | equations, short theory, one or two numericals |
| Group C | 3 long answer | $3\times8 = 24$ | a manufacturing process, a mechanism or a multi-part numerical |

All questions are compulsory. The time per mark is $180/75 = 2.4$ minutes: about 12 minutes for a Group B question and 19 for a Group C question.

### 7.1.2 Where the syllabus hours sit

| Area | Units | Hours | Share | Expected presence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| General and Physical | 1–8 | 48 | 38 % | two or three Group B numericals and a Group C question |
| Inorganic | 9–11 | 35 | 27 % | two Group B questions of reactions and tests |
| Organic | 12–15 | 30 | 23 % | two or three Group B questions; mechanisms and conversions |
| Applied | 16–17 | 15 | 12 % | a manufacturing process, often as Group C |

Two points follow. The single heaviest unit is **non-metals (22 hours)**, one-sixth of the whole course, so reactions of nitrogen, sulphur and halogens must be known cold. And **nothing is safe to skip:** Group A is spread over all seventeen units.

For equal volumes the rates are inversely proportional to the times, so $\dfrac{r_X}{r_{O_2}} = \dfrac{20}{40} = \sqrt{\dfrac{32}{M_X}}$. Squaring, $\dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{32}{M_X}$ and $M_X = 128\ \text{g mol}^{-1}$.

::: note Solutions — Group B (12–19)
**12.** Postulates: (i) matter consists of indivisible atoms; (ii) atoms of one element are identical in mass and properties; (iii) atoms of different elements differ; (iv) compounds form by combination of atoms in simple whole-number ratios; (v) atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction. *Modifications:* atoms are divisible into subatomic particles; atoms of the same element may differ in mass (isotopes).
**13.** Moles of $\text{CaCO}_3 = 10/100 = 0.10$; moles of HCl $= 0.1\times1 = 0.10$. $0.10$ mol $\text{CaCO}_3$ needs $0.20$ mol HCl, so **HCl is limiting**. $2$ mol HCl gives $1$ mol $\text{CO}_2$; so $0.10$ mol HCl gives $0.05$ mol $\text{CO}_2$. Volume $= 0.05\times22.4 = 1.12\ \text{L}$.
**14.** Effective nuclear charge $Z_{eff} = Z - S$ is the net positive charge felt by an outer electron after shielding by inner electrons. Across Period 3 (Na to Ar), electrons are added to the same shell, so shielding barely changes while $Z$ increases: $Z_{eff}$ rises, the outer electrons are pulled in, and the **atomic radius falls** (Na $186$ pm to Cl $99$ pm). The same pull makes the electron harder to remove, so the **first ionization energy generally rises**, with small dips at Al (the $3p$ electron is higher in energy than the $3s$) and S (pairing repulsion in $3p^{4}$).
**15.** Oxygen is small and highly electronegative, so water molecules form strong hydrogen bonds between $\text{O}-\text{H}$ and the lone pair of a neighbouring oxygen; this holds the molecules together as a liquid at room temperature ($100^{\circ}\text{C}$ boiling point). Sulphur is larger and less electronegative, so $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ has only weak dipole attractions and boils at $-60^{\circ}\text{C}$. In ice each oxygen is hydrogen-bonded to four others in an open hexagonal lattice with empty space, so ice is less dense than water ($0.92\ \text{g cm}^{-3}$) and floats.
**16.** $Q = It = 5\times3600 = 18\,000\ \text{C}$. Moles of electrons $= 18\,000/96\,500 = 0.1865$. $\text{Na}^{+} + e^{-} \to \text{Na}$: mass $= 0.1865\times23 = 4.29\ \text{g}$. $2\text{Cl}^{-} \to \text{Cl}_2 + 2e^{-}$: moles of $\text{Cl}_2 = 0.1865/2 = 0.0933$; volume $= 0.0933\times22.4 = 2.09\ \text{L}$.
**17.** Moles: $\text{H}_2 = 2/2 = 1.0$; $\text{N}_2 = 14/28 = 0.5$. $T = 300\ \text{K}$. $p_{\text{H}_2} = \dfrac{nRT}{V} = \dfrac{1.0\times0.0821\times300}{10} = 2.46\ \text{atm}$; $p_{\text{N}_2} = \dfrac{0.5\times0.0821\times300}{10} = 1.23\ \text{atm}$. By Dalton's law the total is $2.46 + 1.23 = 3.69\ \text{atm}$.
**18.** Quicklime: $\text{CaCO}_3 \rightarrow\ [1100\ \text{K}] \text{CaO} + \text{CO}_2$; uses: making slaked lime and cement, drying agent for ammonia, flux in metallurgy. Bleaching powder: $\text{Ca(OH)}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \to \text{CaOCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$; uses: bleaching cotton and paper, disinfecting water. Bleaching action: $\text{CaOCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \to \text{CaSO}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$; the chlorine, with water, gives $\text{HOCl}$, which bleaches by oxidation.
**19.** *Geometrical isomerism:* arises from restricted rotation about a double bond when each doubly bonded carbon has two different groups. But-2-ene has *cis* (both $\text{CH}_3$ on the same side, boiling point $4^{\circ}\text{C}$, polar) and *trans* (opposite sides, boiling point $1^{\circ}\text{C}$, non-polar, more stable) forms. *Optical isomerism:* arises when a carbon carries four different groups (a chiral carbon). In lactic acid, $\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)COOH}$, the middle carbon holds H, $\text{CH}_3$, OH and COOH, giving two mirror-image forms ($d$ and $l$) that rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions and are non-superposable.
:::

::: note Solutions — Group C (20–22)
**20. (a)** In a mixture of non-reacting gases the total pressure is the sum of the partial pressures: $P = p_1 + p_2 + \cdots$. For gas $i$: $p_i = \dfrac{n_iRT}{V}$ and $P = \dfrac{n_{tot}RT}{V}$, so $\dfrac{p_i}{P} = \dfrac{n_i}{n_{tot}} = x_i$ and $p_i = x_iP$.
**(b)** $n_{\text{N}_2} = 2.8/28 = 0.1$; $n_{\text{O}_2} = 3.2/32 = 0.1$; $n_{tot} = 0.2$. $P = \dfrac{0.2\times0.0821\times300}{10} = 0.493\ \text{atm}$. Each gas has $x = 0.5$, so $p = 0.246\ \text{atm}$.
**(c)** $\dfrac{r_{\text{N}_2}}{r_{\text{O}_2}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{M_{\text{O}_2}}{M_{\text{N}_2}}} = \sqrt{\dfrac{32}{28}} = 1.07$; nitrogen diffuses slightly faster.
**21. (a)** Step 1: $\text{HBr}$ is polar; the $\pi$ electrons of propene attack $\text{H}^{+}$, giving a carbocation. H can add to either carbon: adding to $\text{CH}_2$ gives the secondary carbocation $\text{CH}_3\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{HCH}_3$; adding to $\text{CH}$ gives the primary $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\overset{+}{\text{C}}\text{H}_2$. Two alkyl groups push electrons ($+I$) and stabilize the secondary ion, so it forms faster. Step 2: $\text{Br}^{-}$ attacks the carbocation to give $\text{CH}_3\text{CHBrCH}_3$ (2-bromopropane) as the major product. Markovnikov's rule: the negative part of the reagent goes to the carbon with fewer hydrogens.
**(b)** $\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)CH}_3 \rightarrow\ [\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ \text{heat}] \text{CH}_3\text{CH}{=}\text{CH}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.
**(c)** Bromine water test: an orange solution is decolourized because of addition of $\text{Br}_2$ across the double bond. Baeyer's test: pink or purple cold alkaline dilute $\text{KMnO}_4$ is decolourized (a brown precipitate of $\text{MnO}_2$ may form) as the alkene is oxidized to a diol.
**22. (a)** (i) Laboratory research: the reaction, catalyst and conditions are found. (ii) Process design and economic evaluation: flow sheet, material and energy balance, cost estimate. (iii) Pilot plant: a small-scale plant tests the process and safety. (iv) Full-scale design, construction and commissioning. (v) Operation with quality control and monitoring.
**(b)** *Batch:* a fixed charge of reactants is reacted and the product removed before the next charge; flexible, suited to small-volume products (drugs, dyes); higher labour cost per unit. *Continuous:* feed enters and product leaves without a break; suited to large-volume products (ammonia, sulphuric acid, NaOH); lower cost per tonne and steadier quality, but less flexible and with a high capital cost.
**(c)** Treat effluent before discharge (neutralization, sedimentation, biological treatment); use scrubbers for $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{NO}_x$; recycle by-products and unreacted material; use catalysts and heat exchangers to save energy.

## Chapter summary

- The Chemistry paper is $75$ marks: $11\times1$, $8\times5$ and $3\times8$; General and Physical ($48$ h) and Inorganic ($35$ h) make up two-thirds of the teaching time, and non-metals ($22$ h) is the heaviest unit.

- The twenty-four predicted questions are ranked by unit hours and standardness; they are judgements, not leaks.
- In the hall: balance every equation and write conditions over the arrow; write the formula before substituting; draw the flow sheet and label it.

**Group C — Long answer (8 marks each)**

1. Describe the manufacture of sulphuric acid by the contact process. <span class="marks">[8]</span>
2. Describe benzene's structure and the evidence for resonance. <span class="marks">[8]</span>

::: note Answers to Group C
**1.** Stages: (i) burn sulphur or roast pyrites to get $\text{SO}_2$: $\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \to \text{SO}_2$; purify and dry the gas (impurities poison the catalyst). (ii) Pass $\text{SO}_2$ with excess air over $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ at $450^{\circ}\text{C}$ and $1$–$2$ atm: $2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{SO}_3$, $\Delta H = -197\ \text{kJ}$; heat is removed with exchangers to favour the product. (iii) Absorb $\text{SO}_3$ in conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($98$ %) to give oleum, $\text{H}_2\text{S}_2\text{O}_7$. (iv) Dilute the oleum with water to the required strength. Water is not used for direct absorption because it forms a mist of acid. Flow sheet: furnace $\to$ purifier $\to$ dryer $\to$ preheater $\to$ converter $\to$ absorber $\to$ dilution tank.
**2.** Benzene, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_6$, is a planar hexagon of six $sp^{2}$ carbons, each bonded to one hydrogen; the six unhybridized $p$ orbitals overlap sideways to give a delocalized $\pi$ cloud of six electrons above and below the ring. Kekulé's two forms (alternating double bonds) are resonance structures; the real molecule is a hybrid. *Evidence:* all C–C bonds are equal ($139\ \text{pm}$); only one 1,2-disubstituted product exists; the heat of hydrogenation ($-208\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$) is about $150\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ less than three times that of cyclohexene ($-360$); benzene undergoes substitution, not addition, and does not decolourize bromine water or Baeyer's reagent.
:::
